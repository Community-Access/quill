Nemotron live, whisper.cpp, and the benchmark gate, 2026-10-23

The second real-speech run, after the 2026-10-22 record. It answers two questions that run left open, and adds a gate so the figures the apps use keep being checked. Every number here is in dictation-live-and-whispercpp-2026-10-23.json beside this page.

In one minute

  • Nemotron live is as good as Nemotron by phrase, or better. The first run fed Nemotron whole phrases; live dictation feeds it as you speak. At a normal microphone level the two scored the same on Google’s FLEURS read speech (10.0 and 10.1 percent of words wrong), and live was better on audiobooks no model was trained on (4.6 percent against 5.3). Live also used about a third less memory (1.0 GB against 1.5) and had the words written within 1.5 seconds of a pause for 95 phrases in 100.
  • Nemotron is sensitive to quiet audio. The same 200 FLEURS recordings, many of them published very quietly, scored 13.7 percent as published and 10.1 once brought to a normal level. That explains much of the gap between the first run’s 13.7 and NVIDIA’s own 7.9.
  • Live dictation can miss very quiet speech entirely. At the published level, live dictation wrote nothing for 17 of the 200 FLEURS recordings: the voice detector that decides when you are speaking, shared by every engine in live dictation, never heard them start. They were at -50 to -68 dB below full scale – far quieter than a microphone at a normal setting – and most came straight after louder speech. A microphone whose level is set very low could do the same. It is a follow-up, not yet a fix.
  • whisper.cpp base, Locked Dictation’s default, is accurate and light but slow. 6.9 percent of words wrong on the held-out audiobooks and 9.2 on FLEURS, slightly better than the same Whisper base run by sherpa-onnx (7.4 and 10.8), in well under half the memory (about 320 MB against 820). But it took 0.44 seconds of computing per second of speech on one thread – twice sherpa-onnx’s – and its words came about 4.7 seconds after a phrase on one thread, 2.7 seconds on four, because it encodes a full 30-second window for every phrase, however short.
  • A benchmark gate now checks the advice. Ten recordings of real people are committed with the code, and a weekly check runs every model the advice can recommend on them. Its first run found that the catalogue understates Whisper’s cost on short phrases (below).

How it was tested

Everything in the first record’s method holds: the same computer (Intel Core Ultra 7 265H, 16 cores, 64 GB, Windows 11), the same harness (scripts/dictbench.py), the same seeded subsets (200 FLEURS English recordings, 101 held-out audiobook recordings), the same scorer, which ignores capitals and punctuation. What is new:

  • Live mode (dictbench --latency). Each recording is played, at real speed, through the same recogniser dictation uses – the voice detector, the pause, Nemotron’s live stream that runs across phrases – with two and a half seconds of silence between recordings. Words are scored for the whole session as one document as well as per recording, because a phrase that ends late can land in the next recording’s window; the two agree to a tenth of a point except where noted.
  • Levelled audio. A copy of the 200 FLEURS recordings with each one scaled to -26 dB below full scale, about where a microphone at a normal setting puts speech and where the audiobooks already are. Nothing else about the audio changed. It separates what the voice detector does from what the model does.
  • whisper.cpp (dictbench --engine whispercpp:base, scripts/bench_whispercpp.py). QUILL’s own pinned whisper-cli (whisper.cpp v1.9.1, from QUILL’s assets-v1 release) and SHA-256-pinned GGML model, run once per recording through the same command QUILL builds, with no initial prompt. Its memory is the program’s own, measured while it runs. The app passes no thread count, so whisper.cpp uses up to four; it was measured at one (the budget’s measure) and four.

Full stops were found as well live as by phrase (F1 0.94 on levelled FLEURS either way). On the audiobooks the live full-stop score was lower (0.73 against 0.76): a reader’s sentence often runs over a pause, and live dictation closes each phrase as a sentence.

The quiet recordings

At the published level, 17 FLEURS recordings came back with no text in live mode, and the same eight of the first 25 did so again on a second run, so it is not chance. Instrumenting the recogniser showed the voice detector never reported speech starting for them, so Nemotron was never given them. Most of the FLEURS recordings are very quiet (about -62 dB below full scale, against -26 for the audiobooks; the preparation was checked against the published files and is faithful). The misses were the quiet ones that came straight after a loud one: the detector is a neural network with memory, and after loud speech it did not judge very quiet speech to be speech for half a minute or more. The same recordings, levelled, were all heard.

In phrase mode there is no voice detector – each recording is handed over whole – which is why the first run did not see this.

Loading the model and encoding a 30-second window costs whisper.cpp about 3.5 to 4.4 seconds per run on one thread whatever the phrase’s length, which is most of the time it takes on a dictated sentence.

The gate fails a model that runs at more than 1.5 times its catalogue cost. Whisper failed. Measured separately, Whisper tiny’s cost against Moonshine tiny was 2.7 on FLEURS recordings averaging 8.6 seconds and 4.3 to 4.5 on the committed recordings averaging 6 to 7 seconds: Whisper always encodes a 30-second window, so the shorter the phrase, the more each second costs, while Moonshine’s cost follows the phrase’s length. The catalogue’s 2.6 came from the longer FLEURS recordings; at the length of a dictated sentence it is nearer 4.5. Every model’s cost rises somewhat on short phrases, Whisper’s most.

What changed

  • scripts/bench_whispercpp.py and dictbench --engine whispercpp:MODEL.
  • scripts/dictation_speed_gate.py, tests/corpus/dictation (ten recordings, LibriVox, public domain, held out from training; SOURCE.md says how they were chosen), tests/unit/scripts/test_dictation_speed_gate.py, and the weekly .github/workflows/dictation-benchmark.yml. The part that needs no models – every recommendation keeps to its budget, on 72 kinds of computer, in both languages, for every priority – runs with every pull request.
  • No recommendation changed. The two decisions this run raises are below.

What it leaves open

  • The quiet-speech misses. Levelling the audio before the voice detector, or lowering its threshold for quiet input, would very likely fix them; either is a change to every engine’s live path and needs its own testing with real microphones. Until then the guides say plainly that a microphone set very low can make dictation miss speech.
  • Cost on short phrases. The catalogue’s cost factors were measured on recordings of about ten seconds. Re-measuring every model at the length of a dictated sentence would make the advice truer, and would change at least one recommendation (Whisper tiny would no longer be the quickest choice for Spanish where Parakeet TDT v3 fits). That is a decision, not a correction, so it waits for one.
  • whisper.cpp as Locked Dictation’s default. The data now exists. Against whisper.cpp base, Parakeet Unified makes half the mistakes in English, and sherpa-onnx’s own Whisper base is twice as fast for a similar result; but whisper.cpp has the smallest memory footprint and the initial prompt Locked Dictation’s profiles use. Whether to change the default is a decision for the dictation plan (section 39).
  • Everything the first record left open (a real 4 GB computer with a screen reader running, native Spanish questions) still stands.
