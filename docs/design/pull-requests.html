Pull requests you can hear: the plan

Plan of record, started 2026-11-09. QUILL and QUILL Lite, one shared implementation (rule 10). Cited as pull-requests.md <section>.

1. Why this, and why now

QUILL already turns a pull request into something you can walk. You hear the whole pull request in one sentence (“Approved, 2 checks failing, 1 open thread, 3 commits behind main”), you walk an outline of files, threads and checks, you comment on a line or a range, suggest a change, keep comments pending and send one review. GitHub Guide chapter 5 describes all of it, and it is solid.

What it does not do yet is tell you what changed. Today a change sounds like one of these:

  • “Difference 2 of 5. Text changed at line 41.” Then, if you ask for more: “Change: changed ‘count>10’ to ‘count>=10’.”
  • “Difference 3 of 5. 3 lines replaced with 5 at line 80.”
  • “Added line 42: return total” (with every space read, or not, depending on your screen reader).

A sighted reviewer glances at a red-and-green block and knows in a second that a number went from 10 to 20, or that a function moved, or that 30 lines are the same edit made 30 times. A screen-reader user hears quoted fragments and line counts, and has to rebuild the meaning in their head. That is the gap.

Changes come in every size: one character (> became >=), one name (count became total), one line, a block (a new function), many places at once (the same rename in 14 files), a move (code that went somewhere else unchanged) and nothing that matters (indentation, line endings). Each deserves its own kind of sentence.

The goal is that a blind developer reviews a pull request faster and with more confidence than they could on the website, and enjoys it: never overwhelmed, never lost, always able to go one level deeper.

2. Principles

  1. Say what happened, not where the bytes are. “The limit went from 10 to 20” before “line 41”. The line number is always there, second.
  2. Gist first, detail one key away. Every change has three depths: the gist (one short sentence), the change (before and after), and the characters (spelled, with punctuation named). You choose how deep, change by change. No verbosity setting decides it for you (GATE-13’s rule: say less, rather than making people configure how much less).
  3. The size of the sentence matches the size of the change. One character gets one short sentence. A 200-line new file gets a sentence about its shape, not 200 lines read aloud.
  4. Punctuation is content in code. In a change description, QUILL names the characters that changed (“greater-than became greater-than-or-equal”), whatever the screen reader’s punctuation level, the way dictation’s “hear the punctuation” already does. The full line still reads as normal text.
  5. Never hide, never guess. Anything summarised can be expanded to the exact text. A heuristic (“this looks like a rename”) says it is a heuristic when it is not certain, and the plain diff is always one key away.
  6. Your place is sacred. Reading never changes anything on GitHub. Leaving and coming back returns you to the same change. “Viewed” and “visited” are yours.
  7. Speech, braille and sound together. Every sentence goes through the announcement service (GATE-BRAILLE). Braille gets a compact form that fits a 40-cell display. Sounds are optional cues, never the only signal.
  8. Delight is in the timing. The right sentence at the right moment, a soft tone instead of a word you have heard a hundred times, and a moment of celebration when you have reviewed everything.
  9. Descriptions guide; the text decides. A spoken description is where you start, never where you have to stop. Every change can be opened as real text, before and after, to arrow through character by character, select, copy and judge for yourself. A reviewer who wants to check QUILL’s sentence against the code can always do so in one key.
  10. Every decision is yours, item by item. You can accept or decline each change and each suggestion on its own, and QUILL says plainly what that does on GitHub before it does it.

3. The experience, from the reviewer’s chair

A walk-through of what it should feel like. Keys in this section are proposals (section 9 lists them and the rules they answer to).

Opening. Pull request #42 opens. You hear the one sentence you hear today, then one more: the shape.

“Review loaded for pull request #42. Approved, 2 checks failing, 1 open thread. 7 files: 4 source, 2 tests, 1 doc. Mostly in src/billing. Biggest: invoice.py, 48 lines added.”

The story, if you want it. Press What Is This Pull Request? and hear the author’s description, read as text with its headings and lists, then QUILL’s own plain account of the changes, file by file, in a sentence each:

“invoice.py: new function round_total, and the tax rate changes from 0.2 to 0.25. test_invoice.py: 3 new tests. README.md: one paragraph reworded.”

Walking the changes. In the review, Next Change and Previous Change walk every change in the pull request, across files, in order. Each change is its gist:

“invoice.py, change 2 of 5. The tax rate went from 0.2 to 0.25. Line 14.”

“invoice.py, change 3 of 5. New function round_total, 9 lines, at line 30.”

“utils.py. One character: greater-than became greater-than-or-equal, in if count >= limit. Line 7.”

“12 lines moved, unchanged, from line 80 to line 20.”

“Formatting only: indentation, 6 lines.”

Going deeper. On any change, Tell Me More gives the before and after:

“Before: TAX_RATE = 0.2. After: TAX_RATE = 0.25.”

and Spell the Change gives the characters that differ, with punctuation named, then puts you in the details field where you can arrow by character.

Seeing for yourself. Before and After (Ctrl+B) opens the change in a window with two text fields, Before and After, holding the real lines with the lines around them. Arrow through either one; the other follows to the same line. A list of Changed parts takes you straight to each one: Enter selects the changed characters in both fields, so your screen reader reads exactly what differs and your braille display shows it under your fingers. Turn on Mark changes and the changed text is wrapped in spoken markers, “[was: 0.2]” and “[now: 0.25]”, for when you would rather read a line than compare two.

Accept or decline, one at a time. In the walk and in Before and After, Accept says this change is fine, and the outline shows it as accepted. Decline asks you why, in a comment that goes with your review, attached to those lines. When you compose the review, QUILL has counted for you: “18 changes accepted, 2 declined”, and with anything declined it starts the review as Request changes. If you are the author, a reviewer’s suggestion has its own Accept Suggestion, which commits it to your branch after showing you the exact before and after, and Decline Suggestion, which replies with your reason and resolves the thread.

The same edit, many times. When a pull request repeats one edit, QUILL says it once:

“The same change in 14 places across 6 files: print( became logger.info(. Press Enter to list them.”

Commenting where you are. Comment on This Change attaches to exactly the lines of the change you are on, with the gist in the comment window’s title: “Comment on: the tax rate went from 0.2 to 0.25”. A suggestion starts with the new text already in the box.

Keeping track. Mark File Viewed sets GitHub’s own “Viewed” checkbox, so your progress shows on the website too. Progress is one sentence away: “3 of 7 files viewed; 2 comments pending.”

Finishing. When you have walked every change, you hear a short, warm sound and: “That’s every change. 2 comments pending. Compose your review?” Enter opens Compose Review with them listed.

4. Describing a change

This is the heart of the plan: one shared describer, quill/core/change_describer.py, wx-free and strict-typed, used by the pull request review, the diff window, Compare Documents, local Git changes (Review Changes in project tasks) and Story Studio’s revisions. One engine means one way of hearing a change everywhere in the family.

4.1 What it takes and gives

  • Input: the old and new text of one hunk (or one Compare group), the file’s language if known, and the line numbers.
  • Output: a ChangeDescription with kind, gist (one sentence, at most about 15 words before the location), detail (before and after), characters (the spelled difference), braille (a compact form), lines (exact range for comments) and confidence for anything heuristic.

4.2 The kinds, smallest to largest

Each kind has a fixed sentence pattern, so you learn the shapes by ear.

Kind Detected by Gist
Character one line, one changed span of at most 3 characters inside a token “One character: greater-than became greater-than-or-equal, in if count >= limit.”
Case the change is letter case only “Capital letter: Total was total.”
Number a numeric literal changed and nothing else “10 became 20, in limit = 20.”
Text a string literal changed “The text “Save” became “Save As”.”
Name one identifier changed, in one line “count renamed to total.”
Token a few tokens changed in one line “In line 41: > became >= and 10 became limit.”
Line added or removed one whole line “Line added: return total.”
Lines a short block (2 to 5 lines) “3 lines added after def save(self):: …” (the first line, then “and 2 more”)
Block a block with a recognisable start “New function round_total, 9 lines.” / “Removed class LegacyExporter, 40 lines.”
Moved removed lines reappear elsewhere unchanged (or nearly) “12 lines moved, unchanged, from line 80 to line 20.”
Repeated the same token change in several places “The same change in 14 places: print( became logger.info(.”
Formatting whitespace or line endings only “Formatting only: indentation, 6 lines.”
File added, deleted, renamed, binary, generated “New file, tests/test_invoice.py, 3 tests.” / “Renamed from util.py, unchanged.”

4.3 How the parts are found

  • Tokens, not words. Code is split into identifiers, numbers, strings, operators and punctuation (a small tokenizer per language family, falling back to a generic one), so count>10 is three tokens, not one “word”. Prose (Markdown, plain text, comments inside code) keeps word-level description, which already works well.
  • Character spans come from difflib on the line, as Compare Documents’ InlineChange already does, trimmed to token boundaries for the gist.
  • Blocks are recognised by their first line: the language servers QUILL already ships answer “what symbol starts here” for Python, JavaScript, TypeScript, HTML, CSS and JSON; a light pattern list covers the rest (def, function, class, fn, headings in Markdown). Without either, the gist says “N lines added” and quotes the first line.
  • Moves are matched by normalised line hashes across a file and across the pull request, with a minimum of 3 lines so trivial lines do not match.
  • Repeats group identical token-level changes; a group of 3 or more is said once.
  • Generated and lock files (package-lock.json, *.min.js, snapshot files, .ipynb output) are recognised and summarised in one line, with the full diff still one key away.

4.4 Saying characters

A character vocabulary, shared with dictation’s “hear the punctuation”: “greater-than”, “greater-than-or-equal” (said for >=), “not-equal” (!=), “arrow” (->, =>), “open bracket”, “close brace”, “backquote”, “space”, “tab”, “nothing” (for an empty side). Two characters that look alike are told apart: “hyphen” against “en dash”, “straight quote” against “curly quote”, “letter O” against “zero” when the change is between them. Unicode changes name the character (“non-breaking space became space”).

4.5 Length and pacing

  • The gist is short enough to hear while arrowing: at most about 15 words before the location, and the location last.
  • A quote of code inside a sentence is capped (about 60 characters, then “and more”); the full line is in Tell Me More.
  • A change you have already heard, heard again, gives the gist only once more, then “same as before” if nothing moved.

5. The pull request at a glance

5.1 The shape sentence

After the existing one-sentence summary: the number of files by kind (source, tests, docs, config, generated), the area they are mostly in (the deepest common folder holding most of the change), and the biggest change. Built from data the review already loads (file list, additions, deletions), so no new requests.

5.2 What Is This Pull Request?

A window (or the details pane, from the outline’s top row) with:

  1. the title, author, branch into base, and age;
  2. the description, rendered from Markdown to readable text with headings and lists kept (the same renderer the GitHub windows use for issues);
  3. Changes in plain words: one sentence per file, built from the change describer’s gists, biggest first, with generated files folded into one line;
  4. linked issues and what they say they close.

Optionally, with consent and an AI chosen in Choose Your AI, Explain This Pull Request asks the AI for a summary, bounded and cited the way diff_explanation.py already bounds a diff explanation. It is a button, never automatic, and it says which AI was asked.

6. Walking and reviewing

6.1 One walk across everything

Next Change and Previous Change move through every change of every file in outline order; Next File and Previous File jump files. The outline follows along, so the tree always shows where you are, and Tab still reads the details. The existing Next Unresolved and Next Unvisited stay.

6.2 Three depths, three keys

  • Gist: what you hear when you arrive.
  • Tell Me More: before and after, each as a line you can read.
  • Spell the Change: the differing characters, named; focus moves into the details field at the first changed character so arrows can inspect it.

Pressing Tell Me More twice is Spell the Change, matching the family’s “press twice for more” habit (Which AI Am I Using).

6.3 Before and After: the text itself

Spoken descriptions are a guide. The reviewer still needs the text: to arrow through it, read it at their own pace, check a single character, and decide. Before and After (Ctrl+B on a change, a file or a thread; also on each row’s context menu) opens one window for that:

  • Before and After, two read-only multi-line text fields with the real lines of the change and three lines of context around them (more with Show more context, Alt+M). Each line can start with its line number (Line numbers, a check box, kept as a preference) so a line can be found and quoted.
  • The caret follows. Moving to a line in one field puts the other field’s caret on the matching line, so Tab between them always lands in the same place. Nothing is announced when it moves; the screen reader reads the line you land on (GATE-13).
  • Changed parts, a list of every changed span in the change, in order: “Line 14: 0.2 became 0.25”. Enter moves both carets there and selects the changed characters in each field, which is how a screen reader and a braille display show exactly what differs without colour. Ctrl+Alt+Shift+. and , move to the next and previous changed part from either field, as they move to the next difference in Compare Documents.
  • Mark changes (a check box, kept as a preference) rewrites both fields with the differences inline and named in words, “[was: 0.2]” in Before and “[now: 0.25]” in After, or as symbols ({-0.2-} and {+0.25+}) for people who prefer them, using the same Words or Symbols choice as the diff window’s Line marks. Off, the fields hold the code exactly.
  • One column, a check box, interleaves the two: “Before 14:” then “After 14:” line by line in a single field, for comparing line against line without changing fields.
  • Open Before as a Document and Open After as a Document open the text in a QUILL tab, read-only, with every editor tool: Find, Go To Line, word count, and Compare Documents on the two.
  • Accept (Alt+A) and Decline (Alt+D), section 6.6, and Comment on This Change, so the decision is made where the reading is done.
  • The window works for a whole file too: its fields hold every change in the file, with the unchanged stretches between them folded into one line each, “12 unchanged lines”, which Enter unfolds.

The same window serves Compare Documents and Review Changes in project tasks, so the way to look at a change is the same everywhere in the family.

6.4 Viewed, and progress

  • Mark File Viewed uses GitHub’s own viewed state (GraphQL markFileAsViewed / unmarkFileAsViewed), so it agrees with the website. It is a write, so it needs sign-in, and it says so.
  • The outline row for a viewed file says “viewed”; a file that changed after you marked it says “changed since viewed” (GitHub’s DISMISSED state).
  • Review Progress says one sentence: files viewed, changes walked, comments pending, threads you opened.

6.5 Finishing well

When the walk reaches the last change, a soft completion sound and one sentence: what you did and what is next. If comments are pending, Enter opens Compose Review with them listed by gist, not just by line.

6.6 Accept and Decline, change by change

GitHub has no “approve this one change”: a review approves or requests changes on the whole pull request, and comments are what attach to lines. So Accept and Decline are built from what GitHub does have, and each says what it will do before it does it.

  • Accept (Alt+A, and on each change’s context menu) records that you have checked this change and it is fine. The outline row says “accepted”, the walk skips accepted changes when you ask it to (Next Undecided), and accepting every change in a file offers to mark the file Viewed on GitHub. Accepting sends nothing on its own.
  • Decline (Alt+D) opens the comment window on that change’s lines, titled “Decline: the tax rate went from 0.2 to 0.25”, and asks for a reason: a decline without a reason helps nobody. It is added to your review as a pending comment, so it is sent with your verdict, not on its own.
  • Undo a decision with the same key again, or Clear Decision on the context menu. A declined change’s pending comment is removed with it, after asking.
  • Accept All in This File and Accept the Rest exist, and ask first, for formatting-only and generated files; QUILL suggests them for those.
  • Decisions are kept on this computer, for your account and this pull request, beside the pending comments. Each is tied to the change’s exact text: when new commits arrive, an accepted change whose text is unchanged stays accepted, and one that changed reads “changed since you accepted it” and becomes undecided.
  • Composing the review starts from the decisions: the window says “18 changes accepted, 2 declined, 3 undecided”, the action starts as Request changes if anything was declined and Approve if everything was accepted, and Include the tally adds that line to the review message. You can change all of it; Submit Review is still the only thing that sends.

7. Commenting

  • Comment on This Change anchors to the change’s exact lines (the existing anchor_for and lines_for_range already know GitHub’s rules), and the window’s title is the change’s gist.
  • Suggest a change starts with the new text in the box, with the change already made, so the reviewer edits rather than retypes.
  • Pending comments list by gist (“On: the tax rate went from 0.2 to 0.25”) as well as by line, so a review can be checked before it is sent.
  • Quick replies: on a thread, a short list of the replies people send most (“Done.”, “Good catch, fixed in the next commit.”, “Could you say more?”), editable, and saved per person. Never sent without the person choosing.
  • Reading a thread says who said what, in order, with code blocks and suggestions announced as such (“Suggestion: replace line 14 with …”).

7.1 Accepting and declining suggestions (the author’s side)

When a reviewer suggests a change, the author decides on it, item by item:

  • A thread holding a suggestion reads “Suggestion from alice, on line 14: the tax rate becomes 0.25”, with the change described the way section 4 describes any change, and Before and After shows the lines as they are and as the suggestion would make them.
  • Accept Suggestion commits the suggested lines to the pull request’s branch, with a commit message naming the reviewer (“Apply suggestion from alice”), after showing exactly what will change and asking. It needs permission to push to the branch, and says so when the account does not have it. Accept Selected Suggestions puts several into one commit, as GitHub’s “Add suggestion to batch” does.
  • Decline Suggestion asks for a short reason, replies on the thread with it, and offers to resolve the thread.
  • A suggestion on lines that have changed since it was written cannot be applied cleanly; QUILL says so and offers Before and After against the current lines instead of guessing.

8. Sound, braille and the screen reader

  • Optional line cues. In Patch lines, a soft high tone for an added line, a low one for a removed line, nothing for unchanged. With cues on, the words “Added line” and “Removed line” shorten to the line number. Off by default, a preference (section 10), never required to understand anything.
  • Braille form. Each gist has a compact braille rendering: +14 0.2→0.25, ~7 > → >=, mv 80→20 12L. The full sentence is a press of a routing key or Tell Me More away. Arrow characters fall back to -> on displays and tables that lack them.
  • Nothing the screen reader already says (GATE-13): focus moves are not announced; the gist is the tree row’s own text where possible, so the screen reader reads it as part of the row, and is only announced when the change is reached by a command that does not move focus.
  • Punctuation in full lines is left to the screen reader; only the change description names characters.

9. Keys, menus and help

All keys are window-local (the review and diff windows), so they cannot claim an editor chord, and each follows the family rules (quill/core/family_rules.py). Proposals, to be checked against the window’s existing access keys (GATE-14):

No bare letters: in a tree, typing a letter jumps to the next row starting with it, and reviewers use that to find a file by name.

10. Fine-tuning: your preferences

Jeff, 2026-11-09: “go with great defaults but allow the user to fine tune this the way they wish.” Every choice below has a default chosen so that most people never need to change it, and every one can be changed. They live on one page, Pull Requests, in Preferences’ GitHub area, shared by QUILL and QUILL Lite (the same settings, one implementation, rule 10), each with F1 help written where it is built and keywords for Find a Setting. Each is a field in Settings under the settings gates (the vocabulary audit and GATE-SETDOC).

The defaults are chosen to be quiet and complete, not to be tuned away: the preferences are for taste and habit, never the fix for a sentence that says too much (GATE-13). If a default turns out to be chatty, the sentence is changed, not the default.

11. Documentation

Documentation is part of the feature, written as it is built:

  • GitHub Guide chapter 5 is rewritten as a story: “Your first review”, from opening to sending, with every kind of change heard once, then the reference sections as they are now.
  • A sound gallery: a page, “What a change sounds like”, listing every kind in section 4.2 with its example sentence and braille form, so a person can learn the shapes before meeting them.
  • A lesson in Help > Tutorials: review a small practice pull request (a fixture repository, so it works offline) with five planted changes, one of each size.
  • Release notes tell it as the story it is, with “try this” lines.

12. Testing

  • A corpus of real changes: about 200 hunks taken from public pull requests in several languages, each with the expected kind and gist, checked into tests/fixtures/pull_requests/. The describer must keep passing it; a change to a sentence is a reviewed change to the fixture.
  • Property tests: a gist never exceeds its length; every description round-trips to the exact lines it came from; nothing heuristic is stated as certain.
  • Performance: a 300-file, 20,000-line pull request describes in under a second on the reference machine, off the UI thread (task manager), with the outline usable while it works. GATE-PERF gains a case.
  • Screen-reader checks in docs/qa/ for every new window and sentence, with NVDA, JAWS, Narrator and VoiceOver.
  • The existing gates: GATE-13 (no over-announcing), GATE-14 (access keys), GATE-BRAILLE, help audits, the dialog contract, and QUILL Lite’s coverage gate for every new handler.

13. Phases

Each phase ships on its own and is useful on its own.

Phase What Size
1 The change describer (section 4) with its corpus and its preferences (section 10, Hearing changes), used by the diff window’s Differences list and Compare Documents. Character vocabulary shared with dictation. large
2 Walking: Next/Previous Change and File across the pull request, three depths, comment on this change; the Before and After window (section 6.3), shared with Compare Documents. large
3 The shape sentence and What Is This Pull Request? (without AI). Quick replies. Pending comments by gist. medium
4 Accept and Decline (section 6.6) and the decisions in Compose Review; Viewed (GitHub’s own state), Review Progress, finishing well, line cues and braille forms. large
4b Accepting and declining suggestions as the author (section 7.1), including batches. medium
5 Moves, repeats and generated files across the whole pull request; Explain This Pull Request with consent. medium
6 Docs: chapter 5 as a story, the sound gallery, the practice lesson; release notes. medium, alongside each phase

14. Decisions

Decided 2026-11-09, on Jeff’s direction “great defaults, and let people fine tune”: every open question takes the proposed default, and each becomes a preference in section 10.

  1. Names: “Next Change”, “Tell Me More”, “Spell the Change”, “Before and After”, “What Is This Pull Request?”, “Accept”, “Decline”. Still open to a better word from Jeff or testers before phase 2 ships.
  2. Line cues: off by default; a preference.
  3. Viewed: GitHub’s own viewed state by default; “this computer only” is a preference.
  4. The AI summary: in phase 5, a button, never automatic; can be hidden.
  5. Where the describer is used first: pull requests and Compare Documents together.
  6. The practice repository: a fixture shipped with QUILL, so the lesson works offline.
  7. What Accept means: a private checkmark that counts toward the review; posting “Looks good” is a preference.
  8. A decline needs a reason: yes by default; a preference.
  9. Accepting a suggestion: commit after asking, as GitHub does; collecting first is a preference.
  10. Mark changes: off by default, so the fields hold the code exactly; Words or Symbols when turned on.
