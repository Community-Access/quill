# QUILL, Step by Step

A listen-along manual.

## How to read this book

This book is written to be listened to. Load it into ElevenReader, Voice
Dream, or any app that reads to you, and let it play while you sit at your
computer with QUILL open. When the book says "try it," pause the reading, do
the thing at your own speed, and press play again when you are ready. Nothing
in this book is timed, and nothing breaks if you take a week between chapters.

Each chapter teaches one skill. Not one menu, not one list of shortcuts — one
skill, the kind of thing your hands remember after you have done it three
times. A chapter starts by telling you what the skill is for, walks you
through it slowly, has you do it again a slightly different way, and ends by
naming the two or three keystrokes worth keeping. If a chapter ends and you
want to stop for the day, stop. The next chapter will assume only that you
were awake for the ones before it.

Two promises about the writing. First, this book moves slowly on purpose. If
you already know a thing, skip ahead; the chapter titles say plainly what
each one covers. Second, this book will not spend your attention telling you
how thoughtful QUILL is. You will form your own opinion. The book is here to
teach, and everything else is a distraction.

One note on how keys are written. When you hear "`Ctrl+S`," that means hold the
Control key and press S, then let go of both. When a key has three parts,
like `Ctrl+Shift+P`, hold the first two and press the last. There is exactly
one unusual key in QUILL, and it gets its own chapter much later, after your
hands already trust the ordinary ones.

That is the whole introduction. Let's open the program.

## Chapter 1: Meet the window

Start QUILL. It opens without ceremony — no splash screen, no sound, just a
window. If this is the very first time QUILL has run on this computer, a
short setup called Personalise QUILL may offer to ask a few questions about
how you like to work. You can answer it or dismiss it; either is fine, and
you can always run it again later from the Help menu. This book assumes
nothing about how you answered.

The window has three parts, stacked top to bottom. At the top is the menu
bar: File, Edit, and their relatives. In the middle, filling almost
everything, is the editor — the place where text goes. At the bottom is the
status bar, a single row of short facts about your document: where the
cursor is, how many words there are, and a few more things we will meet
properly later.

You are in the editor right now. Prove it: type your own name. There it is.

Now visit the other two parts and come back. Press the Alt key by itself.
Focus moves up to the menu bar and lands on File. Press the Right arrow a few
times and listen to the menus go by: Edit, View, and onward. Press Escape.
You are back in the editor, and your name is still there. The menus never
take anything away from you; Escape always walks you home.

The status bar works the same way. Press `F6`, and focus moves to the status
bar; press `F6` again or `Shift+F6` to come back to the editor. You do not need
anything from the status bar yet. Just know that it is a place you can visit,
not just a line that sits there.

Try the round trip once more, all of it: Alt to the menus, Escape home. `F6` to
the status bar, back to the editor. Type another word to prove you are home.

That is the skill. The window has three parts; you can visit any of them and
come home with Escape or `F6`, and nothing you wrote goes anywhere while you
travel. Keep two keys from this chapter: Alt for the menus, and Escape for
home.

## Chapter 2: Type something and keep it

This chapter is the heart of the whole program: make a document, put words in
it, and keep those words. Everything else in QUILL is decoration around this.

Press `Ctrl+N`. That makes a new, empty document. You may hear the window title
change to something like "Untitled." Untitled means the words exist only in
QUILL's memory so far — real enough to edit, not yet a file on your disk.

Type two or three sentences. Anything. What you had for breakfast. The
weather. It genuinely does not matter, and nobody will read it.

Now keep it. Press `Ctrl+S`. Because this document has never been saved, QUILL
asks where to put it and what to call it. This is the standard Windows save
dialog, the same one every program uses. Type a name you will recognize —
"practice" is a fine name — pick a folder you can find again, like Documents,
and press Enter. The title changes from Untitled to your file's name. The
words are now on your disk.

Here is the habit that separates relaxed people from worried ones: press
`Ctrl+S` again right now. Nothing dramatic happens — the file already has a
name, so QUILL just saves it quietly. That quiet is the point. `Ctrl+S` costs
you half a second, and after the first save it never asks questions. Press it
whenever you pause to think. You will never wonder whether your last
paragraph is safe, because it always will be.

Now close the document and get it back, because keeping a file is only
useful if you can return to it. Press `Ctrl+W`. The document closes. If you had
typed anything since your last save, QUILL would ask whether to save first —
it will not let a close throw words away silently. Now press `Ctrl+O`, which
opens the standard open dialog. Find your practice file, press Enter, and
your sentences come back.

There is an even faster way back. Open the File menu with Alt, then arrow to
Open Recent. QUILL keeps a list of files you have had open lately; your
practice file is on it. Most days, the file you want is the one you were
working on yesterday, and Open Recent is the shortest road there.

Do the loop once more from the top, without the book: new document with
`Ctrl+N`, a sentence, save with `Ctrl+S` and name it something else, close with
`Ctrl+W`, reopen from Open Recent. When that loop feels boring, you have it.

Keep three keys: `Ctrl+N` for new, `Ctrl+S` for save — pressed often and without
thought — and `Ctrl+O` for open. You now know enough to use QUILL for real
work. Everything after this makes the work more comfortable.

## Chapter 3: When you are stuck, ask the program

You will forget things from this book. That is normal and expected, and QUILL
assumes it. This chapter is about the two keys that answer questions, so that
forgetting costs you ten seconds instead of a trip back to a manual.

The first key is `F1`. `F1` answers the question "what is this thing I am
standing on?" It works on nearly everything — a field in a dialog, a button,
the editor itself. Try it now: press `F1` in the editor. A small window opens
and describes where you are and what applies here. Press Escape to close it.
Now open any dialog — the Preferences window, on `Ctrl+Comma`, will do — move
to any control in it with Tab, and press `F1` again. It tells you what that
control wants and what it affects. Escape closes the help, Escape again
closes Preferences. Nothing was changed by looking.

The second key answers a bigger question: "what can QUILL do, and how do I
say it?" Press `Ctrl+Shift+P`. This opens the Command Palette, which is a
searchable list of every command in the program. Type a word — try "spell" —
and the list narrows to commands about spelling. Arrow through them. Notice
that as you move, each command's keyboard shortcut is read along with its
name. Press Escape without running anything.

That detail about shortcuts matters more than it seems. The palette is not
just a launcher; it is how you learn the keyboard without studying. When you
want something, open the palette and type a word for it. If you use that
command twice, you will have heard its shortcut twice, and the third time you
will press the shortcut instead. The palette teaches you out of needing it.

One more key for a specific moment of confusion: sometimes a menu item or a
command is switched off, gray, unavailable — and nothing says why. When that
happens, press `Alt+F1`, which is the command called Why Is This Unavailable.
QUILL explains the reason in a sentence instead of leaving you to guess.

Practice the pair once. Press `Ctrl+Shift+P`, type "count", and you will find
Document Statistics — run it with Enter and hear how many words your practice
file has. Then press `F1` somewhere, anywhere, and close it. Those two keys are
the whole skill.

Keep two keys: `F1` asks "what is this?", and `Ctrl+Shift+P` asks "how do I...?"
Between them, you can stop memorizing and start looking things up in place.

## Chapter 4: Moving through a document

So far your documents have been three sentences long, and the arrow keys were
all you needed. Real documents are longer, and this chapter is about crossing
them without wearing out the Down arrow.

First, the keys Windows gives every editor, in case nobody ever showed you.
Left and Right move by character. `Ctrl+Left` and `Ctrl+Right` move by word, and
this pair alone doubles most people's speed. Home goes to the start of the
line, End to the end of it. `Ctrl+Home` jumps to the very top of the document,
`Ctrl+End` to the very bottom. Page Up and Page Down move a screenful at a
time.

Open your practice file and spend one unhurried minute with just those:
word by word across a sentence, Home and End a few times, `Ctrl+End` to the
bottom, `Ctrl+Home` back to the top. These keys work identically in almost
every program on your computer, so this minute pays rent everywhere.

Now the QUILL-specific ones. Press `Ctrl+G`, and a small dialog asks for a line
number. Type 1 and press Enter — you are on line one. This is Go to Line, and
it matters most when something else tells you a number: an error message from
some tool, or a friend saying "the odd sentence is around line forty."

Next, a kind of travel you cannot get from arrow keys at all: going back to
where you just were. QUILL remembers where your cursor has been, the way a
web browser remembers pages. Press `Alt+Left`, and the cursor returns to its
previous location — for instance, the place you were before that `Ctrl+G`.
Press `Alt+Right` to go forward again. Try it: `Ctrl+End` to the bottom, then
`Alt+Left`. You are back where you started, not because you retraced your
steps, but because QUILL kept the trail.

This back-and-forward pair changes how it feels to check something far away.
Jump anywhere, look at whatever you needed, press `Alt+Left`, and you are home.
The fear of losing your place is what makes long documents tiring, and this
is the key that removes it.

Keep three things: `Ctrl+Left` and `Ctrl+Right` for words, `Ctrl+Home` and
`Ctrl+End` for the ends of the document, and `Alt+Left` to go back to where you
just were. Longer documents will stop feeling like long hallways.

## Chapter 5: Selecting text

Selecting is how you tell QUILL "I mean this part." Everything you do to
text — copying it, deleting it, making it bold someday — starts by marking
which text you mean. There are two ways to select in QUILL: the Windows way,
and a second way that is kinder to your hands on big selections. You need
both.

The Windows way first: hold Shift while you move. Every movement key from the
last chapter selects when Shift is held. `Shift+Right` selects a character.
`Ctrl+Shift+Right` selects a word. `Shift+End` selects to the end of the line.
`Ctrl+Shift+End` selects everything to the bottom of the document. Try a few
of those in your practice file, and press a bare arrow key afterward to
collapse the selection away. Selecting text touches nothing until you act on
it; marking and unmarking is free.

Now the second way, and if you remember one thing from this chapter make it
this one. Put your cursor somewhere, and press `F8`. QUILL says it is starting
a selection. Now move — plain arrows, `Ctrl+arrows`, End, anything, with no
Shift held — and the selection grows from where you pressed `F8` to wherever
you go. When the selection covers what you meant, just act on it, or press `F8`
again to switch the mode off. Escape also cancels it. This exists because
holding Shift across a three-paragraph journey is genuinely awkward, and a
selection you can steer with free hands is calmer to get right.

QUILL also has commands that select a natural piece of text in one stroke,
without any steering. `Ctrl+Shift+W` selects the word under the cursor.
`Ctrl+Shift+E` selects the current line. `Ctrl+Shift+H` selects the current
paragraph. Try the paragraph one: put your cursor anywhere inside a
paragraph, press `Ctrl+Shift+H`, and the whole paragraph is marked.

Two companions round this out. `Ctrl+Shift+Y` speaks the current selection, so
you can confirm exactly what is marked before you act on it — worth its
weight the first time you are about to delete something. And `Ctrl+Shift+A`
unselects, leaving your cursor where it is, for when you marked something and
changed your mind.

Practice: select a word with `Ctrl+Shift+W`. Select a paragraph with
`Ctrl+Shift+H`, and press `Ctrl+Shift+Y` to hear it read back. Then press `F8`,
walk the cursor down two paragraphs with plain arrows, hear the selection
grow, and press Escape to let it go.

Keep three: Shift plus any movement for small selections, `F8` for big ones,
and `Ctrl+Shift+Y` to hear what you have marked. Next chapter, we finally do
something with a selection.

## Chapter 6: Cut, copy, paste, and the key that forgives

These four keys are the oldest keys in computing, and QUILL uses the same
ones as everything else: `Ctrl+C` copies the selection, `Ctrl+X` cuts it —
copies it and removes it — and `Ctrl+V` pastes at the cursor. If you know them
already, glance at the second half of this chapter, because that part is
QUILL's own.

If they are newer to you, walk it once with the book. In your practice file,
select a sentence — `Ctrl+Space` selects the sentence under the cursor, or use
any method from the last chapter. Press `Ctrl+C`. Nothing seems to happen, but
the sentence is now held on the clipboard. Move to the bottom of the document
with `Ctrl+End`, press Enter for a fresh line, and press `Ctrl+V`. The sentence
appears. That is the entire trick: mark, copy or cut, go somewhere, paste.
The clipboard holds one thing at a time, and it is shared with every program
on the computer, so a sentence copied in QUILL can be pasted into an email.

Now the forgiving key. Press `Ctrl+Z`. The paste you just did is undone — the
sentence is gone from the bottom. Press `Ctrl+Z` again and again, and each
press takes back one more edit, walking backward through everything you have
done. Press `Ctrl+Y` and it re-does what undo took back. Nothing you type or
delete or paste in the editor is beyond `Ctrl+Z`'s reach.

Understand what this means for how boldly you can work. You do not need to be
careful in QUILL. Try the edit; if it is wrong, `Ctrl+Z`. Move the paragraph;
if it reads worse, `Ctrl+Z`. Every mistake in the editor is a rented mistake.
People who trust undo edit better, not because they err less, but because
trying things is how good text happens.

QUILL adds one refinement worth knowing today. Sometimes you undo and are not
sure what just came back or went away — especially after several undos in a
row. `Alt+Shift+F3` is Undo and Say What Changed: it undoes one step and tells
you out loud what that step was. When you are unwinding a series of edits and
counting on your ears, use that instead of bare `Ctrl+Z`.

And one honest paste for the modern world: text copied from web pages drags
its formatting along. `Ctrl+Shift+V` pastes the words only, stripped clean.
When pasting from a browser into your writing, `Ctrl+Shift+V` is nearly always
the one you meant.

Practice the confidence more than the keys: cut a paragraph from one place,
paste it somewhere it does not belong, listen to it there, and undo your way
back to safety.

Keep four: `Ctrl+C`, `Ctrl+X`, `Ctrl+V` — and above all `Ctrl+Z`, the reason none of
the others are scary.

## Chapter 7: Finding and replacing

Somewhere in a long document is the sentence you need to fix, and arrowing
through everything to reach it is not a plan. Finding is a skill of its own,
and it is the last piece of what I would call the survival set — after this
chapter, you can genuinely live in this editor.

Press `Ctrl+F`. The Find window opens with a box for the text you are hunting.
Type a word you know is in your practice file, and press Enter. QUILL jumps
to the first place that word appears and reads you the line, so you have some
context around the hit. The Find window is still open; press Enter again to
go to the next hit, or use the buttons to go forward and back. Press Escape
when you are done, and you are back in the editor at the place you found.

Most of the time you do not need the window at all after the first search.
`F3` repeats the last search from wherever you are — next match, no dialog.
`Shift+F3` goes to the previous match. This makes checking every mention of a
name feel like flipping through index cards: `F3`, listen, `F3`, listen.

If the word is not there, QUILL says so plainly. Not found means not found —
check the spelling, try a shorter fragment. Searching for a fragment is often
smarter than a whole word: hunting "recei" finds both "receive" and
"received", spelled either way.

Now replacing. Press `Ctrl+H`, and the Replace window opens with two boxes:
what to find, and what to put in its place. Here is the one piece of real
advice in this chapter: replace one at a time, not all at once, until you
have seen a few. Find the next hit, hear it in its sentence, replace it,
move on. The Replace All button does the entire document in one stroke, and
it is exactly right when you have renamed a project and forty instances need
the same change — but words live inside other words, and replacing "cat" with
"dog" everywhere also turns "catalog" into "dogalog". `Ctrl+Z` does take back a
replace-all, so even that mistake is rented. But taste a few before you buy
them all.

Practice: search for a word with `Ctrl+F`, walk three hits with `F3`, then use
`Ctrl+H` to change one word to another a couple of times, hearing each one
before you say yes.

Keep three: `Ctrl+F` to find, `F3` for next, `Ctrl+H` to replace. That completes
the survival set — everything from here on is comfort, speed, and reach.

## Chapter 8: Spelling

You could write forever without this chapter; misspelled words save to disk
just fine. But a spell check before anyone else reads your work is cheap
insurance, and QUILL's is built for checking by ear.

Open your practice file and plant a mistake on purpose — type "freind"
somewhere. Now press `F7`. The spell check dialog opens on the first word QUILL
does not recognize, reads it, usually reads the sentence around it, and
offers suggestions. Arrow through the suggestions until you hear "friend,"
and accept it. The dialog moves to the next problem word on its own. When
there is nothing left, it says so, and you are done. Escape leaves the dialog
at any point.

The choices in the dialog are few and honest. Change fixes this occurrence.
Ignore skips this one without fixing it. And Add sends the word to your
personal dictionary, which is the choice people underuse: your own name,
your town, the odd product name you type weekly — the first time the checker
trips on one, add it, and it is never questioned again. Your dictionary
belongs to you, and a word added is a tiny permanent improvement to every
future check.

There is also a way to handle spelling without the ceremony of a dialog.
`Ctrl+F7` jumps the cursor to the next questionable word right in the editor;
fix it by hand, or press `Ctrl+F7` again to keep touring. `Ctrl+Shift+F7` goes to
the previous one. And when the cursor is already sitting on a word you know
is fine, `Ctrl+Alt+F9` adds it to your dictionary on the spot, no dialog at
all. That trio suits the way real proofreading happens: skimming your own
text, pouncing where something is off.

One neighbor worth introducing while we are here: with the cursor on a word,
`Shift+F7` opens a thesaurus and offers alternatives. When a sentence has said
"important" three times, this is the key that rescues it.

Practice: plant two misspellings, run `F7` and fix them both, then add some
word the dictionary does not know — your surname is a good candidate — with
`Ctrl+Alt+F9`.

Keep two habits: `F7` for a full pass before your text meets its readers, and
Add whenever the checker questions a word that is actually right.

## Chapter 9: A pause, and what comes next

No new keys in this chapter. It is here because you have just crossed a real
line, and the book should say so rather than rushing past it.

Count what your hands now know. You can make documents, save them, and get
them back. You can cross a long document, mark exactly the text you mean,
and move it around. You can undo anything, find anything, replace carefully,
and run a spell check by ear. When you are lost, `F1` explains where you are,
and `Ctrl+Shift+P` finds any command from a half-remembered word.

That is not "the basics." That is the actual job. A large share of everything
ever written was written with exactly the set of skills you have right now,
and if you closed this book today, QUILL would serve you well for years.
Nothing in the remaining chapters is required. It is all leverage.

Here is what remains, so you can choose your own order. Chapter ten gives
documents a skeleton — headings — and then lets you travel by that skeleton,
which changes what long documents feel like. Chapter eleven is bookmarks:
leaving markers in your text and snapping back to them. Chapter twelve has
QUILL read your writing aloud to you, which is the single best editing trick
this book knows. Chapter thirteen is working in several documents at once.
Chapter fourteen is the honest chapter about crashes and safety nets.
Chapter fifteen makes QUILL fit you — keys, chattiness, preferences. Chapter
sixteen introduces the one unusual key I promised in the introduction. And
chapter seventeen tells you where to go when this book runs out.

They are written in a sensible order, but they do not lean on each other
much. If reading your work aloud is what made you install this program, jump
to chapter twelve; nothing will feel missing.

Take the break. The next chapter is here whenever you are.

## Chapter 10: Headings, and traveling by structure

A document with no headings is one long corridor: to know what is where, you
walk it. Headings turn the corridor into rooms with names, and once your
documents have rooms, QUILL can carry you between them in single keystrokes.
This chapter builds the rooms first, then does the traveling.

A heading in QUILL carries a level from one to six. Level one is the top —
usually the title, one per document. Level twos are the major sections.
Threes live inside twos. Most working documents never need more than three
levels, and plenty do fine with just twos.

Build one now. Make a new document with `Ctrl+N`. Type "My report" on the
first line, and with the cursor still on that line, press `Ctrl+Alt+1`. That
line is now a heading, level one. Press Enter, type "Background", and press
`Ctrl+Alt+2`. Press Enter and type an ordinary sentence or two — body text
needs no key at all. Then add two more level-two sections the same way:
"Findings" with a sentence under it, and "Conclusion" with another.
`Ctrl+Alt+3` through `Ctrl+Alt+6` make the deeper levels when you someday want
them, and if you overshoot — a heading that should have been plain text —
`Ctrl+Alt+0` turns the line back into body text.

Now the payoff. Press `Ctrl+Home` to go to the top, then press `Ctrl+Alt+H`.
That is Next Heading: the cursor jumps to "Background", skipping the body
text entirely. Press it again — "Findings". Again — "Conclusion".
`Ctrl+Alt+Shift+H` walks backward the same way. Four keystrokes just toured a
document that could have been forty pages, and it is the same four
keystrokes whether it is one page or four hundred.

One more view of the same idea. Press `Ctrl+Shift+O`, and the Outline Navigator
opens: your headings as a list, nothing else. Arrow through it, pick a
heading, press Enter, and you land there in the document. When a report gets
genuinely large, this list is how you hold its shape in mind — the table of
contents that maintains itself.

The habit that makes all this pay is small: when you start anything longer
than a page, give its sections headings as you go. Not for looks — headings
are how you and QUILL will both navigate it next week.

Keep three: `Ctrl+Alt+2` to make a section heading, `Ctrl+Alt+H` to travel by
headings, and `Ctrl+Shift+O` when you want the whole map at once.

## Chapter 11: Bookmarks — leaving a trail

Chapter four gave you `Alt+Left`, the key that returns you to where you just
were. Bookmarks are the deliberate version of the same comfort: instead of
QUILL remembering your trail automatically, you plant a marker on purpose, at
a place you know you will need again.

The simplest one is the temporary bookmark, and it covers most real needs.
Put your cursor anywhere in a document and press `Ctrl+Alt+J`. QUILL confirms
the spot is marked. Now wander — `Ctrl+End`, arrow around, edit something far
away. When you want your spot back, press `Ctrl+Shift+J`, and the cursor
returns exactly there. One marker, two keys, no bookkeeping. The classic
moment for it: you are mid-sentence and suddenly need to check a paragraph
near the top. Drop the marker, go look, snap back, finish the sentence.

When one marker is not enough — a long document where you keep returning to
several places — QUILL has numbered bookmarks. Press `Ctrl+Shift+B` on a line
to set one there; do it somewhere else to set the next. `F2` hops the cursor
forward to the next bookmark in the document, and `Shift+F2` to the previous
one, so a handful of numbered marks become stepping stones you can walk in
both directions. And when you have marked more spots than your memory is
carrying, `Alt+Shift+G` opens a list of all your bookmarks; pick one and press
Enter to land on it.

Two honest notes so the feature never surprises you. Numbered bookmarks are
saved with QUILL and survive closing the file — set marks in a novel
manuscript today and they greet you tomorrow. And the temporary bookmark is
exactly what its name says: one slot, overwritten each time you press
`Ctrl+Alt+J`, meant for right now rather than posterity.

Practice in your headed report from last chapter: drop the temporary mark in
"Findings", jump to the top, come back with `Ctrl+Shift+J`. Then set numbered
bookmarks on two different sections and walk between them with `F2` and
`Shift+F2`.

Keep the pair: `Ctrl+Alt+J` to mark, `Ctrl+Shift+J` to snap back. Reach for the
numbered ones the day a document is big enough to need a trail rather than a
spot.

## Chapter 12: Hearing your writing

Here is the best editing advice this book contains, and it happens to need a
computer: listen to your own writing being read back, at reading speed,
without you doing the reading. Your eyes — and your inner voice — smooth over
what is actually on the page. You wrote it, so you read what you meant. A
voice that is not you reads what is there. Doubled words, a sentence that
never ends, the word "that" four times in two lines: things you would miss
forever on the page are unmissable in the air.

If you use a screen reader, you might reasonably ask why QUILL needs a
reading voice of its own. The difference is the job. Your screen reader
follows your focus and tells you where you are; QUILL's Read Aloud is a
performance of the document, flowing on without your hands on the keys, while
you lean back and just be the audience. Different tool, different posture —
and the posture is the point. Editing ears work best when the hands are
still.

The keys in this chapter use the QUILL key, which chapter sixteen explains
properly. The short version, enough for today: press `Ctrl+Shift+Grave` — the
grave is the back-tick key just above Tab, and you press this all together
like any other shortcut, then release it. QUILL plays a soft double-tone,
meaning "I'm listening for one more key." Then press the second key on its
own. That whole two-step gesture is written "`QUILL Key + R`" in QUILL's menus,
and spoken that way here.

So: open a document with a few paragraphs — your practice file or the report
from chapter ten. Press the QUILL key, then R. Read Aloud begins from your
cursor and flows onward. Let it run a little. The same gesture,
`QUILL Key + R` again, pauses it, and once more resumes. When you have heard
enough, `QUILL Key + Shift+R` stops it entirely.

Two smaller companions do the same job at different sizes. `Alt+F8` is Read
All — the whole document from the top, regardless of where your cursor is,
which is the natural way to take in a finished draft. And `Ctrl+Shift+Y`, which
you met in the selection chapter, speaks just the current selection — right
for testing whether one rewritten sentence finally sounds like a sentence.

The practice for this chapter is really a habit for life: before anything
important leaves your hands — the email that matters, the cover letter, the
chapter — have QUILL read it to you once, start to finish, and fix what your
ears catch. Ears catch what eyes forgive.

Keep one gesture and one habit: `QUILL Key + R` to start and pause the reading,
and a full listen before anything important ships.

## Chapter 13: More than one document

Real work rarely lives in one file. You are writing in one document and
checking notes in another, or copying lines from an old draft into a new
one. QUILL keeps every open document in the same window, and this chapter is
about moving between them without losing your place in any of them.

Have your practice file open, and open the report from chapter ten as well —
`Ctrl+O`, or File, then Open Recent. Two documents are now open, and the window
title tells you which one you are looking at.

The main key is `Ctrl+Tab`: it switches to your next open document. With two
documents, it simply flips between them — press it a few times and hear the
title alternate. With more open, `Ctrl+Tab` walks the whole ring, and
`Ctrl+Shift+Tab` walks it the other way. Each document keeps its own cursor
position while you are away; flipping to notes and back does not cost you
your sentence.

When several documents are open, the ring gets slow, and QUILL has a faster
door: each open document has a number, and `Alt+1` jumps straight to document
one, `Alt+2` to document two, and so on up through `Alt+9`. The Window menu
lists your open documents with their numbers, so you never have to guess —
and after a day of this, `Alt+1` for "the main thing I am writing" and `Alt+2`
for "the notes" becomes muscle rather than thought.

Try the flow that makes this real: in your report, put the cursor in the
Findings section. Flip to the practice file with `Ctrl+Tab`, copy a sentence
with `Ctrl+C`, flip back, and paste it in. That round trip — flip, take, flip
back, drop — is nine tenths of everything people do with two documents.

Closing is the same key you already know: `Ctrl+W` closes the document you are
in, asking about unsaved changes first, and leaves the rest open. After a
long session with many files open, `Ctrl+Shift+F4` closes every document
except the one you are standing in — and if any of the others have unsaved
changes, QUILL asks once about the lot, offering to save all with one answer
rather than interrogating you file by file.

Keep two: `Ctrl+Tab` to flip between documents, `Alt+1` through `Alt+9` to jump by
number. Your notes can stay open all day without ever being in the way.

## Chapter 14: When something goes wrong

This chapter is about crashes, power cuts, and mistakes discovered too late.
Nobody plans for these, so the only honest time to learn the safety nets is
now, while nothing is wrong.

Start with what QUILL does on its own. As you work on a document with unsaved
changes, QUILL keeps recovery snapshots of it in the background, without
being asked. If QUILL is ever cut down mid-sentence — the machine loses
power, Windows restarts itself overnight, the program crashes — then the next
time you start QUILL, it offers you a choice: here is what was open when
things ended badly, would you like it back? Take it, and your almost-lost
words return. Nothing needed from you in advance. It is worth knowing this
before you ever see it, because the offer appearing after a bad evening is a
moment people meet at their most rattled — and the right answer is usually
simply yes.

To be clear about the division of labor: `Ctrl+S` is still the plan. The
recovery offer is the net under the trapeze, and nets are for falling, not
for living on. Save when you pause; let recovery be the thing you almost
never need.

The second net catches a different kind of loss: the file that was saved,
but saved wrong. You deleted a section on Tuesday, saved, and on Thursday
realize you needed it — `Ctrl+Z` cannot help, because undo history belongs to
the editing session, and that session is long over. For this, QUILL keeps
earlier versions of your files. Press `Ctrl+Alt+Shift+E`, Restore Previous
Version, and QUILL shows the versions it holds for the current document,
labeled by when they were saved. You can bring one back — and Tuesday's
section with it.

The third net is the smallest and the one you will use most. QUILL speaks —
confirmations, warnings, results — and speech vanishes the moment it ends.
When something was said and you missed it, or you half-heard a warning while
typing, press `Alt+Shift+E`. That is the Spoken Echo: a list of the last things
QUILL announced, newest first, that you can arrow through at your own pace
and even copy from. Missed announcements stop being missed; they are just in
the Echo, waiting.

Nothing to practice here, and I would not wish the practice on you. Just
carry the three facts: after a crash, QUILL offers your work back — say yes.
For a bad save discovered days later, `Ctrl+Alt+Shift+E` reaches earlier
versions. And when speech got away from you, `Alt+Shift+E` replays it.

## Chapter 15: Making QUILL yours

Everything so far used QUILL exactly as it installs. That default is a fine
place to live, so this chapter is not homework — it is a map of the three
adjustments that matter most, for whenever a default starts to chafe.

The front door is Preferences, on `Ctrl+Comma`. It is a window of settings
organized into pages: how files open, how spell check behaves, what QUILL
says and when, and a great deal more. You do not browse a window like that;
you arrive with a question. And you already own the tool for questions —
`F1`. Land on any setting, press `F1`, and it explains what the setting does and
what changing it would mean. Preferences plus `F1` is how you tune a program
without reading its manual. Escape backs you out, and nothing changes unless
you changed it.

The second adjustment is the keyboard itself. Every shortcut this book has
taught is a default, not a law. `Ctrl+Alt+Shift+R` opens the Keymap Editor,
where you can search for any command and give it the key you wish it had.
Two details make it trustworthy. You can choose Record Keys and simply press
the combination you want, rather than spelling it out. And if the key you
press already belongs to another command, QUILL tells you which one, by
name, and offers to move the key properly — nothing is ever silently
overwritten. If some gesture from an editor you used for years is wired into
your hands, spend the one minute making QUILL match your hands. You will
save that minute back within the week.

The third adjustment is how much QUILL talks. Some people want a running
narration of everything; others want silence broken only by problems. Open
the Command Palette with `Ctrl+Shift+P`, type "verbosity", and you will find
the settings that govern how chatty QUILL is, ready to be tuned in either
direction. There are also sound events — QUILL can mark moments like a save
or a search miss with a short tone instead of words, or alongside them, and
`Alt+Shift+M` switches sound notifications on and off as a whole. Whether a
day goes better with tones, words, both, or neither is entirely a matter of
taste, and it is your taste that gets to decide.

If you adjust nothing today, that is a fine outcome. The point of the
chapter is smaller: when a default does bother you, it is not a fact of
life. It is a setting, `F1` will explain it, and five minutes will fix it.

Keep one key and one idea: `Ctrl+Comma` opens Preferences, and `F1` inside it
means you never have to guess.

## Chapter 16: The QUILL key

You met this key briefly in chapter twelve, where it started Read Aloud.
Now, properly: the QUILL key is the one genuinely unusual thing on QUILL's
keyboard, it unlocks the program's larger features, and it works differently
from every other shortcut you know.

An ordinary shortcut is one gesture: hold modifiers, press a letter,
done. The QUILL key is instead a prefix — a way of getting QUILL's full
attention first. Press `Ctrl+Shift+Grave` (that back-tick key above Tab, all
pressed together like any chord, then released). QUILL answers with its soft
double-tone: "listening." Now press one more key, alone, and that second key
picks the command. Menus write this two-step as "`QUILL Key + R`", "QUILL Key
+ G", and so on.

Why would a program complicate a keystroke this way? Arithmetic. The
ordinary combinations — Ctrl plus this, Ctrl and Shift plus that — are nearly
all spoken for, in QUILL and in Windows itself. The prefix opens a whole
second keyboard: every letter, fresh again, and QUILL uses that space for
its bigger machinery. You already know `QUILL Key + R`, reading aloud. QUILL
Key + D starts dictation — speaking your words instead of typing them, on
machines set up for it. `QUILL Key + I` asks QUILL to describe an image. QUILL
Key + Y turns a whole document into spoken audio you can keep.
None of these need to be learned today; what should settle in is the shape —
prefix, tone, one letter — because every big feature you meet from now on
will speak it.

Two useful corners of the mechanism. If you press the QUILL key and change
your mind, Escape cancels the prefix, and pressing it by accident costs
nothing — the little tone, then Escape, and no harm done. And you never have
to memorize what the second keys are: press the QUILL key and then the
question mark, and a cheat sheet opens listing every chord the prefix knows.
`QUILL Key + ?` is the directory for the whole neighborhood.

Try the shape three times, none of them dangerous. `QUILL Key + R` to start
your document reading, and again to pause it. `QUILL Key + ?` to open the
cheat sheet and arrow down what lives here — just looking. And once, press
the QUILL key and then Escape, so your hands know that backing out is free.

Keep the shape, not a list: `Ctrl+Shift+Grave`, the tone, one letter. And
`QUILL Key + ?` whenever you wonder what else it can say.

## Chapter 17: Where to go from here

This book ends here, but it was never the whole story — it is the floor of
the house. You can stand on it. Here is what is above, and honestly what
each thing is good for.

The follow-along tutorials, on QUILL's website under Tutorials, are the
natural next room. Where this book taught the editor, each tutorial walks
one real project end to end at the keyboard: rescuing a scanned PDF into
clean text, turning a document into an audiobook, keeping a linked vault of
notes, listening to internet radio and podcasts without leaving your editor.
When one of those is a thing you actually want, an hour with its tutorial
gets you there.

The QUILL Cast is the same teaching in audio form — an episode series you
can subscribe to in any podcast app, with a full transcript for every
episode. Episodes are short; a commute fits two of them. It suits the
moments when your hands are busy but your ears are free.

The full user guide is the reference book, open from inside QUILL with
`Ctrl+Shift+F1`. Nobody reads it cover to cover, and nobody should. It is
where the complete answer lives when you need everything QUILL knows about
one topic. Ask it questions; do not move into it.

And QUILL can do things this book deliberately left alone: take dictation,
describe images, translate, sit an AI assistant beside your work for the
days you want one — all of it switched off until you ask. When you get
curious, the Help menu's tutorials, on `Ctrl+Alt+F1`, include gentle
introductions, and the setup wizards walk you through anything that needs
installing. Curiosity is the only prerequisite.

If you ever need a person rather than a document, `Ctrl+Alt+F2` — Get Help
from Support — opens a short form and hands it to your own mail program,
addressed to people who read what arrives. Real problems and honest
suggestions are both welcome there; a report like the one that caused this
book to be written is how the program gets better.

Last thing, and then the book will stop talking. Six weeks from now, open
the Command Palette and just arrow through it for two minutes. You will find
a command you did not know you had, and by then you will simply take it and
use it — because somewhere along these seventeen chapters, this stopped
being software you were learning and became a tool you own. That was the
whole idea.

Thanks for listening. Go write something.
