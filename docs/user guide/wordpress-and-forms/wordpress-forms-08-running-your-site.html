Chapter 8: Running your site

What this chapter covers

Beyond the writing, somebody has to look after a site: its health, its settings, its plugins and themes, its look and its updates. If that is you, this chapter is for you. Most of it needs the Administrator role, and the Workbench only offers what your role can use.

You will read your site’s overview and health, change its settings, look after plugins, themes and fonts, change the site’s look and its templates, and see what needs updating. You will also meet the tools for the jobs WordPress only does in wp-admin.

By the end you will know how your site is, and how to keep it that way. Everything that changes the site shows you what will change, and asks first.

QUILL asks the site first

Sites differ. One runs the newest WordPress with a block theme, another an older version with a classic theme and a security plugin. QUILL reads what each site offers, once a session, before it offers anything. A command your site does not have, or your role cannot use, says why in one sentence, such as “Community Radio does not offer this. It may be turned off, or the site may need a newer WordPress.” Nothing is sent.

The site at a glance

Each site’s Overview in the Workbench (Ctrl+2) is wp-admin’s Dashboard, in words. It has three places:

  • At a Glance says the counts as sentences: “Posts: 12 published, 3 drafts, 1 scheduled”, then pages, comments, people, the WordPress version and the theme. If the site runs WooCommerce, The Events Calendar, Yoast SEO or Paid Memberships Pro, each gets a sentence too, such as orders waiting or events coming up. A count your role cannot see is left out, never said as zero.
  • Activity lists what is publishing soon, what was published lately, and the newest comments, five of each. Enter on a post opens it in the editor.
  • wp-admin Screens lists every wp-admin screen, the place in the Workbench nearest to it, and whether the Workbench does all of it, part of it, or none. Enter says what QUILL cannot do there and opens the screen in your browser. Go to Its Place in the Workbench takes you to the place.

Site Health

Tools > WordPress > Site Check opens the Workbench at the site’s Site Health. It lists what the site says about its own health, critical things first: the WordPress version, HTTPS, the time zone, readable addresses, and every Site Health test the site runs, each in one line. **Needs My Attention

Site Health** gathers the problems from every site, worst first.

If the authorization header test fails, the row explains that application passwords cannot work on that site until the host fixes it. That one line has saved many people an afternoon.

WordPress gives each problem a label and not much else. In the Workbench, Enter on a Site Health row is Explain: what the problem means for the people who visit your site, what usually causes it, and the steps to fix it, followed by WordPress’s own description. The row’s context menu has three more:

  • Fix… does the fix from here when that is possible. A fixed time offset becomes a city time zone, such as Europe/London, after a preview, and Ctrl+Z puts it back. When only wp-admin can fix it, such as the address style under Permalinks, Fix… says why and opens that wp-admin screen. When nothing outside the server can fix it, the command is greyed and says what to ask your host.
  • Take Me There goes to the place in the tree where it is fixed, such as Settings or Plugins.
  • Open in Editor works on a broken link, below.

The same commands work on Needs My Attention > Broken Links. There, Fix… asks for the right address, shows how many times the post holds the old one, and changes it after you say yes; Ctrl+Z puts the old link back. Open in Editor opens the post that holds the link, when you would rather change the words around it too.

Under Site Health:

  • Information shows the tests and how much room the site’s folders take. The rest of wp-admin’s Info tab, such as the server and database, has no way in from outside, so its last row opens that tab in your browser.
  • Security asks the site whether XML-RPC, an old way in that QUILL never uses, still answers, and warns you if it does.

Search and Upkeep, in each site’s branch, covers whether search engines may list the site, its sitemap, scheduled posts that missed their time, the site’s certificate, and who is editing what. Every row ends with where QUILL’s answer came from.

Site settings

Tools > WordPress > Site Settings… opens the Workbench at the site’s Settings: the title, tagline, language, time zone, date format, front page, posts page, site icon and more, each on its own row.

To change one, arrow to it and press Alt+Enter. Change the value and press OK. QUILL reads the setting again first, and checks afterwards that the site kept it.

Each row says what the setting does on your site, with your own example: your posts page by name, what a date looks like in your date format. Below the settings you can change are the ones WordPress keeps to wp-admin, still explained with your own site:

  • Permalinks says what your newest post’s address would become under each choice (Plain, Day and name, Month and name, Numeric and Post name), and which one your site uses now.
  • The Discussion rules say how many of your last hundred comments each rule would have held: manual approval, a previously approved comment, two or more links, and signed-in visitors only.
  • Search engine visibility says what that check box really does: it asks search engines to leave the site out, and hides nothing from anyone with the address.
  • Plugin settings rows, for WooCommerce, The Events Calendar, Yoast SEO, Gravity Forms, Paid Memberships Pro and the others QUILL recognises, say which of the plugin’s settings the Workbench reaches. Open Its Documentation opens the plugin’s own help.

Open in Dashboard, on any of these, opens the wp-admin screen that changes it.

The site language matters more than it looks. It decides which voice a visitor’s screen reader uses for the whole site. Language Pack Status…, on a setting’s context menu, says each site’s language and whether WordPress.org has its translation for that WordPress version. It asks WordPress.org, so QUILL asks you first, naming the address and what is sent.

Plugins

Tools > WordPress > Manage > Plugins… opens the Workbench at the site’s Plugins: every plugin, whether it is active, and its version.

  • Activate or Deactivate turns the selected plugin on or off, after a preview. Before you turn one off, QUILL asks the site what depends on it and puts the answer in the question: other plugins that need it, pages and posts that use its blocks or shortcodes, and the QUILL features that use it. A plugin QUILL itself relies on, such as QUILL Accessible Forms, also says what stops working. Ctrl+Z turns it back on.
  • What Depends on It asks the same question without changing anything. Use it before you delete a plugin in wp-admin, too.
  • Find a Plugin on WordPress.org… searches the plugin directory and installs the one you choose, switched off, after a preview.
  • Check on WordPress.org… reads every plugin on the site from the directory: its rating, how many sites use it, when it was last updated, the WordPress version it was tested with, and what it needs. A plugin WordPress.org has closed, or has not seen updated in over two years, gets a warning. Only the plugins’ short names are sent, and QUILL asks first.
  • Open in Dashboard opens wp-admin’s Plugins screen in your browser.

What WordPress cannot do from outside. WordPress lets apps install, activate, deactivate and delete plugins, but not update them, and there is no way to put back an earlier version. If a plugin causes trouble, deactivate or delete it. An earlier version only comes back from the site’s own backup, or an upload in wp-admin.

Themes and fonts

Tools > WordPress > Manage > Themes… opens Appearance > Themes: the installed themes and what the active one supports. WordPress does not let apps switch or install themes, so Activate… and Open in Dashboard take you to the right wp-admin screen. Find a Theme on WordPress.org… searches the theme directory, after asking.

Tools > WordPress > Manage > Fonts… opens Appearance > Fonts. Each font is described in words, such as “serif, suits body text”.

Updates

Needs My Attention > Updates lists, for every site, WordPress itself with its version, every plugin and theme with the version installed, and, where the site runs it, Site Health’s automatic updates test.

WordPress does not tell apps whether a newer version is waiting, so the Update column says “Not reported over REST” unless the site itself says otherwise. QUILL never guesses. Enter on any row opens that site’s Dashboard > Updates in your browser, where updates are run. The Workbench never updates anything itself.

Check for Updates on WordPress.org…, on a plugin, theme or Updates row, asks WordPress.org which versions are current, after you agree. For a plugin with an update waiting, it also reads the plugin’s changelog for the versions you would skip. The row then says how many versions those are, and says plainly when the changelog mentions security fixes, breaking changes or a database update. A database update is the one to back up for, because turning the plugin off afterwards does not undo it. Read in Full shows what the changelog says for each of those versions, as plain text.

To hear about updates as they come, turn on the Watch for them, below.

The site’s look

The look of a block theme lives in Appearance in the Workbench. You need to be an Administrator for all of it, and QUILL tells you before any window opens if you are not.

Global Styles lists the theme’s colours and font sizes. Each colour says how it contrasts with the page text and background. Change… previews a new value, with its contrast, and Ctrl+Z puts it back.

Templates and Template Parts list the theme’s templates, such as Single Posts, and its parts, such as the header. On one:

  • Edit Block Markup… opens it as text. When you press OK, the change is read back line by line, such as “Line 4 added”, before anything is sent. From then on the site keeps its own copy. Ctrl+Z puts it back.
  • Earlier Versions… lists the versions the site kept, and restores the one you pick after the same preview.
  • Reset to the Theme’s Version… removes the site’s copy, so the theme’s own is used again. A template the theme never had is deleted instead, and the preview says so. Ctrl+Z brings your copy back.
  • New Template… asks for a name such as single-post-news or page-about, and starts from the template WordPress would use for it today.
  • Open in the Site Editor opens it in wp-admin, for laying it out by sight.

Additional CSS has one row. On a block theme, Edit Additional CSS… opens the CSS as text, warns that it changes every page, and reads the change back before saving. CSS with tags in it, or braces that do not pair up, is refused before anything is sent. A classic theme keeps its CSS in the Customizer, so its row opens that in your browser.

Patterns lists the synced patterns, the blocks you reuse and change in one place. Tools > WordPress > Manage > Synced Patterns… opens it.

Widgets and Sidebars lists a classic theme’s widget areas, such as the sidebar or the footer. Tools > WordPress > Manage > Widgets… opens it. On an area:

  • Add a Widget… offers every kind of widget the site has. A Block widget asks for its words. Any other kind, such as Search or Recent Posts, opens on its own settings, labelled as WordPress labels them.
  • Change a Widget…, Move a Widget… and Remove a Widget… work on one of the area’s widgets. A removed widget goes to Inactive Widgets, where WordPress keeps it.
  • Preview Widgets reads the whole area as a visitor hears it.

Each one is previewed, Ctrl+Z undoes it, and a widget somebody changed in the meantime is never overwritten.

The look, from the editor

These are in Tools > WordPress > Design and Manage, for sites with a block theme:

  • Style Variations… lists the ready-made looks your theme comes with, each with how many of its colour pairings are too faint for body text. Details reads out its whole Palette Report, and Apply makes one the site’s look. QUILL keeps the look you had first, so Put Back the Previous Look brings it back.
  • Site Styles… lists your site’s colours and font sizes by name, each with how it reads on the page background, such as “Accent (dark green), #1a4d2e, 7.2 to 1 on the page background, passes”.
  • Previous Looks… lists your site’s earlier styles, dated. Go Back to This Look… restores one, and keeps the look you had.
  • Download a Copy of the Site’s Design… saves a zip of your block theme with your changes, a good thing to have before you try something new.
  • Template Outline lists a block theme’s templates and their blocks, starting with the one your open page uses. It explains the things on a page that are not in the page, such as a sign-up box the theme adds to every post.

The shape of the site

  • Tools > WordPress > Manage > Site Map… opens the Workbench at Pages, shown as a tree, each page under its parent.
  • Tools > WordPress > Manage > Page Order… shows your pages as a tree. Moving one sets its parent and its place.
  • Tools > WordPress > Manage > Bulk Edit… lists your posts or pages. Select the ones you want, then change their status, author, comments or parent, or add a category or tag. Undo Bulk Edit puts them back. The Workbench does the same with Alt+Enter on several rows (Chapter 3).
  • Tools > WordPress > Manage > Space Used says how much room your uploads, themes, plugins, WordPress itself and the database take, in plain words.

Content Model, in each site’s branch, lists every kind of content and every way of grouping the site has, with who added it, such as Gravity Forms or The Events Calendar. These are defined in a plugin’s or theme’s code, so they are read only here. Their items are still edited in their own lists.

Jobs only wp-admin does

Some of WordPress has no way in from outside: updates, switching themes, privacy requests, most of Site Health’s Info tab. QUILL says so on the row where it matters, before you act, and takes you to the right screen.

  • On any place in a site’s tree, the context menu has wp-admin for This Place…, which offers the screens nearest to it.
  • Privacy Requests has a row for each of WordPress’s export and erase screens. Enter opens it in your browser. QUILL cannot see whether a request was done there, and never says it was.
  • Administration Areas lists every part of running a site, such as backups, security, caching, translation, email, newsletters, courses, events, analytics and sharing, and says for each what QUILL does and where the rest is.
  • More from This Site lists the other ways in the site’s plugins offer, grouped by plugin, with each one’s routes.

AI Abilities and Ask the Site

WordPress 6.9 lets a site offer actions to AI, called abilities, such as “list upcoming events” or “create a draft”.

  • Tools > WordPress > Manage > AI Abilities… opens the Workbench at the site’s Abilities, each by name. Run… asks for what it needs, shows a preview, then runs it. Offer to Ask the Site, on the same context menu, turns an ability on or off for Ask the Site. An ability that can change the site asks you first. Your choice is kept on this computer.
  • Tools > WordPress > Manage > Ask the Site… sends your question to the AI you chose in Choose Your AI. It only offers the AI the abilities you have turned on for that site, each one separately, and says so if none is on. If the AI wants to use one, QUILL asks you first, every time, and says exactly what it will be given. The answer ends by saying what ran on your site.

Watches

Watches tell you when something happens on your sites while QUILL is open. They are all off until you turn them on.

  1. In the Workbench, choose Tools > Workbench Options… (Ctrl+Shift+O).
  2. Check the Watches you want, and press OK.

You can watch for new comments waiting, new form responses, plugin updates available, scheduled posts going live, a site not answering and answering again, items with a reviewer leaving the queue, Site Health status changes, and form spam questions, once a week. Site Health stays silent for an account that is not an administrator.

Each site is checked no more often than every fifteen minutes. The first check is silent, so you only hear about what changes after it. Go > Watches (Ctrl+5) says when the last round ran and whether each site is answering.

The REST Console

For anything a site offers that has no command of its own, Tools > REST Console… (Ctrl+Shift+T) in the Workbench sends a request to any route the site has. It completes route names as you type, the Accepts box lists what a request takes, and What It Returns says what comes back. A change is previewed first, with any missing or unknown arguments named, and goes in Change History.

Save Request keeps a request you use often. Tools > Saved Requests lists them, numbered 1 to 9, and Tools > Run a Saved Request… (Ctrl+Alt+Q) opens the console with one. Tools > Forget a Saved Request… (Ctrl+Alt+Shift+F) removes one.

Before a big change

Take a snapshot first. Site > Take a Snapshot… (Ctrl+Alt+S) saves the site’s content on this computer, and Site > Put Back from a Snapshot… (Ctrl+Alt+Shift+S) sets it back. Chapter 9 shows how.

Signing out of a site

Tools > WordPress > Manage > Sign Out This Site… revokes QUILL’s own application password on the site, as Chapter 1 describes.

Across all your sites

These are in Tools > WordPress > Your Sites, and look at every site you have connected:

  • Coming Up… lists everything scheduled on all your sites, soonest first.
  • What Changed on My Sites… lists new comments, posts other people published, and health warnings since you last looked. It only appears when you ask. Run it again and it says nothing is new.

The Workbench’s Across Sites and Fleet do much more, and Chapter 9 covers them.
