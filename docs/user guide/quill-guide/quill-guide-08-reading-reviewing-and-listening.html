Chapter 8: Reading, reviewing and listening

This chapter is about taking things in rather than typing them. You will have QUILL read your document to you, look closely at text you did not write, see the formatting that normally stays hidden, and compare two versions of a file. Later on it covers turning documents into audiobooks, and keeping your place in everything you listen to. Start with Read Aloud. The rest is there when you need it.

Read Aloud

Read Aloud speaks your document in a voice of your choosing, separate from your screen reader. It is handy for proofreading by ear, for resting your ears from a fast screen reader voice, or for simply listening to a long piece. You can start, pause, stop, preview, and choose a voice.

Starting and stopping

Everything for listening lives on Tools > Reading and Dictation:

  • Read Aloud is a submenu:
    • Start / Pause (press the QUILL key, then R) starts reading from the cursor, and pauses or carries on when you press it again.
    • Voice… and Settings… choose the voice and how fast, how loud and how high it speaks.
    • Generate Audio… saves the reading as a sound file instead of playing it, described below.
    • Announcement Backend… chooses how QUILL’s own short messages reach you: automatically, through your screen reader, or on the status bar only.
    • Announcement Trace (in Settings)… opens Settings at the switch that keeps a record of QUILL’s announcements. It is only useful when support asks you for it, and it never records your document.
  • Stop Reading (press the QUILL key, then Shift+R) stops the current reading straight away.
  • Say Selected (Ctrl+Shift+Y) reads the text you have selected.
  • Read All (Alt+F8) reads from the cursor to the end of the document.
  • OCR Image…, OCR Clipboard Image and OCR Screen Capture… turn a picture of text into text you can read. There is more on them under OCR Image, later in this chapter.

Generate Audio… makes a recording of your document in your Read Aloud voice. It uses the selection if you have one, and the whole document if you do not. Choose a name and a type in the save window: a Wave file always, and MP3, M4A, M4B audiobook, OGG, Opus or FLAC as well when the free ffmpeg tool is on your computer. Making audio from your documents, later in this chapter, covers the bigger jobs.

There is one setting worth knowing about. Move cursor to follow Read Aloud (in Settings > Read Aloud) makes the cursor select each sentence as it is read, so the caret tracks what you hear and stops where the reading stopped. It is off by default. With a screen reader running, moving the selection makes the screen reader say “selected” over the Read Aloud voice. If you are sighted or have low vision and want the cursor to follow along, turn it on.

The rest of the Read Aloud page in Settings tunes the voice:

  • Read Aloud engine chooses which speech engine reads. Choosing a voice, below, explains them.
  • Read Aloud rate is the speed of the Windows voice in words per minute, from 80 to 450. The default is 200.
  • Read Aloud volume runs from 0 to 100. The default is 100.
  • Read Aloud pitch runs from 0 to 100. The default is 50.
  • Read Aloud sentence pause (milliseconds) adds a pause between sentences, up to two seconds. The default is no extra pause.
  • ElevenLabs Read Aloud voice and ElevenLabs Read Aloud model are used when the engine is ElevenLabs. Pick a voice in the Voice Browser rather than typing one; left blank, ElevenLabs uses its default voice and model.
  • AI Voice provider, AI Voice model and AI Voice choose the cloud voice used by Read Aloud with AI Voice and by audio export: OpenAI (the default), Google Gemini, or ElevenLabs (export only). Leave the model and voice on Provider default unless you have a favourite.

Choosing a voice

Read Aloud uses voices on your own computer. The Windows system voice runs on SAPI 5. It is always there, works offline and needs no download, so Read Aloud works the moment QUILL is installed. eSpeak NG, Piper, and Kokoro (neural, offline) are extra downloads from Speech Center, which keeps the installer smaller. DECtalk works if you have installed it yourself; QUILL no longer downloads or distributes it, because its redistribution terms are unclear. Before any download starts, QUILL tells you what is available right now and what it suggests you do next. For cloud voices, see Read Aloud with AI Voice, which covers OpenAI and Google Gemini voices.

The Kokoro voice models (about 120 MB) and the eSpeak NG engine with its voice data (about 40 MB) are not in the installer. The first time you choose one of these voices, QUILL downloads it for you from its own verified source. The download is checksum-checked and has a progress window you can cancel. Your other voices, including the SAPI 5 system voice, keep working while it downloads. If you are upgrading from a version that bundled these, your existing copies are kept and keep working, so there is nothing to download.

Phonon voices

Phonon is a newer neural voice that comes with QUILL, so there is nothing to download. It has 37 English voices plus its own default voice, and it runs entirely on your computer. It starts talking quickly, which you notice most when you press a key and want to hear something now. It reads numbers, dates and email addresses well on its own.

Pick it in Manage Voices like any other engine, choose a voice, and press Preview to hear it. The speed control goes from 0.85 to 1.3 times normal. That is a narrower range than Kokoro’s, because Phonon starts to fall apart past it, and QUILL would rather tell you than let you pick a speed that sounds broken. QUILL remembers your choices as read_aloud_phonon_voice and read_aloud_phonon_speed.

Phonon needs a processor from about 2015 or later (one with AVX2). On an older computer the voices are still listed, marked “needs a newer computer”, and choosing one tells you so and suggests Piper, which runs almost anywhere. Phonon never sends your text anywhere.

Phonon 31m is by Gradium and is free under the Apache 2.0 licence. Phonon builds on Pocket TTS and the Mimi codec, developed by Kyutai. QUILL offers no voice cloning, and the voices should not be used to pass speech off as a real person’s.

Manage Voices is the Speech (Offline) tab of the Speech Settings dialog. It lists SAPI 5, DECtalk, Piper, Kokoro, Phonon, and eSpeak NG. ElevenLabs, the only cloud voice, has its own Speech (Online) tab, so local and cloud voices are never mixed in one list. The Set as Default button (also on the voice list’s right-click menu) applies your engine and voice choice at once, without closing the dialog with OK.

To hear a voice before you choose it:

  1. In Manage Voices, select the voice.
  2. Press the Preview button.

If the voice is already downloaded, QUILL speaks the preview phrase with that voice’s real model. If it is not downloaded yet (a Kokoro voice, for example), QUILL plays a short pre-recorded sample so you can still hear it before you decide. The rate, volume, pitch, and speed controls only apply to real speech, so they stay dimmed until the voice is downloaded.

Starting a new preview stops the one already playing, so previews never overlap. Some neural voices take a moment to get going. While they do, QUILL plays a short earcon and, by default, says “Generating preview, please wait”. You can turn either off on its own: the earcon in Tools > Reading and Dictation > Sound Scheme…, and the announcement in Settings > Accessibility and Announcements. While a preview is generating or playing, the Preview/Test button becomes a Stop button, so you can cancel it at any time.

Voices in other languages

Read Aloud speaks more than English. The Windows engine lists every voice installed on your PC, in any language. Add one in Windows Settings under Time & language > Speech > Manage voices (Italian’s Elsa, say, or a Spanish, French, German, or Japanese voice) and it appears in QUILL’s voice list, ready to use, with nothing to download from QUILL.

The offline neural voices go further too:

  • Kokoro includes Spanish, French, Hindi, Italian, and Brazilian Portuguese voices. They are part of the voice pack you already downloaded, so picking one just works.
  • The Piper catalog includes Italian voices (Paola and Riccardo) that you download like any other.
  • eSpeak NG offers those languages from its built-in data.

Pick the voice whose language matches your document, and Read Aloud, audiobook export, and batch speech all speak it correctly.

Text cleanup for speech

Real documents are full of typography that trips up speech engines. The optional text cleanup pass (turn it on in Read Aloud settings) fixes curly quotes, em and en dashes, ellipses, invisible spaces, ligatures, bullets, symbols, fractions, and emoji, the same way every time. It also speaks phone numbers, emails, and URLs clearly. For each type you choose whether to announce it, speak it, or speak it and then repeat it, which makes an address easy to catch. Cleanup runs first, before anything else, so live Read Aloud and batch export sound the same.

Teaching QUILL how to say a word

Tools > Speech > Manage Pronunciations… teaches QUILL how to say particular words: names, brands, acronyms, and technical terms. Each entry is a substitution, either literal or a regular expression, and can be case-sensitive. QUILL applies it silently to the text just before speaking, so a correction is heard everywhere speech happens, in batch export and live Read Aloud alike.

  • A dictionary can be global (all projects) or belong to one project.
  • An entry can target a specific engine.
  • You can preview an entry to hear the result.
  • A small starter dictionary already covers common terms.

SSML Builder and SSML playback

For fine control over how a passage is spoken, the SSML Builder dialog writes <speak> markup for you from ordinary controls: emphasis, pauses (<break>), say-as, phonemes, and prosody. A read-only preview shows the markup it made, so you never type the tags yourself. Read Aloud plays this markup natively on SAPI 5 and eSpeak NG, so the pauses and emphasis happen instead of being read out.

Read Document in Browser (experimental)

Your web browser has voices QUILL’s own engines cannot reach, including Microsoft Edge’s “Online (Natural)” voices. This optional feature reads your document with them.

To turn it on:

  1. Open Settings > Experimental.
  2. Check the acknowledgement box.
  3. Turn on Read the document aloud in your browser.
  4. Apply Settings. It works straight away, with no restart.

A Read in Browser (Experimental) item then appears under Tools > Reading & Dictation > Read Aloud, and the command palette gains Read Document in Browser (Experimental).

When you use it, QUILL writes a self-contained, accessible reader page and opens it in the browser you chose under Settings > General > Preview browser. The page has:

  • a labelled Voice picker. Each voice is marked “on this device” or “online”, and the list is filtered to your QUILL display language. Check Show all languages to see the rest.
  • a Speed slider.
  • Play and Stop buttons.

Focus lands on Play, changes are announced through a live region, and Escape stops. Reading goes section by section, so even a book-length document stays reliable. Pause keeps your place and tells you where you are (“Paused at section 12 of 300”), and resuming carries on from that section. Stop goes back to the top. Your voice and speed are remembered between sessions, and the document text carries your display-language tag so a screen reader reading the page pronounces it correctly.

About privacy: QUILL itself makes no network call, and no audio file is made. Voices marked “on this device” speak locally. The browser’s “Online (Natural)” voices are made in the voice company’s cloud, so choosing one sends the text being read to that service. Pick an on-device voice to keep everything local. The reader page holds your document text. It is written to QUILL’s app-data folder and deleted when you close QUILL, so no plain copy is left behind.

Voice commands on the same menu

The Speech menus also hold QUILL’s voice commands. They are listed here so you know what they are when you meet them. Chapter 9, Dictation and transcription, covers them properly.

  • Voice Command (Offline) (Tools > Speech) is push-to-talk for QUILL. Run it, speak one command such as “save file”, “word count” or “next heading”, then run it again to stop and act. Recognition happens entirely on your computer with the offline speech engine, and only a safe list of non-destructive commands can ever run, so a misheard phrase cannot do damage. Say “cancel” or “never mind” to give up. Turn it on with the Voice commands (push-to-talk) setting. It is off by default and always off in Safe Mode.
  • Voice Conversation Mode (Tools > Speech) is the hands-free version. Run it once and QUILL listens for a command, then keeps a short follow-up window open after acting, so you can chain commands (“next heading”, then “read aloud”, then “word count”) without arming it again. A sound marks every state: a rising chime when conversation mode starts, a soft two-note when the microphone opens, a gentle rise when your command is recognized (with a brief moment to say “cancel” before it runs), a sparkle when the action finishes, a quiet tick while QUILL is working, and a calm falling tone if nothing matched. Run it again, or say “stop”, to turn it off. The nine cues are Sound Events you can retune or replace in any sound pack. Four settings let you pace it to your own rhythm: the pause that ends a command, the cancel window, the follow-up window, and the “still working” tick interval. Set any of them to 0 to switch that part off. It uses the same on-device recognition and the same safe list as Voice Command, and is also off in Safe Mode.
  • Listen for Hey QUILL (Tools > Speech) turns on always-listening. QUILL listens continuously, on your computer, for the phrase “Hey QUILL”. Say “Hey QUILL, save file” to run a command straight away, or just “Hey QUILL” to start one. The status bar shows that the microphone is live and plays a reminder now and then, so an open microphone never takes you by surprise. Run the command again, or say “stop”, to end it. The Listen for ‘Hey QUILL’ (wake word) check box on the Transcription page of Settings is the same switch, so turning it on in either place turns it on in both; check it and press OK, and QUILL starts listening straight away. If voice commands are off, QUILL tells you and leaves the box unchecked. Normally listening stops when you close QUILL, so the microphone is never on by surprise the next time. Turn on Keep listening for “Hey QUILL” across restarts and QUILL picks up listening again when it starts. Off in Safe Mode.
  • Speak Voice Status (Tools > Speech) tells you what the voice features are doing right now: whether QUILL is listening for “Hey QUILL”, whether conversation mode is on, and whether it is listening for a command. When none of them is, it says “Voice is not listening right now”, which is the quick way to be sure the microphone is closed.
  • Ask a question by voice. In conversation or wake mode, if you speak a question rather than a command (starting with “ask”, or shaped like a question, such as “how do I save my document”), QUILL opens Ask QUILL with the question filled in and ready to send. You press Enter to send it, so a person always decides what reaches the AI. This needs AI set up. Questions are the only thing voice ever sends to a network service, and never on their own.

For the complete reference (every voice mode, every spoken command, and the privacy rules) see Voice Interaction.

Reading without changing anything: the Read-Only Guard

When you open a document only to read it, such as a manual, a long book or minutes you must not alter, one stray key can change it, and a Ctrl+S later saves the change. The Read-Only Guard locks the document against edits while you read.

Press Ctrl+Alt+Backspace, or choose Tools > Advanced > Toggle Read-Only Guard. You hear “Document is read-only”. Press it again to hear “Document is editable”.

While the guard is on:

  • Typing, Backspace, Delete, Enter and pasting do nothing.
  • Commands that change text say “Document is read-only” on the status bar and change nothing: snippets, the line tools, case changes, Copy Tray pastes, Markdown and HTML inserts, and a thesaurus or dictionary Replace, among others. They stop before they say anything, so you never hear “Inserted snippet” about a snippet that was not inserted.
  • Reading and moving work as always: the arrow keys, Find (Ctrl+F), Go To (Ctrl+G), browse-mode navigation by heading, paragraph and link, selecting and copying, Read Aloud.
  • Bookmarks work as usual. They are kept beside the document, not inside it, so setting one never changes the file.
  • Document notes work too. A note you add with Add Inline Note (Alt+Shift+I) while the guard is on is kept beside the file instead of written into it, and Alt+Shift+J and Alt+Shift+K still move between notes.

QUILL remembers the guard for that file. Close QUILL, open the same file tomorrow by double-clicking it in File Explorer, from Open Recent or with Ctrl+O, and it opens locked again, even if File Explorer spells the folder with different capitals. QUILL Lite has the same guard on the same key, with its own list.

If QUILL cannot write down that you locked the file, for example because its settings folder is on a full disk, the document is still locked for now and you hear why:

Document is read-only for now, but it could not be remembered for next time (There is not enough space on the disk).

An untitled document cannot be remembered either, because it has no file name yet. Save it first if you want the lock to stay.

A file Windows marks read-only, or one on a share you cannot write to, opens locked as well, and QUILL says so as it opens. Turn the guard off to type anyway, then save under a new name.

Long documents. Locking a document does not make it open faster: the text still has to be put into the editor, which for an eight-megabyte book takes about four tenths of a second on a fast computer. What the guard does speed up is moving around. Because a locked document cannot change, browse-mode keys (next heading, next paragraph, next link) read QUILL’s own copy of the text instead of copying the whole document out of the editor on every key. On that eight-megabyte book a heading jump went from about 46 milliseconds to well under one.

Tools for Reading, Review, and Inspection

These tools help when a file is hard to read, or when you need to know exactly what is in it. They answer questions like “which kind of space is that?”, “where does the bold end?” and “did this scan come through properly?”

Describe Character at Cursor

Some characters sound the same but are not: a curly “smart” quote and a straight apostrophe, a no-break space and an ordinary space, an invisible zero-width space that quietly breaks a search, or a stray byte-order mark pasted from the web. Tools > Advanced > Describe Character at Cursor (Ctrl+Shift+C) tells you exactly what sits under the cursor. It says the answer where you are and leaves it in the status bar, so you can read it again.

When you want to study a character, Character Details (Ctrl+Alt+C) opens the full description in a read-only window, the same kind F1 help uses, so your screen reader can read it all in one pass or line by line. It gives:

  • the character’s name (for example “No-break space” or “Right single quotation mark”),
  • its Unicode code point in both hexadecimal and decimal,
  • its category in plain words (uppercase letter, dash punctuation, control character, and so on), and
  • a note for the invisible characters that are easy to miss, plus a flag when the character is not ASCII.

If the cursor is at the very end of the document, it tells you so instead of guessing.

A page break is named as one. A PDF opens with a page break between each of its pages (a form feed character, which is what Go To’s Page list and the Page part of the status bar count). With the cursor on one, Describe Character says “Page break U+000C” and Character Details adds that one page ends there and the next begins. Before 2026-10-17 it said “Unnamed character”, which told you nothing. For example, open a three-page PDF, press Ctrl+G, choose Page, pick page 2, then press Left Arrow once and Ctrl+Shift+C: you hear “Page break”.

QUILL also never hands that character, or any other control character, to your screen reader inside an announcement. When a message quotes text that crosses a page, such as the first words of a selection, the page break is read as a pause rather than as a character name. The text in your document is not changed.

Reveal Codes (Alt+F3)

QUILL keeps formatting codes hidden, so your editing text stays clean and fast. Reveal Codes lets you see and hear every one of them when you ask. If you used WordPerfect, you will know it: this is the same idea, built for a screen reader.

Press Alt+F3 (or check View > Reveal Codes) to open a pane below the editor. It shows your document as an ordered stream of bracketed codes and text: [Bold On], [Font: Arial], [Size: 14], [Center], [Heading 2], [Link: ...], [Tab], [¶ Hard Return], [No-Break Space], [Smart Quote], and so on. The editor itself does not change. Reveal Codes is a window onto the hidden formatting, not a different way of editing.

Reveal Codes is hidden by default and costs nothing until you open it.

QUILLBee’s tip: Not sure whether a word is really bold, or where the bold stops? Press Alt+F3, arrow to it, and you will hear “bold on” right where it starts. Press Alt+F3 again to close the pane.

Moving between the panes

F6 moves through the regions (Editor, then Reveal Codes when it is shown, then the Status Bar), and Shift+F6 goes back, just like switching windows. The two carets stay in step however you move: arrow keys, word jumps, Home and End, Page Up and Page Down, a mouse click, or a jump from Find. Move in the editor and the pane follows to the same spot. Move in the pane and the editor’s cursor follows to the matching place. Sit in whichever you like and your place in the document comes with you.

Moving through the codes

In the default Flowed view the pane reads like your document with the hidden formatting showing, and the caret moves the way it does in the editor:

  • Left Arrow and Right Arrow move one character at a time, but step over a whole code in one go. One press moves you across [Bold On] and your screen reader just says “bold on”. It is never spelled out letter by letter.
  • Ctrl+Left and Ctrl+Right move by word, stopping at each code.
  • Up Arrow and Down Arrow move by line and read the new line, as in the editor.
  • Home and End go to the start and end of the line. Ctrl+Home and Ctrl+End go to the top and bottom of the document.
  • Land on a code and you hear it named as one thing: “bold on”, “tab”, “centred”, “font Arial”. An opening code also tells you how far its formatting reaches (“bold on, 12 characters”).

While you arrow through the pane, your screen reader does the talking. The region is named once when you arrive, and after that you hear the character, word, line, or code your screen reader reads. QUILL puts its fuller phrase (for example “italic on, 15 characters”) quietly in the status bar, where you can read it whenever you want it. Jumps you ask for (next or previous code, go to the matching pair) and mouse clicks are still spoken by QUILL.

If you would rather hear QUILL’s own phrase on every arrow press, turn on Reveal Codes: Speak Codes Aloud in the command palette. It is off by default.

In either view you can also jump code to code, skipping the plain text, and from an opening code to its matching close.

Editing a run in place (F2)

  1. Move onto text that sits between a pair of formatting codes, for example the words inside [Bold On] ... [Bold Off].
  2. Press F2. The pane limits you to just that stretch of text, and you can edit it freely.
  3. Press Enter to put your change back into the document, or Escape to cancel.

The codes around it are left exactly as they were. The whole stretch edits as one piece, so a run that contains a [Tab] or a code inside it (bold text with one italic word, say) comes along together. The inner codes appear as their markup and are put straight back when you press Enter. If you press F2 on plain text with no formatting around it, QUILL tells you there is nothing to edit there.

Views and how much it says

In Settings > Editing (or on the View menu) you can choose:

  • Flowed (the default): the interactive pane described above, with codes shown inside the running text ([Bold On]Hello[Bold Off]), movement by character, and F2 editing. It is the closest to the classic WordPerfect look and the most natural layout on a braille display.
  • Structured: one item per code in a list. It is clear and labelled, and good for scanning structure. Your screen reader reads each item as you arrow through it. Movement by character, word and line, and F2 editing, belong to the Flowed view only.
  • Verbosity: quiet (just the code name), balanced (adds how far an opening code reaches), or detailed (adds Unicode notes for invisible characters).

QUILL remembers your view and verbosity between sessions.

EPUB Navigator

When you open an EPUB, the navigator lets you move chapter by chapter instead of scrolling blindly through one long block of text.

Chapter headings are also placed in the text itself, so single-key heading navigation (H and Shift+H) walks every heading in the book, not just the chapter breaks. Many older or home-made EPUBs never used real headings at all. For those, QUILL works the headings out from the book’s layout (a paragraph styled as a title or chapter, or a short bold line on its own), so heading navigation and the chapter outline still work where they would otherwise be empty.

Equations are spoken, not skipped. When a chapter contains mathematics (MathML, or LaTeX written as a math span or between $$...$$ or \(...\) delimiters), QUILL turns each equation into plain words and places it in the text as “Math Equation: …” so you hear the formula as part of its sentence. It uses the same math speech as the editor: the MathCAT engine when it is installed, and QUILL’s built-in reader otherwise. A formula QUILL cannot understand is read as its plain source instead of being dropped, and a book with no math reads just as before.

OCR Image

OCR Image… (on Tools > Reading and Dictation) reads the text out of a picture, using optical character recognition on your own computer. Nothing happens behind your back: you choose the image, confirm, and hear progress as it works.

Two more commands on the same menu skip the file altogether:

  • OCR Clipboard Image reads the picture you have just copied, for example with the Print Screen key or Copy Image in a web browser. If there is no picture on the clipboard, QUILL tells you so.
  • OCR Screen Capture… takes a picture of the screen and reads it. QUILL asks whether you want The whole screen or The active window, with the active window already chosen.

Both show you the recognised text to review, just like OCR Image.

Document intake and extraction quality

When you bring in a scanned PDF or another hard-to-read file, the text QUILL pulls out may not be perfect. These commands help you check. Instead of assuming every import is right, QUILL helps you ask whether the extraction is good enough, what may have been lost, and whether you should quote from it directly.

Comparing documents

When you have two versions of something (a draft and the edited copy, or your file and the one that came back by email), comparing them tells you exactly what changed. Everything happens on your own computer. You can compare one file with another, review several open documents, move through the differences one at a time with both documents following along, and make a summary of what changed.

The Comparison menu

Tools > Comparison holds:

  • Compare with File…
  • Compare Open Documents
  • Next Difference / Previous Difference
  • Announce Current Difference
  • Difference List…
  • Toggle Synchronized Navigation
  • Toggle Ignore Whitespace
  • Compare Options…
  • Generate Accessible Report
  • Create Difference Summary
  • Copy Current Difference / Copy All Differences

Toggle Ignore Whitespace decides whether spaces count as a change. Each press moves one step and says where you are: exact (every space counts), ignore trailing (spaces at the ends of lines do not count), then ignore all (no difference in spacing counts), and back to exact. Use it when one copy went through a program that tidied the spacing and you only care about the words.

Generate Accessible Report writes every difference into a new Markdown document, under a heading that names the two files and says how many differences there are. Because it is an ordinary document, you can read it at your own pace, search it, and save or send it.

Moving through the differences

Once a comparison is open, these keys do most of the work:

  • Ctrl+Alt+Shift+. moves to the next difference.
  • Ctrl+Alt+Shift+, moves to the previous one.
  • Ctrl+Alt+Shift+D reads the current difference.

The compare dialog is a list you can review with your screen reader, one difference at a time, entirely from the keyboard.

Compare sounds

If you use a sound pack, compare mode plays short earcons: one when a comparison opens, one when it closes, separate ticks for moving to the next or previous difference, and a soft “blocked” tone when you reach the first or last difference and there is nothing further. You can turn each one on or off in Tools > Reading and Dictation > Sound Scheme… (Ctrl+Alt+Shift+O), under the Compare section. The topic on sound notifications and earcons has more about sound packs.

Making audio from your documents

QUILL can turn your writing into audio files: a single document, a whole folder, or a chaptered audiobook ready to publish. This section is for when you want something to keep, share or put on a phone. Live listening is Read Aloud, earlier in this chapter.

Audiobook and Batch Speech

Tools > Speech > Audiobook & Batch Speech… (Ctrl+Shift+Grave then Y) is the guided home for audio production. It turns a whole folder of documents into speech audio in one pass, and it builds chaptered audiobooks from documents, from a folder of recordings, or from both. It is a wizard that asks one thing at a time. It is listed in the command palette under “Audio Studio…”. It replaced the old single-page Batch Export to Speech Audio dialog; every option is still there, spread across clear steps, and your saved project settings still fill everything in.

What do you want to make

The first page asks what you want to make. It remembers which one you chose last time and selects it for you.

  • Narrate documents into an audiobook or speech audio. The steps are: What should I read? (folder, file types, filters); Who should read it? (engine, voice with a preview button, pace, round-robin voices, voice casting rules, translated editions); How should chapters work? (chapter mode, spoken headings, the transition sounder and its volume, pauses); Output and diagnostics (format, existing-file policy, loudness, incremental-rebuild reuse, dry run, temp folder); the book page (optional, to put everything together into one audiobook); and a summary in plain words.
  • Combine audio files into one chaptered audiobook. A shorter path: pick the folder of recordings, describe the book (title, author, narrator, cover, M4B or MP3, ACX loudness), review the summary, and Start. Each file becomes one chapter, and you always review the chapter list before the book is built. There is more on this under Build an audiobook from a folder of recordings, below.
  • Edit an existing audiobook. Pick a finished MP3 or M4B and it opens in the Chapter Workbench, described below. A file with no chapter markers opens as one chapter, ready to split up.

Moving through the wizard

Each step is announced as you arrive (“Step 2 of 7: What should I read?”). Back and Next move between steps. Skip to summary jumps ahead when your saved project profile already fills every page, so a repeat run is three keystrokes. The work runs in the background with progress for each file, and you can cancel it. It uses the same speech engine as live Read Aloud, so the voice you audition is the voice you get.

The source pages offer a list of recent source folders, and the edit path’s file picker remembers recently opened audiobooks. Both lists are kept between sessions, are separate from your recent documents, and never grow without limit.

What should I read

  • Source folder, and whether to include subfolders.
  • The file types to include: .docx, .md, .html/.htm, .txt.
  • Optional include/exclude filters: patterns separated by semicolons or commas, matched against the file name and its path.
  • Skip files larger than (MB): a maximum file size, in megabytes (0 means no limit). One accidental 400 MB export in a folder can no longer take over an overnight run. Any file over the limit is left out, and the live document count on the same step changes as you change the limit. Press Count documents to hear the settled count.

Who should read it

  • Engine, voice, and pace. Any offline engine (SAPI 5, DECtalk, Piper, Kokoro, eSpeak NG), with a Preview button to hear the voice first.

  • Round-robin voices read each article or heading in a different voice, cycling through a list you build:

    1. Pick a voice from the Round-robin voices combo box.
    2. Press Add voice.
    3. Repeat for each voice, and use Move Up, Move Down and Remove to set the order.

    Article 1 uses the first voice, article 2 the second, and so on, starting again at the top when the list runs out. The list uses voices from the engine you selected, so the sound quality stays consistent, and changing the engine clears it. Leave the list empty to use the single chosen voice.

  • Voice casting lets you say exactly who reads what, on top of the rotation. On the same page, add rules: a pattern plus the voice that should read every matching chapter. A pattern matches the heading title as a wildcard (Chapter *, *interview*, or an exact title, ignoring case) or a section number (#1 for the opener). The first rule that matches wins; chapters no rule matches go to the rotation or the single voice. Rules use voices of the selected engine (switching engines clears them) and are saved in job files. A dialogue-heavy drama or an interview show can cast its voices on purpose instead of by rotation order.

  • If a voice ever fails to speak (a broken system voice, a missing model, or a cloud voice the provider rejects), QUILL notes it and skips it on later runs, so one bad voice in a rotation cannot keep stopping your batches.

Your combine and round-robin choices are remembered for each project.

Translated editions

Also export in other languages (translated) makes a translated audiobook alongside the original.

  1. Choose a language.
  2. Pick a voice for it.
  3. Press Add language. Repeat for as many languages as you want. Remove language drops one.
  4. Choose Translate with: AI provider (any AI provider you have set up) or LibreTranslate (runs locally).

Voices are offered local ones first (eSpeak speaks nearly every language offline, plus any Windows voice installed for that language), then the premium multilingual cloud voices: OpenAI, Gemini, and ElevenLabs. Those need the matching provider API key, the same key the AI Voice feature uses. When you press Start, each document is translated and written as <doc> (<Language>).<ext> next to the original, with the same chapters, page-turn cue, and loudness. A pronunciation dictionary can be limited to one language, so it only affects that language’s narration. If a cloud voice you chose has no key set up, that language is skipped with a note and the run carries on.

You see the cost first for cloud runs. Cloud translation and cloud narration are paid by use, so if your choices would use a paid cloud service, QUILL shows a combined cost estimate and asks you to confirm before it starts. Runs that use only local engines and LibreTranslate start straight away.

How should chapters work

  • Chapters turn a long document into a chaptered file you can move around in. Choose MP3 (ID3 chapter markers) or M4B audiobook (native MP4 chapter atoms, the Apple and audiobook format). Apple Podcasts, Overcast, VLC, foobar2000, and others recognize both.
  • A transition sound plays between articles: a natural page-turn by default, or a different earcon from your active sound pack. Whichever you use is matched to the audio format automatically, so it always joins cleanly.
  • You choose whether each heading is spoken, and set pauses between articles and between sentences, plus a short silence at the end that stops clipping.
  • A very long section is split automatically at a safe sentence boundary, so no single passage runs past the engine’s time limit. The run report lists how many chapters each document got.
  • Chapter mode: keep the whole document as one chaptered file, or choose Separate file per article to write each section under a heading as its own NNN - <heading> audio file, in a folder for that document.
  • Combine empty headings: when a heading has no text of its own (a “Part” heading followed straight away by a “Chapter” heading), fold it into the next article that does have text and join their titles (“Part One: Chapter 1”), so you do not hear an empty chapter. Off by default. Turn it on with the Combine empty headings into the next article checkbox.

Output and diagnostics

  • Output format: WAV (always available) or, when ffmpeg is installed, MP3, M4A, M4B (audiobook), Opus, FLAC, or OGG. When ffmpeg is missing, compressed formats fall back to WAV with a note for each file, instead of failing. MP3 has a quality setting, and WAV can be made to one sample rate and channel count throughout.
  • Normalize loudness: check Normalize loudness to audiobook (ACX) level to bring every exported file to the same audiobook loudness, the standard the Build Audiobook tool uses. QUILL measures each file and re-levels it in two passes, without touching the chapters or their timing. Needs ffmpeg; without it, the file is saved as it is, with a note.
  • If a file already exists: Skip (a cheap way to resume), Overwrite, or Rename so both are kept (name (2).mp3).
  • Output layout: mirror the source folders or flatten everything into one folder. You can also rename outputs with a template such as {index:03d} - {stem}, which gives 001 - chapter-one.
  • Audiobook metadata: album, author or narrator, genre, and year are written into compressed files. Each file’s title comes from its heading or name, and its track number from its position.
  • Dry run: instead of speaking, write a <doc>.preview.txt for each document with the exact text that would be spoken (after cleanup, pronunciation fixes, and polish) and how many pronunciation substitutions were made. You can proofread what will be said before waiting on slow speech.
  • Audition: check Audition on the output step to convert only the first document. You can judge the voice, the pace, and the loudness in a couple of minutes before you commit to an overnight run.
  • Run report: optionally write a manifest.json and manifest.csv listing every file’s status, output path, length, and any error.

Incremental rebuilds

With Reuse unchanged audio from the last run checked (the default), QUILL takes a fingerprint of each document’s text together with every setting that shapes its audio. On the next run, documents that have not changed, and whose audio is still on disk, are reused instead of spoken again, and you hear “Reused … (unchanged since last run)”. Edit one chapter of a forty-document book and only that chapter is narrated again. Change the voice or any audio setting and everything rebuilds by itself. Uncheck the box to force a full rebuild. The fingerprints are kept in the source folder’s .quill/speech-cache.json; deleting that file also forces a fresh run.

The book page

  • Look up book details. Type the title (and the author, if you know it) and press Look up book details. QUILL searches Open Library and MusicBrainz, two free public catalogs, and the match you pick fills in the author, genre, and year. QUILL asks before the first contact, and only the title and author you typed are sent. If the match has a cover image on Open Library and you have not set one, QUILL offers to download it as cover.jpg next to your audio and fills the cover field. Say no and nothing is fetched.
  • Spoken credits. Check Add spoken opening and closing credits and QUILL narrates the usual audiobook frame (“My Book. Written by Jane Doe. Narrated by Sam Reader.”) in the run’s own voice, as the first and last chapters.
  • Polish during the build. The audio source page’s Trim silences from each recording and the book page’s fade-in / fade-out / tempo controls are applied while the audiobook is built. Each chapter file is prepared first (silence trimmed from the start and end, fades added, and the tempo changed without changing pitch) and then joined into the master. The prepared copies live in the build’s temporary folder, so your source recordings are never overwritten. If polishing one file fails, the build keeps going with that file as it was. The defaults (no trim, 0 ms fades, 1.0 tempo) leave the audio unchanged.

Settings that stay with the folder

A folder remembers its whole speech setup in <folder>/.quill/speech-project.json: engine, voice, output format, chapters, text cleanup, pronunciation dictionaries, metadata, and run policy. Set up a project once and run it again whenever you like. When you open the wizard on a folder you have set up before, those settings fill in by themselves, and your choices are saved when you press Start. Anything you change this run beats the folder’s saved settings, which beat your global defaults.

Job files go one step further. On the summary page, Save a job file writes a small .quilljob file that pins this exact run: folder, voices, chapters, mastering, and book details. You can edit it by hand. Load a job file on the first page brings everything back, filled in. Keep one per project, or edit it in Notepad between runs.

Running it without you

  • Library mode (a checkbox on the combine-audio path) builds every subfolder as its own audiobook in one unattended run. Point it at a folder of book folders and come back to a shelf of finished masters, each titled after its folder.
  • The watch action “Build audiobook from the folder” keeps a drop folder working for you. Whenever new recordings land in a watched folder, its chaptered master is rebuilt automatically. A batch of files arriving together causes one rebuild, not many.

Export to Translated Speech Audio (one document)

Tools > Speech > Export to Translated Speech Audio is the one-document version of the translated export above. It translates and narrates just the document you are editing, with no folder involved.

  1. Pick the output format.
  2. Choose Translate with: any AI provider you have set up, or local LibreTranslate.
  3. Add one or more language + voice targets, the same way as in the wizard. Local voices are listed first, then the premium cloud voices.
  4. Press Export.

QUILL writes <doc> (<Language>).<ext> next to your document. If the document has unsaved changes, QUILL offers to save first, so your latest edits are the ones translated. As with the wizard, a combined cost estimate appears before any paid cloud run.

The dialog also has an “Open in Audio Studio” button. It closes the dialog and opens the Audiobook and Batch Speech wizard with your document already filled in, for when you want chapters and the rest.

Build an audiobook from a folder of recordings

The wizard’s Combine audio files path (Tools > Speech > Audiobook & Batch Speech…, the second choice on the first page) joins a folder of existing audio files into one chaptered audiobook, the kind your audiobook or podcast app moves through track by track. Where the narrate path turns documents into speech, this one stitches a folder of audio (your own recordings, or speech QUILL exported earlier) into a single master.

  • Point it at a folder of audio files. Each file becomes one chapter, in natural sort order, titled from the file name (a leading track number such as 01 - is removed).
  • Edit the chapters before building. The dialog lists the chapters, and works fully from the keyboard. You can rename the selected one (type a new title and press Rename or Enter), move it up or down, merge it into the chapter above (the two files then play as one chapter with one marker), or remove it from the book. Restore original undoes every edit in one press.
  • Bring your titles with you. Import titles… reads chapter names, in order, from an Audacity label track, a CUE sheet, plain timestamp lines, Podcasting 2.0 chapters JSON, or a CSV spreadsheet. Any sheet with a “Title” column works, and so does a simple number,title list, so a chapter list you keep in Excel can name your MP3 files in one step. Export titles… writes the current list back out as text.
  • Honest numbers, before and after. The run log starts with a pre-flight check. It names files whose sample rate, channels, or format differ (the build still works; it re-encodes them) and gives an estimated length and size. After the build, QUILL re-reads the finished book and tells you what a player will actually see: “verified 24 chapters”, or exactly what went wrong. A plain-text chapter report and a Podcasting 2.0 ...chapters.json file are written next to the book.
  • Choose M4B audiobook (native MP4 chapter atoms, the Apple and audiobook format) or MP3 (ID3 chapter markers). You can move by chapter in both in Apple Podcasts, Overcast, VLC, foobar2000, and others.
  • Check Normalize loudness to ACX (Audible/audiobook) spec to bring the master into the loudness range audiobook platforms require (RMS between -23 and -18 dB, peak under -3 dB). After the build, QUILL measures the result and says in the status bar whether it met the ACX range.
  • Fill in the book’s title, author, narrator, genre, and year. A cover image in the folder (named like cover.jpg, folder.png, and so on) is picked up automatically, or you can browse for one.
  • The build runs in the background, and the status bar tells you when it is done.

The Chapter Workbench

The Workbench is where you fix a finished audiobook by ear. Reach it from the wizard’s Edit an existing audiobook path, or, for any recording at all, from Tools > Speech > Find Chapters in a Recording… (QUILL Key + Shift+O).

Automatic chapters for a lecture, a meeting or any MP3

A two-hour lecture with no chapters is hard to move around by ear: there is nothing to skip to. Find Chapters in a Recording… fixes that. It asks for a recording (MP3, M4A or M4B), opens the Workbench on it, and starts Propose chapters from silences straight away.

  1. Choose the recording and press Open.
  2. The Propose chapters from silences window asks two things: how quiet counts as silence (in decibels; -30 suits most speech, -40 a noisy room) and how long a pause must last (in seconds; 0.8 catches a speaker drawing breath between topics, 2 or 3 only the real breaks). OK starts the scan.
  3. The scan runs in the background, and you can keep listening while it does. Each new chapter starts in the middle of a silence and is titled Proposed chapter 1, Proposed chapter 2 and so on, so you can always tell a guess from a name somebody chose. You hear how many were proposed.
  4. Arrow through the list. Enter plays a chapter from its start; Hear boundary plays the moments around where it starts; Set start to playhead, Split at playhead, Nudge back and Nudge forward move a boundary until it sounds right; Merge into previous removes one that should not be there. Rename each one you keep (type the title and press Enter).
  5. Save writes the chapters: an MP3 in place (only its tags change; the audio is untouched), an M4B as a new file. Export chapters… writes them as a CUE sheet, timestamps, Audacity labels, Podcasting 2.0 JSON or a spreadsheet instead. Reopen the file and the chapters are there.

Some things to know:

  • Your own chapters are never silently replaced. If the recording already has two or more chapters, QUILL asks first. Keep mine, add proposals (the default) keeps every chapter you had, with its title and start, and adds a proposed chapter only where a silence is at least ten seconds from one of yours. Replace with the proposal swaps the whole list. Cancel changes nothing. A recording with no chapter marks of its own is not asked, because there is nothing of yours to lose.
  • Stopping. While the scan runs the button reads Stop proposing chapters. Press it again, or close the Workbench, and the scan’s answer is thrown away and the list stays exactly as it was. (The scan program finishes its pass in the background and nothing is written.)
  • Restore original takes back the whole proposal at once, whichever way you answered.
  • Long recordings. The scan is given time in proportion to the recording: at least ten minutes, and a tenth of the recording’s length for anything over a hundred minutes, so a whole day’s recording is scanned to the end.
  • It needs ffmpeg. Without it you are told so, and the button comes back.
  • Silence is a guess about structure, not knowledge of it. A speaker who pauses mid-sentence makes a boundary that is not a topic change; that is why every proposal is labelled and nothing is saved until you press Save.

The chapter list and the player

The chapter list reads each row in full (“3. The Long Road, starts 1:02:03, runs 12:40”). Press Enter on a chapter to hear it.

The built-in player has Play/Pause, Stop, Previous and Next chapter, Rewind and Forward, a position slider that speaks minutes and seconds, and a volume slider. The current chapter is announced as playback moves into it.

  • It remembers where you stopped in each book and picks up there next time.
  • It has a playback speed control, from 0.75x to 2x, with the pitch kept natural.
  • It answers “Where am I?” with the whole picture: “Chapter 4 of 24: The Long Road. 3:12 into the chapter, 9:28 left in it. 1:02:03 of 7:41:00 in the book, 6:38:57 remaining.”

Playback normally uses Windows’ built-in media engine, with nothing to install. For gapless audio, exact seeking, and instant chapter jumps even in 8-hour books, download the mpv player engine from Help > Download Optional Components (about 44 MB, checksum-verified; its licenses and a source offer come inside the download). The player switches to libmpv the next time it opens. If you prefer, you can instead put a libmpv-2.dll into the data folder’s engine-packs\mpv folder, or set the QUILL_LIBMPV environment variable to point at one. If the DLL is missing or broken, QUILL quietly stays on the built-in engine.

Fixing the chapters

  • Split at playhead: listen for the spot where a chapter should begin, pause there, and press the button. The chapter is cut in two exactly at that point. This is how you fix a book whose chapters landed in the wrong places.
  • Set start to playhead: move an existing boundary. Select the chapter, park the playhead where it should start, and press. The previous chapter’s end moves with it, so there are never gaps.
  • Merge into previous, Rename (type and press Enter), and Restore original (one press undoes every edit).
  • Add chapter… puts a marker at the playhead, or at a time you type, and asks what to call it. Past the end of the last chapter it adds a new one at the end instead of refusing.
  • Delete chapter removes a marker only. The audio joins its neighbour, and unlike Merge it works on the first and last chapters too.
  • Edit chapter… opens a small window for the title and the exact start and end (and a chapter link and image, if you publish with Podcasting 2.0).
  • Preview chapter plays just that chapter and stops at its end.

Nudging a boundary until it sounds right

Nudge back and Nudge forward move the selected chapter’s start by one step. From the chapter list, Alt+Left and Alt+Right do the same, and adding Shift moves ten steps at once.

  • The Step box goes from 100 milliseconds up to 10 seconds, and remembers your choice for next time (audio_studio_chapter_nudge_ms, half a second to start with).
  • Hear boundary plays three seconds before the marker and two after it, then stops. It is the quickest way to tell whether a boundary falls in the middle of a sentence.
  • Check Hear after each nudge to have that happen automatically after every press. It is off by default, so you only get sound on every keypress if you ask for it.
  • Each nudge speaks only the new time (“0:00:09.500”), so it keeps up when you hold the key down. The full sentence follows about half a second after you stop.
  • Push a marker into its neighbour and it stops there and says “Cannot move further” once.

Finding chapters for you

  • Propose chapters from silences… listens for silences in the recording, using ffmpeg’s silencedetect. A small dialog asks for the noise threshold (dB, default -30) and the minimum silence length (seconds, default 0.8), and QUILL suggests chapter boundaries in the middle of each silence. The suggestions land in the chapter list for you to review; nothing is applied without you. Press Restore original to undo. So one long recording is no problem: open a single 80-minute file (it opens as one chapter), press one button, review, and save.
  • Propose AI titles… names a folder of track07-style chapters by listening to them. The first minute of each chapter is transcribed on this computer by the local speech model, so the audio never leaves your computer. Only that text goes to the AI you have set up (a local model keeps even the text on your computer). A short suggested title for each chapter lands in the list for you to review, and Restore original undoes the lot. Needs AI set up; not available in Safe Mode.

Checking, importing and splitting

  • Check against ACX measures the file with ffmpeg’s loudnorm and shows a result with integrated loudness, true peak, and noise floor, plus a plain suggestion for each thing that fails (“Integrated loudness is -14.2 LUFS, target -20 plus or minus 3; make the recording 5.8 dB quieter”). The result is announced when the measurement finishes, however you close the dialog.
  • Import chapters… replaces the whole list from Audacity labels, a CUE sheet, timestamp lines, Podcasting 2.0 JSON, or CSV. Export chapters… writes the list in any of the same five formats.
  • Split into files… goes the other way: one audio file per chapter, named NN - Title, in a folder you pick. That gives you podcast episodes straight away, or tracks for players without chapter support.

Book details and tags

Book details (title, author, narrator, genre, year) are edited right in the Workbench. All tags… opens the full Tag Editor for everything else: twenty-six fields over five pages (main, details, publishing, sort order and cover art). They include track and disc numbers, composer, publisher, copyright, BPM, ISRC, language, grouping, the compilation flag, the four sort-order fields, and cover art you can load, save out or remove. Ctrl+Tab moves between pages. The five quick fields and the editor always agree with each other, in both directions.

You can also open the Tag Editor on its own, without the Workbench: check Edit the tags only on the Studio wizard’s Open a book page.

The same editor is in podHarvest. A file edited in one reads back exactly as you left it in the other, with the same fields, operations and keys. If you use both, you only learn it once.

Saving

Save writes an MP3’s edits in place: only the tags are rewritten and the audio is left alone. An M4B depends on what you changed. Tag edits alone also save in place and finish at once. A chapter change needs Save As, which copies the audio into a new file without re-encoding, so there is no loss of quality. Long saves run in the background, so QUILL stays responsive.

Publishing

Publish… offers ways to get the finished book out into the world. Each one is something you choose to do:

  • Podcast feed: QUILL writes a complete .rss file next to the book (RSS 2.0 with iTunes and Podcasting 2.0 tags; chapter navigation uses the chapters.json file beside it). Upload the feed and audio wherever you like. Making the feed never touches the network.
  • Folder feed (all episodes): run a whole show from one folder. Every MP3, M4B, or M4A master becomes an episode (the oldest file is episode 1), with its title from its tags or your own, your description for each episode (edited in an accessible episode list), its real publication date, length, and chapter link. The show settings are kept in the folder, so after each new build, one press of Write feed.rss now makes the complete feed again. Write show notes page makes an accessible show-notes.html beside it, with a heading for each episode, the descriptions, and chapter lists, ready to upload with the feed.
  • SFTP upload: save a destination (host, folder, and the public web address that matches it), and QUILL uploads the book and its companion files over SSH, with the same strict host-key checking as editing over SSH. The password is kept in the Windows Credential Manager, never in QUILL’s settings.
  • Auphonic: send the book to your own Auphonic account for professional post-production. QUILL uploads it, waits for the production to finish, and downloads the results into a folder next to the book. Your API token is also kept in the credential vault, and you can manage it on the Services page of AI > AI Hub…, with QUILL’s other service keys. (AI Hub is on the AI menu once AI > Show advanced AI features is checked.) Press Check account and load presets first: QUILL tells you whose account the token belongs to and how many processing credits remain, and fills the preset picker with your saved Auphonic presets. The confirmation then names the preset and your balance before anything uploads. QUILL asks before anything is uploaded.

Publishing is not available in Safe Mode. Every upload runs in the background and tells you when it is done. SFTP uploads and Auphonic transfers show a progress dialog that speaks whole-percent steps as the file moves (“Uploading book.m4b: 42%”) and has a real Cancel button. What cancelling does depends on when you press it, and QUILL tells you which happened:

  • Cancelling an SFTP upload stops in the middle of the file. Files that already finished stay on the server.
  • Cancelling Auphonic before the upload finishes means no production starts.
  • Cancelling Auphonic after that leaves the production running in your Auphonic account and skips the download.

The Universal Audio Converter

Apart from the audiobook wizard, QUILL has a general audio converter. It changes audio files from one format to another, and pulls the sound out of video files, entirely on your computer using the bundled ffmpeg. It works in Safe Mode and never uploads anything. There are three ways in:

  • Voices > Convert Audio…: the dialog, from the audiobook and batch speech wizard.
  • quill convert: a command for scripts, for example quill convert *.wav --to mp3 --preset podcast --out ./done. Add --dry-run to see the plan first, --list-presets or --list-formats to see what is available, and any of the Advanced options (--bitrate, --sample-rate, --channels, --loudness, --gain, --high-pass, --trim-silence, --speed, --compress, --level, --fade-in, --fade-out).
  • Convert from URL…: paste a link (YouTube, and the many sites yt-dlp supports) and QUILL downloads the best audio and hands it to the converter. This is the only part that uses the internet. yt-dlp is not bundled, so the first time you use it QUILL asks once, shows a rights notice (“only download content you have the right to use”), and installs the small yt-dlp component. It is not available in Safe Mode.

Building a batch

  1. Add single files or whole folders. A folder is searched, and its layout of subfolders is copied into the output folder.
  2. Choose the output format. Only formats your ffmpeg can actually make are offered, so a conversion never fails halfway for lack of an encoder.
  3. Pick a preset: Just convert (change the container only), MP3 128/192/320, Podcast, Audiobook, Voice memo, Web Opus, Archival FLAC, or Hearing-aid mono.
  4. Choose an output folder, or leave it to make a Converted folder beside the source.
  5. Choose what happens when a file already exists: auto-number (never overwrites an original), skip, or overwrite.

The batch runs in the background on several files at once, so the window stays responsive. Progress shows in the status bar and, when QUILL is minimized, in the tray tooltip. The final summary is spoken and always names any files that failed, so a problem is never hidden behind “done”.

Advanced options

Check Advanced options to show more controls on top of the preset:

  • exact bit rate, sample rate, channels (keep, mono or stereo), and bit depth
  • loudness normalized to an audiobook (-20 LUFS, ACX) or podcast (-16 LUFS) target
  • gain in decibels
  • a high-pass filter to remove low rumble
  • trim silence from the ends
  • speed, a tempo change from 0.5x to 2x with no change in pitch
  • a compressor and a volume leveler
  • fade in and fade out, in seconds

Each control leaves the preset alone until you change it, so Advanced only overrides what you set on purpose. The effects are the same ones QUILL uses elsewhere, so the results match the rest of the app.

Media

QUILL also plays podcasts, recorded programmes and audiobook chapters. This part is about keeping your place in all of it, even across computers, and about letting it stop by itself when you fall asleep.

Continue Listening

Continue Listening… (in the Command Palette) is one list of everything you started and did not finish, newest first, with the kind named on every row: a podcast episode you are twenty minutes into, a recorded programme or LibriVox chapter you were halfway through. It answers the question “what was I in the middle of?” in one place.

  • Resume starts the highlighted row where you left off.
  • Forget This One drops the saved place and removes the row. The episode stays unplayed, because “I am not going back to this” and “I finished it” are different things. Clearing what you have given up on keeps the list worth opening.

Where you were in each podcast

The Podcasts window remembers, for each podcast, which episode you last had selected, and puts you back on it the next time you choose that podcast – after closing the window and after restarting QUILL. It knows the episode by who it is (its feed’s own episode ID, with its address and date as fall-backs), not by its row number, so it still finds the right one after new episodes arrive at the top, after you change the episode sort, and after a refresh.

If the episode has gone – the feed dropped it, or a filter hides it – you land on the remaining episode published closest to it (the newer of two equally close), so you are still in the same stretch of the back catalogue rather than back at the top. A podcast you have never opened starts at the first row, as before.

For example: you are working through a history podcast from its first episode and stop on episode 41. Next week four new episodes have arrived. Choose the podcast and the cursor is on episode 41, not on the newest one.

QUILL Cast does the same for a podcast opened from Favorites with Enter. The places that mix podcasts (New Episodes, the Inbox, the Play Queue) do not move a podcast’s remembered place. Each app keeps its own memory, in podcast_episode_cursor.json in its data folder; deleting the file only forgets the places.

Carry My Place Between Machines

If you listen on more than one computer, Carry My Place Between Machines… makes your place follow you. It needs two things: a folder both computers can see, and a recovery phrase.

The folder is yours. Point it at somewhere inside OneDrive, Dropbox, iCloud Drive, a network share, or even a memory stick you carry between the two. QUILL runs no server of its own and never holds your listening history. Everything is encrypted before it reaches the folder, so whoever runs that folder learns nothing but file sizes and times.

The recovery phrase is eight ordinary words, made up for you, something like earth dock hollow iris farm laser daisy amber. Write it down. It is the key, and QUILL cannot get it back for you if you lose it. Read My Phrase Aloud says it back numbered, one word at a time, which is just what you want while writing it on paper.

To set up the second computer, use the same window:

  1. Open Carry My Place Between Machines… on the second computer.
  2. Point it at the same folder.
  3. Type the phrase from the first computer.

QUILL notices the folder is already set up and says so. If the phrase is wrong it tells you at once (“that recovery phrase does not match this folder; nothing was changed”) instead of half-syncing and failing later. Typing is forgiving about capitals, spacing and punctuation, and a word that is not on the word list is named, so you only need to check that one.

Sync Now runs it and tells you in a sentence what happened: what came back, what went out, and whether two computers disagreed about a place (the most recent wins, and you are told). It can also run when playback stops, if you like. If both computers remembered a different place in the same thing and they are more than a few minutes apart, QUILL treats that as a real disagreement and tells you, instead of quietly picking one.

Everything works normally with no sync at all. Nothing ever waits on the folder before playing.

Sharing your place with apps that are not QUILL

The sync above carries your place between two copies of QUILL. It is encrypted from end to end, which is right, and which is also why no other app can read it. If you listen on a phone with a podcast app that is not ours, it does not help you.

Listening Places is the other half, and it is a separate checkbox in the same window. In the folder you already chose, QUILL also writes a small, plain file in a published format that any podcast app is free to read and write. There is no account, no server, and no signup. The format is written down publicly, so another app can simply support it.

What that means for you:

  • Turn on only the half you want. The encrypted QUILL-to-QUILL sync and the shared plain file are separate switches, because they reveal different things. You can use either, both, or neither.
  • No recovery phrase is needed for this half. The shared file cannot be encrypted and still be readable by another app, so setting it up is one checkbox.
  • Titles are left out unless you say so. Every show and episode is identified by a scrambled fingerprint instead of its name, so a folder you share with a family member does not become a list of what you listen to. A third checkbox, off by default, adds readable titles if you want them.
  • Each device writes its own file. That stops a cloud drive ever making a “positions (Jeff’s conflicted copy)”, and it means a third and fourth device work with no extra setup.
  • The most recent place wins, not the furthest. If you jumped back twenty minutes on purpose and then opened the episode somewhere else, that jump is kept.
  • It reads at launch and when you press Sync Now, and at no other time. So a position from another device never moves the playhead under you in the middle of an episode.

Sync Now covers both halves in one press and reports on each.

Sleep Timer

The sleep timer stops whatever is playing after a while, from one place. In this release radio and podcasts live in their own apps, so you set it in Quill Radio with Sleep Timer… on its Playback menu (Ctrl+Shift+Z), or in Quill Cast. The editor’s own Tools menu shows a Media submenu with the same Sleep Timer only in developer builds, where radio and podcasts are still part of the editor.

  1. Choose a preset (15, 30, 45, 60, or 90 minutes), or type your own number of minutes.
  2. Press Start.

Over the last 20 seconds, playback fades gently down and then stops. Your volume is then set back to what it was before the fade, so pressing play later is not suddenly quiet. Open the dialog again while a timer is running to hear how much time is left, or press Cancel Sleep Timer (also in the Command Palette as Media: Cancel Sleep Timer) to stop the countdown early.

What you learned, and where to go next

You can now have QUILL read to you in the voice and language you like, and teach it how to say the words it gets wrong. You can inspect a character, hear the hidden formatting with Reveal Codes (Alt+F3), read an EPUB by chapter, and step through the differences between two documents. You know where QUILL makes audiobooks, fixes their chapters and publishes them, and how to keep your place in what you listen to.

To practise with QUILL beside you, open Help > Tutorials… (Ctrl+Alt+F1) and try “Have it read to you”, “See the formatting that is normally hidden” and “Inspect a document you did not write”. On the website, Rescue a scanned PDF and From document to published audiobook walk through the longer jobs.
