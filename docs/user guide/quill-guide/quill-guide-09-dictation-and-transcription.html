Chapter 9: Dictation and transcription

This chapter is about turning speech into text. You will learn to talk straight into a document, to turn a recording into a transcript, and to have QUILL make meeting minutes or notes out of that transcript for you. Most of it runs entirely on your own computer, with no account and nothing uploaded. If you would rather speak than type, or you have a pile of recordings to get through, this chapter is for you. The first section is a map of QUILL’s speech engines, to help you choose one. The parts about speech models, optional downloads and memory come last, for when you want to tune things.

Which speech engine? A guide to choosing

QUILL can turn your voice into text in several ways, and you can choose the speech engine that does the listening. This section is the map. It tells you which setting matters for what you are doing, what each engine costs you in privacy, speed, accuracy and memory, how to switch, and where the full details are. You do not need to read it before you start: everything works the moment QUILL is installed.

The short answer

  • You do not have to choose anything. Live Dictation (Ctrl+F11) starts with Moonshine, which comes with QUILL. It is fast, puts in punctuation for you, and keeps everything on your computer. The offline tools (Locked Dictation, Ctrl+F9, and the others below) start on Whisper.cpp, whose small engine (about 8 MB) QUILL offers to fetch the first time you need it. If you would rather download nothing at all, choose QUILL speech models as their engine: it includes the same built-in Moonshine.
  • If you want fewer mistakes and your speech to stay on your computer, download a larger speech model. It takes a few minutes, and the worked example below walks you through it.
  • If you want the very best accuracy and you are happy for your speech to go to a company you already pay, choose OpenAI or a speech provider with your own account. Nothing is ever sent until you choose one and say Yes.

Two speech systems, one shared set of models

QUILL has two sets of speech tools. Each has its own engine setting, and changing one never changes the other.

  1. Live Dictation and its family. Live Dictation (Ctrl+F11), live transcripts, talking to the AI, and Transcribe a Recording… (Shift+F5). This is the same dictation QUILL Lite, QUILL Transcribe and Quill Inkwell have, on the same keys. You choose its engine in the Speech engine list in Dictation Settings (Alt+Shift+F6).
  2. The offline speech tools, which only QUILL has. Locked Dictation (Ctrl+F9), Dictate (Offline), Transcribe Audio or Video (Offline)…, Generate Captions (Offline)… and watch folders. You choose their engine in Manage Speech Models (Tools > Speech > Speech and Dictation…), or in the guided setup: Help > Download Optional Components…, row Dictation (offline speech).

To switch: press Alt+Shift+F6, move to Speech engine, arrow to the engine you want, and press Enter to save. Models you downloaded are listed with “(downloaded)” after the name. The next time you press Ctrl+F11, the new engine listens. Test Microphone, in the same window, records four seconds and tells you what the engine heard.

The tiers tell you how hard an engine works:

  • Lightweight: keeps up comfortably even on a modest computer. The built-in Moonshine and Whisper, Moonshine base, Whisper base.en, and both Parakeet models (Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 and Parakeet Unified 0.6B).
  • Balanced: more work than the built-in engines; keeps up on a computer with four or more cores. Nemotron and Whisper base.
  • High accuracy: the fewest mistakes, for the most work; needs a capable computer, or patience. Whisper small and larger.

The tiers come from our tests with real recordings (see “How we chose: tested on real speech”). Every downloaded model runs on the processor. A graphics card makes no difference to Live Dictation. The full list of models, their sizes and their measured speed is in “Better accuracy: optional speech models”.

The offline speech tools’ engines

Locked Dictation, Dictate (Offline), Transcribe Audio or Video (Offline), captions and watch folders use one of three engines. The guided setup on Help > Download Optional Components… offers them in this order:

  1. Whisper.cpp, the default: “light and fast, works on any computer”. The engine itself is about 8 MB and is not in the installer; QUILL offers to fetch it from QUILL’s own verified release the first time you need it. Then you choose a model in Manage Speech Models: Tiny (75 MB), Base (145 MB), Small (465 MB, the recommended starting point), Small English with speaker detection (465 MB), Medium (about 1.5 GB) or Large (v3) (about 3.1 GB). Choose Whisper.cpp when you are not sure, and whenever you want a transcript that marks Speaker 1, Speaker 2 and so on: only its speaker-detection model does that.
  2. QUILL speech models (Parakeet, Nemotron and more): the very models Live Dictation, QUILL Lite and QUILL Transcribe use, from the same folder. Choose this when you already downloaded Parakeet or Nemotron for Live Dictation, or when you want Parakeet’s great strength: it never invents words from silence (see “Parakeet 3 and silence”). It runs on the processor.
  3. Faster Whisper: “most accurate; larger and can use your graphics card”. It is the only engine in QUILL that uses a graphics card. It installs when you choose it, in a copy of QUILL that can install it; where it cannot, it is not offered. It has its own models (Tiny to Large v3, and an English Distil-Large v3). Choose it for long recordings on a capable computer, especially one with a graphics card. It does not label speakers.

Which engine is used when you have not chosen. Your own choice always wins. Otherwise, an installed Parakeet model means the QUILL speech models engine; then Whisper.cpp, once it has a model; and otherwise the built-in Moonshine through the QUILL speech models engine. That last step is why Locked Dictation needs no model: once the small Whisper.cpp engine is here, the built-in Moonshine transcribes until Whisper.cpp has a model of its own. Dictate (Offline), Transcribe Audio or Video (Offline) and captions do not fall back like that: they use the engine you chose and ask you for one of its models if it has none. With QUILL speech models chosen, every one of them works with nothing downloaded, because the built-in Moonshine and Whisper count as its models. Whisper.cpp stays the default for now.

If you used Nemotron or Parakeet in an earlier QUILL. Locked Dictation used to have its own Nemotron and Parakeet engines, with their own copy of the models. They are now one engine, QUILL speech models, and your choice moved with it; a Parakeet you had was moved, not downloaded again. QUILL tells you once, a moment after it starts, and never over an open dialog. You hear: “Locked Dictation’s Nemotron and Parakeet engines are now one engine, QUILL speech models…” If the older Nemotron model’s files are still on your computer, nothing uses them any more, so the same message asks a question instead: “Delete the older files to free 650 MB? No keeps them.” No is the default, so pressing Enter keeps them. Choose Yes and QUILL deletes only those old folders and tells you how much space it actually freed, for example “Freed 650 MB.” Either way you are not asked again. To dictate with Nemotron after that, download Nemotron 3.5 in Manage Speech Models.

To switch: open Tools > Speech > Speech and Dictation…. On the Dictation (Offline) tab, choose the Speech Engine, choose a model, and press Set as Default. Or use the guided setup, which takes you through engine, model and a test one step at a time; see “Setting up offline dictation”. The details are in “Manage Speech Models” and “Choosing a speech engine”, later in this chapter.

Privacy at a glance

  • Nothing leaves your computer with Moonshine, Whisper, any model you downloaded, Windows speech recognition, Whisper.cpp, the QUILL speech models or Faster Whisper. The only thing that comes over the internet is a model or engine file, once, when you ask for it and answer a question about it. Every file is checked against a fingerprint, and a damaged one is never used.
  • Your speech is sent to a company only with OpenAI or a speech provider, and only after you choose one and answer Yes.
  • QUILL’s free AI never carries your voice. It is not used for dictation at all.
  • AI > Transcribe Audio > Transcribe Audio File… and the cloud providers on the Dictation (Online) tab are separate: they upload a recording only when you ask them to transcribe it. See “Transcribe Audio File” and “Cloud transcription providers”.

Cloud engines: OpenAI and the twelve speech providers

You may already pay for a speech service. Live Dictation can use OpenAI, with your own OpenAI key, or one of twelve speech providers, with your own account: Microsoft Azure AI Speech, Google Cloud Speech-to-Text, Amazon Transcribe, Deepgram, AssemblyAI, Speechmatics, ElevenLabs Scribe, Gladia, Rev.ai, Groq, Mistral Voxtral and IBM Watson Speech to Text.

  • Your own account. The company bills you, not QUILL. QUILL shows no prices and counts nothing, so check the company’s own usage page.
  • What is sent. Each phrase dictation hears, over an encrypted connection, and the words come back. Silence is never sent. Names from My Words and Phrases go with it as words to expect, where the company supports that. Nothing passes through QUILL’s servers, and QUILL keeps no copy of your voice.
  • Your agreement. Choosing one in the Speech engine list asks a question that says exactly what is sent and to whom. No is the default: if you just press Enter, nothing is sent.
  • Your keys. An OpenAI key is saved in Use My Own AI Key (Alt+F2). A provider’s details are saved in Speech Providers… (Alt+V) in Dictation Settings, where Test Connection (Alt+T) checks your account without sending speech. Keys go straight to Windows Credential Manager and are never written in QUILL’s settings or logs.
  • Safe Mode turns every cloud engine off.
  • If the connection drops, that phrase is lost, you hear one sentence, and dictation keeps listening. QUILL never switches to another engine by itself.

Setting them up is explained step by step in “OpenAI dictation, with your own key” and “Speech providers: dictation with your own account at another company”.

Worked example: fewer mistakes on an 8 GB laptop

Sam has a laptop with four processor cores and 8 GB of memory. Moonshine is quick, but it keeps getting colleagues’ names and fast sentences wrong. Sam wants fewer mistakes, and wants the speech to stay on the laptop.

  1. Press Alt+Shift+F6 to open Dictation Settings. The read-only Recommended for this computer line already names a model and says why.
  2. Press Better Accuracy: Speech Models… (Alt+B).
  3. Press Alt+W to reach What matters most, and choose The fewest mistakes.
  4. Press Alt+C to read the Recommendation box. On this laptop it names NVIDIA Parakeet Unified 0.6B, and says whether it keeps up here, about how soon words appear after a pause, that the speech stays on this computer, and that the download is 663 MB. In our tests with real recordings it got 3.2 percent of words wrong, the fewest of any model that keeps up on one processor thread.
  5. Press Show Recommended Model (Alt+S) to move to it in the list, and Details (Alt+T) to read about it.
  6. Press Download… (Alt+D). A question names where it comes from, its size, its licence and the folder it goes in. Answer Yes. You hear “Downloading”, then every quarter of the way. Cancel Download (Alt+L) stops it, and pressing Download again later carries on from where it stopped.
  7. When it says the model is downloaded, press Use for Dictation (Alt+U), or Switch to Recommended Model (Alt+H), then OK in Dictation Settings. If Sam had downloaded another model before, a question offers to delete it to free its space. No is the default.
  8. Press Ctrl+F11 and talk. Parakeet Unified writes each phrase when you pause.

Sam would like Locked Dictation to use it too. In Tools > Speech > Speech and Dictation…, Sam chooses QUILL speech models as the speech engine, chooses Parakeet Unified, and presses Set as Default. Nothing downloads again.

If Sam would rather see the words while speaking, NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 ASR Streaming 0.6B does that, at the price of more mistakes (5.3 percent of words wrong where Parakeet Unified got 3.2). The advice depends on the computer, for example:

Computer A balance The quickest response The fewest mistakes
4 GB, 2 cores Moonshine Moonshine Moonshine base
8 GB, 2 cores Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 Moonshine Parakeet Unified, on two threads
8 GB, 4 cores Parakeet Unified Moonshine Parakeet Unified
16 GB, 8 cores Parakeet Unified Moonshine Parakeet Unified
4 GB, 2 cores, dictating in Spanish Whisper Whisper Whisper
8 GB, 4 cores, dictating in Spanish Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 Whisper Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3

When little memory is free at the moment you ask, the advice names a lighter model on any computer, usually the built-in Moonshine (English) or Whisper (Spanish).

The recommendation is only advice. It never changes your engine, it never suggests a cloud engine, and its figures come from our tests with real recordings, on one computer; yours may differ. Your own voice is the real test: use a model for a day, and keep the one that needs fewer fixes (see “How to tell which is more accurate for you”).

What is not available yet

  • Speech providers are for Live Dictation only. They are not offered in Transcribe a Recording, Transcribe Audio or Video (Offline), captions or watch folders. OpenAI, with your own key, is offered in Transcribe a Recording.
  • Google, Groq and Mistral show no words while you speak. Each phrase is written when you pause.
  • Spanish commands. You can dictate Spanish text now, but commands such as “scratch that” stay in English for now. See “Dictating in Spanish”.
  • A graphics card helps only Faster Whisper. Every other engine runs on the processor.
  • A few further speech features, such as a small listener just for the command word, are built but not switched on yet.

Dictating into a document

QUILL gives you three ways to dictate. Live Dictation writes each phrase as you pause, which suits most people most of the time. Locked Dictation records a whole passage and writes it when you finish. Dictate (Offline) is the simple start-and-stop toggle on the Speech menu. All three put the words at your cursor, and all three work without an internet connection.

QUILLBee’s tip: Can’t remember what you are allowed to say? While Live Dictation is running (Ctrl+F11), just say “what can I say” and the whole list opens in a window.

Live Dictation (Ctrl+F11)

This is the dictation QUILL Lite has, in QUILL too, on the same keys. It lives at Tools > Speech > Live Dictation.

  1. Put your cursor where you want the words.
  2. Press Ctrl+F11.
  3. Talk, then pause. Each phrase is written at the cursor when you pause. A soft tone tells you it arrived, and the words are read back to you.
  4. When you are done, press Ctrl+F11 again, or say “stop dictation”.

If you would rather hold Ctrl+F11 down while you talk and let go to stop, you can turn that on; see Holding Ctrl+F11 to talk, below. Dictation On, the first row of the submenu, is checked while dictation is writing, and the status bar says what dictation is doing.

Nothing leaves your computer, nothing is downloaded, and no recording is kept, unless you choose OpenAI dictation with your own key, or a speech provider with your own account (see “Which speech engine? A guide to choosing” above). Unlike Locked Dictation, which records a passage and transcribes it afterwards, Live Dictation writes as you go.

The same submenu has more windows:

  • Recent Phrases… (Shift+F11) lists the last twenty phrases of the session, with Insert Again and Copy. Words a correction replaced are there too, marked “(before correction)”, so nothing a correction changed is lost. After them come any words kept from before. See “Words that were not written, and dictation history”.
  • My Words and Phrases… (Alt+Shift+F10) is a window with Add Word, Add Phrase, Add Correction, Edit and Remove. It edits your dictation.md file for you. Import… and Export… move the list to another computer or another copy. See “Importing and exporting your words”.
  • Switch Dictation Language (Ctrl+Shift+F11) swaps between English and Spanish. See “Switching between English and Spanish”.
  • Start or Stop Live Transcript (Ctrl+Alt+Shift+PageDown) writes everything you say into a new document of its own. See “Live transcripts”.
  • Dictation Context for This Document… (Ctrl+Alt+Shift+PageUp) tells QUILL what this document is, in a sentence. See “Dictation Context for This Document”.
  • Speak a Command (F11) makes the next thing you say a command, as if you had said QUILL first. Press it twice for command mode. See “The command word”.
  • Review Dictation… (Shift+F4) holds the last phrase in a window to read, change and insert once. See “Review before inserting”.

A few things that are good to know while you talk:

  • Escape throws away the phrase being heard, and the words heard so far.
  • Your words go where you were when you started speaking, even if you move the cursor while they are being recognised (see “Where the words go”).
  • Pressing Ctrl+F11 in a second document moves dictation there.
  • If your microphone goes away, dictation pauses, and it picks up again when the device is back.
  • If the engine has a problem, QUILL quietly restarts it once before it tells you about a failure.

While dictating, say “what can I say” to open that list in a window. Near its end are Worked examples (what you have, what you say, what you get: going before and after words, picking the second of three matches, Copy All, scratch that, all twelve clips, snippets with spaces in their names, every capitals command, an email address spelled with pauses, and Markdown) and Limits, including what cannot work when dictating into another program. Every example there is checked by QUILL’s automated tests in both QUILL and QUILL Lite. How accurately and how quickly each engine hears you has not been measured yet; that waits on the dictation benchmarks.

The command word

Commands such as “scratch that” work when you say them on their own, after a pause. To be sure a command is taken as a command and never written, say the command word first: “QUILL scratch that”, “QUILL select the cat”, “QUILL caps on”. Every command in the list works this way.

  • QUILL counts only as the first word after a pause. “I told Quill about it” is written as words.
  • If what follows is not a command, nothing is written. You hear the error sound and “Not a command. Say it again, or say QUILL type that to write it.” Say “QUILL type that” and the words are written after all, once.
  • To write the word itself, say “literal QUILL”.
  • Say “QUILL” on its own, then pause, and the next thing you say is a command. A short rising tone tells you QUILL heard it.
  • Command mode: say “QUILL command mode”. You hear the tone and “Command mode”, and everything you say is a command, without the word, until you say “dictation mode”. If you give no command for eight seconds, command mode ends by itself with a falling tone, and what you say next is written again. The status bar says “Command mode” while it lasts.
  • Speak a Command (F11) does the same from the keyboard. Press it and the next thing you say is a command. Press it twice for command mode, and once more to go back to dictation. If dictation is off, it starts dictation first.

Three choices in More Dictation Settings go with it: the command word itself (Alt+C; empty means no spoken command word, so only F11 gives commands), Commands only after the command word (strict mode) (Alt+E), and how many seconds command mode waits (Alt+D, 5 to 30). With strict mode on, “scratch that” on its own is written as words; the stop phrase always works. Strict mode is off in QUILL and QUILL Lite, so the commands you know keep working, and on in QUILL Transcribe. A common word, or a word from your own My Words and Phrases, is refused as the command word, with the reason.

Dictation profiles: the kind of writing you are doing

A dictation profile changes dictation for one kind of writing in one step, instead of changing several settings each time. There are seven:

Profile What it changes
Standard Nothing: your Dictation Settings as they are. Headings are written as Markdown marks, as before. Everybody starts here.
Email Removes filler words and adds punctuation. Headings, bold and italic use QUILL’s own formatting.
Document Headings, bold and italic use QUILL’s own formatting.
Markdown Headings, bold and italic are written as Markdown marks.
Chat Removes filler words; plain text, with no heading marks.
Coding Removes filler words, adds no punctuation of its own (you say every mark), and waits longer for a pause. Markdown marks.
Verbatim Keeps every word you say, fillers too; plain text.

Choosing one. Open Dictation Settings (Alt+Shift+F6), press More Dictation Settings…, and choose in Dictation profile, for the kind of writing (Alt+A). Or, while dictating, say the profile’s name after the command word: “QUILL email mode”, “QUILL markdown mode”, “QUILL standard mode” (“email profile” works too). You hear “Email profile.” and the choice is kept for next time. The status bar shows a profile other than Standard beside the dictation modes, so a braille display shows it too.

Lock the profile so it cannot be switched by voice (Alt+B, in More Dictation Settings) stops a spoken profile name from switching: you hear “Lock Profile is on, so the profile stays Email.” Nothing ever switches profiles on its own.

Profiles and the Dictation style. While a profile other than Standard is on, its own choice about filler words wins over the Dictation style in Dictation Settings: Verbatim keeps fillers even if the style is Clean, and Email, Chat and Coding remove them even if the style is Verbatim. A profile never changes Fix it when I correct myself or polishing. Standard leaves your style exactly as it is.

Headings, bold and italic by voice. “heading one” to “heading six” at the start of a phrase, and “QUILL bold that” and “QUILL italic that” (or “italicize that”), act on the selection, or on the phrase you dictated last. What happens depends on the profile:

  • Markdown and Coding, and Standard for headings: the marks are written. “bold that” puts two asterisks either side, “italic that” one; one Ctrl+Z takes it back.
  • Document and Email: In a rich text document, QUILL’s own Heading and Bold commands apply the style. In a Markdown or HTML document, QUILL’s own commands write the marks or tags, and you hear “Heading 2.” or “Bold.” In a plain text document nothing is typed for the heading, and you hear “Headings work in QUILL documents or the Markdown profile.” Bold and italic say the same about themselves.
  • Chat and Verbatim: “heading two” starts a new line with your words and nothing else, and bold and italic say “This profile writes plain text, so nothing was formatted.”

Example. In a letter written as a rich text document, choose the Document profile, then say “heading two dear Sam” and pause. “Dear Sam” goes on a line of its own with the Heading 2 style. Select a word, say “QUILL bold that”, and it is bold.

The settings are windows_dictation_profile and windows_dictation_profile_locked, under the same names in QUILL, QUILL Lite and QUILL Transcribe.

Importing and exporting your words

Export… saves your whole list to a file you name. Choose Word list in Markdown for the same kind of file as dictation.md, or Spreadsheet, say and write columns for a CSV file with three columns: say, write and kind (word, phrase or correction). QUILL then says how many it saved, such as “Exported 15 entries to my-dictation-words.md.”

Import… reads a Markdown word list or a CSV file and adds what is new. A CSV file needs a say column; write and kind are optional. A row with nothing to write is a word, and any other row is a phrase unless kind says correction. Before anything changes, a window shows how many entries will be added and how many are already in your list, with the new ones listed. Press Add Them to add them, or Cancel to change nothing. Then you hear, for example, “Imported 12 words, 3 already there.”

Import never replaces anything. If the file has a word or phrase you already have, but spelled or written differently, yours is kept, and QUILL tells you how many differed. Your list as it was is saved first, as dictation-before-import.md beside dictation.md. To undo an import, copy that file over dictation.md. Export a list and import it into an empty list on another computer, and you get the same file back.

QUILL, QUILL Transcribe and Quill Inkwell all use this one list. QUILL Lite keeps its own unless you choose Use the shared word list in its My Words and Phrases window.

Where your word list lives

Your word list is one file, dictation.md, shared by QUILL, QUILL Transcribe, Quill Inkwell and (when you choose it) QUILL Lite. Since this version it lives in the family’s own dictation folder, with your dictation instructions (dictation-instructions.md) and your dictation contexts (dictation-contexts.json) beside it:

  • On an installed copy: %LOCALAPPDATA%\QuillVille\Dictation. To open it, press Windows+R, type %LOCALAPPDATA%\QuillVille\Dictation and press Enter. The speech models you download live there too.
  • On a portable copy: the dictation folder inside the portable copy’s own data folder. A portable copy never writes to the computer it runs on, and shares only with the programs in its own folder.

How your words got there. The first time any QuillVille program needs your list after the update, it copies dictation.md and its two companions from QUILL’s data folder (%APPDATA%\Quill, or the data folder you chose) into the new folder. It copies; it never moves or deletes. Your old files stay exactly as they were, as a backup, and you do not have to do anything.

  • If the new folder already has a list, it is never replaced by the old one.
  • If you go on adding words in a program that still uses the old file (an older copy of QUILL, say), the new words are added to the shared list the next time you dictate, the way Import adds them: where the two lists spell something differently, the shared list’s spelling is kept. The shared list as it was just before is saved as dictation-before-merge.md in the same folder.
  • If the copy cannot be made, for example because the disk is full, QUILL goes on reading your old file, so your list never looks empty, and tries the copy again the next time.

An example. You taught QUILL the name “Okonkwo” last month. After the update, open My Words and Phrases (Alt+Shift+F10): “Okonkwo” is still there, now read from %LOCALAPPDATA%\QuillVille\Dictation\dictation.md. Add “Tucson” in QUILL Transcribe, and the next phrase you dictate in QUILL spells it right too.

Sharing dictation settings with the family

Share dictation settings with every QuillVille program (Alt+Q) is a check box in More Dictation Settings. When it is on, the dictation settings you save in QUILL are used by QUILL Lite, QUILL Transcribe and Quill Inkwell on this computer too, and what you save in any of them reaches QUILL.

  • What is shared: the speech engine and model, the language, the microphone, automatic punctuation, the pause that ends a phrase, filler words, the wake phrase, the command word, the live preview, review first, the never-type list and the rest of the dictation choices.
  • What each program keeps to itself: its keys and tray, the working tick and strict mode (on in QUILL Transcribe on purpose), what you hear after each phrase (Inkwell starts with a tone, because your screen reader already reads what is typed), and every permission to send your speech to a company. Saying yes to OpenAI in QUILL is not saying yes in QUILL Transcribe.
  • When it arrives: the moment you save Dictation Settings with OK, the others can see it. QUILL picks up what another program saved when you switch back to its window, and the status bar says “Dictation settings updated from the family.” QUILL Transcribe picks it up within a second. Quill Inkwell picks it up the next time it starts Dictate Anywhere or opens Dictation Settings.
  • On or off to begin with: on for a new install, where nothing has been changed yet. If you had already changed your dictation settings before this update, it starts off, so nothing you chose is replaced behind your back; check the box when you want to join in.
  • Nothing is lost. The first time a program takes settings from the family, its own are saved first as before-sharing- and the program’s name, for example before-sharing-quill.json, in the family’s dictation folder.
  • Two programs saving at once: the last one saved wins. Each save writes the whole file at once, so it is never half written.
  • If it cannot be saved for the others, you hear why, for example “Saved here, but the other QuillVille programs will not see this change: the shared dictation settings could not be written (Access is denied).” QUILL’s own copy is saved either way.

An example. In QUILL Transcribe, choose Spanish with Switch Dictation Language (Ctrl+Shift+F11). Switch back to QUILL. The status bar says “Dictation settings updated from the family.”, and Ctrl+F11 now dictates in Spanish.

Review before inserting

When you want to check what you said before it goes into the document, review it first. Your words wait in the Review Dictation window, and nothing goes into the document until you insert them.

  • Say “QUILL review that”, or press Review Dictation (Shift+F4), while dictating. The last phrase is taken back out of the document and opened in the window. If dictation was paused with words held, those are what you get. Press Shift+F4 with dictation off and the window opens empty.
  • Read the words with the arrow keys. They are in an ordinary text box, so your screen reader reads them by line, word and character, and a braille display shows them. Type to change them, or keep talking: while the window is open, what you dictate is written into it, not into the document.
  • Ask the AI to change them. Type an instruction such as “make it shorter” in Ask the AI to change it (Alt+K) and press Enter, or say it after the command word: “QUILL make it shorter”, “QUILL more formal”, “QUILL fix the grammar only”. You hear “Working”, then what changed, in two sentences at most, such as “Changed next Thursday to Friday. Removed um, so.” QUILL works out what changed by comparing the words itself; it never repeats the AI’s own account. When more than half the words change you hear “Large change” and a reminder to read it. While that is said, the microphone does not listen, so your screen reader is never taken for your next instruction. Talk about it (Alt+A) makes everything you say an instruction, without the command word.
  • Insert (Ctrl+Enter, or say “QUILL insert it”) puts the words where they came from, as one step that Ctrl+Z takes back. The words as you said them and as they went in both go into Recent Phrases.

The window’s keys, and what to say instead:

Key What it does Say, after the command word
Ctrl+Enter Insert the words “insert it”
Ctrl+R Read all the words aloud “read it back”
Ctrl+D Say again what the last change did “what changed”
Ctrl+Z Undo the AI’s last change; with none, undo your typing “undo that change”
Ctrl+Shift+Z Back to the words exactly as you said them “back to what I said”
Escape Stop a request on its way; otherwise discard the words, asking first if the AI changed them “discard it”

The AI, and what is sent:

  • Changing words with AI needs AI help switched on and your ChatGPT subscription or your own AI key, as Tidy Dictated Text does; it never uses the free service’s allowance. Each change you ask for is one request, and the window’s Status line says so.
  • The first time, QUILL asks before anything is sent, and names who receives it. No is the default, and No sends nothing. Only the words in the window and your instruction are sent, with My Dictation Instructions if you wrote any. This document’s dictation context goes too only if you check Include this document’s dictation context with requests (Alt+O).
  • With AI off, or in Safe Mode, “QUILL review that” says “AI is off. Your words were written as you said them.” and leaves them where they were. The window still opens from Shift+F4, to edit and insert by hand.
  • If you close the window with Close, or QUILL closes or stops unexpectedly, your words are kept. The next time QUILL starts it says so, and Review Dictation brings them back. Discard throws them away.

To send everything you dictate to the window first, turn on Send dictation to review first (Alt+V) in More Dictation Settings. The first thing you say opens the window, and nothing reaches the document until you insert it.

Polishing your dictation with AI, only when you ask

Sometimes you want the punctuation and capitals tidied without rewording anything. Polish does exactly that, as one request you ask for each time. It is off until you turn it on, and it never runs by itself.

  1. Open Dictation Settings (Alt+Shift+F6), press More Dictation Settings… (Alt+A), and check Allow polishing my dictation with AI when I ask (Alt+G). Press OK in both windows.
  2. Dictate as usual. When you want a phrase polished, say “QUILL polish that” (or “QUILL polish it”). The last phrase is taken back out of the document and opened in Review Dictation, then the polish request is made there. If Review Dictation is already open, the words in it are polished.
  3. In the Review Dictation window you can also press Polish (Ctrl+P). That button is there only while polishing is allowed.
  4. You hear “Polishing”, then what changed, in two sentences at most, worked out by QUILL comparing the words itself. Read the words, then Insert (Ctrl+Enter) or take the change back with Undo Change (Ctrl+Z).

What the AI is asked: to fix punctuation, capital letters, spelling and obvious grammar slips only, and to keep your words, your meaning, your order and your tone. It is the same request every time.

What is sent, and when: polish uses the same AI, the same account and the same question as the rest of Review Dictation. The first time, QUILL asks before anything is sent and names who receives it; No is the default and sends nothing. Only the words in the window are sent. With AI off or in Safe Mode the Polish button is greyed out. With polishing not allowed, “QUILL polish that” says “Polish my dictation is off” and sends nothing.

Correcting yourself as you talk, and dictation styles

People correct themselves mid-sentence: “meet on Thursday, no, Friday”. With Fix it when I correct myself, like Thursday, no, Friday (Alt+X) checked in Dictation Settings, QUILL writes only what you meant:

You say QUILL writes
meet on Thursday, no, Friday at noon meet on Friday at noon
in June, no I mean July in July
I have two no three cats I have three cats
call me at 5, sorry, 6 call me at 6
send it to Ana, I mean Maria (with Maria and Ana in My Words) send it to Maria
Monday scratch that word Tuesday Tuesday

The words that mark a correction are “no”, “I mean”, “no I mean”, “sorry” and “scratch that word”. A correction is made only between two things of the same kind: two days (including today, tomorrow, tonight and yesterday), two months, two numbers, or two names from the Vocabulary list in My Words and Phrases. Everything else is written exactly as you said it, so these are left alone:

  • “no, I don’t think so” (nothing before the “no”);
  • “Is it Thursday? No, Friday.” (a question and its answer);
  • “I said no, Friday” (a word that is not a day before the “no”);
  • “I may, no, March” (“may” is a verb unless you say it as the month May).

It all happens on this computer, with no AI and no internet. It is off until you turn it on, and a live transcript never uses it, because a transcript keeps what the speaker said.

Dictation style (Alt+Y), just above, is a quick way to set these choices together:

  • Verbatim: every word as I say it. No filler removal, no correction.
  • Clean: no filler words, and my corrections fixed. Turns on Remove filler words and Fix it when I correct myself.
  • Polished: Clean, then review, and polish with AI when I ask. Clean, plus Send dictation to review first and Allow polishing my dictation with AI when I ask. It is offered only when AI help is on. Choosing it sends nothing: you still say “QUILL polish that” or press Polish each time.

If you change a check box yourself, the style shows Your own mix of the settings below, and choosing that changes nothing.

Learning from your corrections

When you correct the same word the same way twice, QUILL offers to remember it, so you do not have to correct it a third time. A correction counts when you say “QUILL correct that” and then “choose 2”, or when you say “QUILL correct Ann” and then say the new words.

The second time, a window called Add a Correction asks, for example, “Add a correction: Ann becomes Anne? It goes into My Words and Phrases, under Corrections.” No is the default. Press Yes to add it, or No or Escape to leave it. Dictation keeps going while you answer.

On Yes the correction goes into the Corrections list of My Words and Phrases (Alt+Shift+F10), the same list you can edit by hand, and the next phrase uses it. You hear “Added: Ann becomes Anne. It is in My Words and Phrases.”

Limits: each pair is offered once each time QUILL runs, whatever you answer. Only a single word or a short phrase of up to four words is offered, never a sentence. Nothing about your corrections is saved anywhere until you say yes.

Words for one kind of writing. My Words and Phrases’ file can hold a section headed, for example, ## Context: email, with words (one a line) and corrections (heard => write) meant only for that kind of writing. QUILL keeps these sections when it saves the file. They are ready for named dictation profiles, which are not in this version, so for now they are not used.

Words that were not written, and dictation history

Dictation never throws away words it could not write. If dictation pauses because its field moved, or Windows stops typing a phrase part-way through, the words are kept on this computer until you deal with them, even if QUILL is closed, crashes or the computer sleeps. Stopping dictation while words are held says so: “1 phrase held while paused was not written. They are kept in Recent Phrases.” The next time QUILL starts, it says once how many are waiting.

They are in Recent Phrases (Shift+F11), after this session’s phrases, labelled “Not written” with the time. So is a review that did not finish, labelled “Review not finished”. Choose one and:

  • Insert Again (Alt+I, or Enter) writes it at the cursor, and then it is deleted from this computer;
  • Copy (Alt+C) puts it on the clipboard and keeps it;
  • Discard (Alt+D) deletes it without writing it.

Only words that were never written are kept, and they are sealed for your Windows account, so another account on the same computer cannot read them. Nothing you dictate is kept as sound.

Dictation history is off unless you turn it on. In More Dictation Settings, Keep a dictation history (Alt+Y) can be Off, One day, Seven days or Thirty days. While it is on, the text of each phrase you dictate is kept for that long, sealed the same way, and Recent Phrases lists the earlier ones after the words not written, newest first, labelled “Earlier” with the time. Clear History (Alt+H) in Recent Phrases deletes all of it, after a question whose answer is No unless you choose Yes. Turning the history off deletes it too. Neither the kept words nor the history ever go into QUILL’s log or a diagnostic report.

Holding Ctrl+F11 to talk

Press Ctrl+F11 to start dictating and press it again to stop. That is all most people ever need, and it is how dictation starts out.

If you prefer to hold the key while you talk, like the button on a walkie-talkie, turn on Hold the dictation key to talk; a quick press still turns it on and off in More Dictation Settings. Then:

  1. Put the cursor where you want the words.
  2. Press and hold Ctrl+F11. You hear the two rising tones and “Dictation on” straight away.
  3. Keep holding the keys, and say what you want to say.
  4. Let go. Dictation writes your last phrase, then you hear the two falling tones and “Dictation off”.

With that turned on, a quick press still works just the same: dictation turns on and stays on until you press Ctrl+F11 again. QUILL tells the two apart by how long you hold the keys. Half a second or more is a hold.

Stopping never cuts you off. If you press or let go of Ctrl+F11 while you are still finishing a sentence, dictation waits for that phrase, writes it, and then stops.

Seeing the words while you speak

With most engines, your words appear when you pause. With Nemotron (an optional download, see “Better accuracy: optional speech models”) or OpenAI dictation, QUILL knows the words while you are still saying them, and shows them to you:

  • The status bar shows the words so far after the word Hearing, for example “Hearing: Can you send me the”.
  • If you use a braille display, the same words appear on it as they arrive.
  • They are not in your document yet, because the engine may still change its mind as it hears more. When you pause, the final words go into the document, and the preview goes away.

You do not hear these words spoken, so nothing talks over you while you talk. If you would like to hear them, open More Dictation Settings and set While you speak, the words heard so far to Show it, and say new words quietly. You then hear new words every couple of seconds, each one once, and only once the engine has settled on it: a word it is still changing its mind about is not spoken until it stops changing, and a word you have already heard is not said again if the engine corrects it later (the words that go into your document are always the final ones). Choose Do not show it to turn the preview off.

Nemotron also gets its punctuation right at the end of a sentence now. It decides whether a sentence was a question once it hears the next one, so a full stop it wrote at your pause can turn into a question mark a moment later, when you carry on talking.

Punctuation

You do not need to say punctuation. The built-in engines add full stops, commas, question marks and capitals by themselves. When you want a particular mark, say it (“comma”, “new paragraph”) and your word wins. If a pause splits a sentence and the next part begins with a word like and or which, the full stop the pause put in is taken back out.

Punctuation and layout, anywhere in a phrase. You can say “period”, “comma”, “question mark”, “open quote” and “close quote”, brackets, “hyphen”, “dash”, “slash”, symbols like “at sign” and “percent sign”, “new line”, “new paragraph” and “tab”. Say “literal” first to write one of them as a word.

What you hear

After each phrase you hear a soft tone and the words read back to you, so you know what landed without reading the line. The read-back says the punctuation too, by the same names you would use to dictate it. You say “Hello, world.” and you hear “Hello comma world period”. A new paragraph is read as “new paragraph”, a quotation mark as “open quote”, and so on. That happens whatever punctuation level your screen reader is set to, so you always know whether the comma went in.

A few things are left quiet because saying them would only get in the way: an apostrophe or a hyphen inside a word (“don’t”, “well-known”), and the point or comma inside a number (“3.5”, “1,000”). A web address reads the way you would say it: “example.com” is “example dot com”. In Spanish you hear the Spanish names, “coma” and “punto”.

The status bar and a braille display show the real characters, for example “Dictated: Hello, world.”, so on braille you read exactly what was written.

If you would rather hear just the words, turn off Say punctuation marks in the read-back in More Dictation Settings. To choose a tone only, the words only, both or neither, use What you hear after each phrase in Dictation Settings.

While a phrase is still being worked on. With a large model, such as one of the bigger Whisper models, there can be a few seconds between your pause and the text. Turn on Tick while a phrase is being worked on in More Dictation Settings and a soft tick plays once a second while you wait. There is no tick for a phrase that arrives within a second. The first time in a session that a phrase takes four seconds, you also hear “Still working on that phrase.” The ticks stop as soon as the text arrives, or if recognition fails. While a tick plays, the microphone is not listened to for a moment, as with every QUILL sound, so the tick is never written as speech. If your sound scheme has no tick sound, you hear the sentence at four seconds instead, each time. What you hear after each phrase decides it too: set to nothing, there is no tick and no sentence; set to words only, there is no tick, and the sentence is said each time a phrase takes four seconds. It is off by default in QUILL and QUILL Lite, and on in QUILL Transcribe.

Letters, numbers and symbols

Most of the time you just talk and the engine writes words. For the moments when you need something exact, an email address, a file name, a heading in a Markdown file, here is what you can say.

Spelling a whole run. Say “start spelling”, then the letters, then “stop spelling”. The phonetic alphabet (alpha, bravo, charlie…) is the reliable way. Say “capital” before a capital letter and “space” for a space. While you are spelling, punctuation goes in with no spaces around it, and “dot” (or “point”) writes a full stop. So “start spelling, juliet dot sierra mike india tango hotel at sign echo x-ray alpha mike papa lima echo dot charlie oscar mike, stop spelling” writes j.smith@example.com. Say “all caps” and every letter after it is a capital, until you say “no caps” or the phrase ends.

Spelling with pauses. You do not have to say an address in one breath. While spelling, each phrase joins the letters you spelled just before it, with no space, so “juliet oscar”, a pause, “at sign echo x-ray”, a pause, “dot charlie oscar” writes jo@ex.co. Say “space” when you want a space. Two limits: only letters, digits and punctuation are written, so a whole word such as “smith” is left out (spell it instead), and if you move the cursor between phrases the next letters start fresh, with a space in front as usual.

Spelling one word. Say “spell” and the letters, all in one phrase: “spell bravo alpha delta” writes bad. You do not need to start and stop spelling for that. A sentence that only happens to begin with the word “spell” (“spell it out for me”) stays words.

Spell that. If a name came out wrong, say “spell that”. The last phrase is selected, and the very next phrase you say is spelled and takes its place. After that one phrase you are back to ordinary dictation.

Capitals and spacing that stay on. These last until you turn them off, or until dictation stops, and the status bar shows you which are on:

  • “caps on” and “caps off” (or “capitals on” and “capitals off”): every word starts with a capital, handy for titles and names.
  • “all caps on” and “all caps off”: everything in capitals.
  • “no space on” and “no space off”: the words are joined with no spaces and no automatic capitals, for web addresses and file names.

Marks for Markdown and code. These can go anywhere in a phrase:

Say Writes
“backtick” or “back quote” a backtick, the mark under Escape on most keyboards. QUILL counts the ones on the line, so the next one opens or closes
“triple backtick” or “code fence” three backticks on a line of their own
“tilde” a tilde
“vertical bar” or “pipe symbol” a vertical bar
“caret” a caret
“greater than sign” a greater-than sign
“less than sign” a less-than sign

Starting a line. These only work as the first words of a phrase. If the cursor is not at the start of a line, QUILL starts a new one first:

Say Writes
“bullet” or “list item” a hyphen and a space, a bullet in Markdown
“numbered item” 1, a full stop and a space
“block quote” a greater-than sign and a space
“heading one” to “heading six” (or “heading 1” to “heading 6”) one to six number signs and a space

Because they only count at the start, “the heading two lines down” in the middle of a sentence is written as words, just as you meant it.

A Markdown link, by voice. Say “open bracket the guide close bracket open paren page close paren” and QUILL writes [the guide](page), with no space between the closing bracket and the opening parenthesis, which is what makes it a link. It works across two phrases too, if you pause after “close bracket”. An asterisk and an underscore join the words on both sides (two*three, snake_case), so for bold text say “bold that” with the Markdown profile rather than saying the asterisks.

Voice commands

Commands, as a whole phrase after a pause:

  • “scratch that” removes the last phrase
  • “undo that”
  • “select that”
  • “capitalize that”, “all caps that”, “no caps that”
  • “delete word”, “delete sentence”, “delete previous paragraph”
  • “read that” reads all of what last went in, a whole snippet included
  • “read last sentence”, “read last paragraph”, “read selection”
  • “go to beginning of line”, “go to end of line”, “go to top”, “go to end of document”
  • “select sentence”, “select line”, “select paragraph”
  • “go to start of paragraph”, “go to end of paragraph”
  • “spell that”, “caps on”, “no space on” and the others in “Letters, numbers and symbols”
  • “copy that”, “copy all”, “show clips”, “show snippets”
  • “stop dictation”

Reading back, a worked example. You have dictated “Thanks for the notes comma I will send the draft on Friday period” and want to check it by ear. Say “read last sentence”: you hear “Thanks for the notes comma I will send the draft on Friday period”, with the marks named when Say punctuation marks in the read-back (More Dictation Settings) is on, so a missing comma is easy to catch. “read last paragraph” (or “read this paragraph”) reads the whole paragraph the cursor is in, and “read selection” reads what you selected (“Nothing is selected.” when nothing is). “read that” reads all of what last went in, so after “insert snippet sign off” it reads the whole snippet, not only its name. Reading never moves the cursor or changes the text. In another program only “read that” works, because QUILL reads only the words it typed there.

Commands with words after them, said at the start of a phrase:

  • “select” and some words, “go to”, “go before” or “go after” and some words, “correct” and some words (see “Moving and selecting by voice”)
  • “replace” some words “with” new words, and “change that to” and new words (see “Replace and change that”)
  • “paste clip three”, “insert snippet” and a name, “insert abbreviation” and a trigger (see “Snippets and clips by voice”)
  • “switch to Spanish” (see “Switching between English and Spanish”)
  • “dictation context” and a name (see “Dictation Context for This Document”)

If you have typed into the last phrase since you said it, the commands that change it leave it alone, and tell you so.

That is the everyday set. Every command and every mark, in English and Spanish, is on the Dictation commands page, and “what can I say” opens the same list while you dictate.

More about reading back. Reading back also works in a document that is read-only, because it changes nothing. If there is nothing before the cursor you hear “There is no sentence here.” (or paragraph). To hear the words without the mark names, clear Say punctuation marks in the read-back. In Spanish, say “lee la última frase”, “lee el último párrafo” (or “lee este párrafo”) or “lee la selección”; “leer” works as well as “lee”.

Moving and selecting by voice

You can move around and pick out words by saying them, without reaching for the keyboard. Say these at the start of a phrase:

  • “select” and the words, for example “select the blue car”. QUILL looks back from the cursor for the nearest place those words appear, and if they are not behind you, it looks ahead. Whatever you say next replaces them.
  • “select” one set of words “through” another, for example “select dear Sam through regards”. Everything from the first to the last is selected.
  • “go to” or “go before” and the words puts the cursor just before them. “go after” puts it just after.
  • “correct” and the words selects them and tells you “Correcting the blue car. Say the new words”. Say the right words and they take the place of the wrong ones.
  • “select again” or “select next” moves on to the next place the same words appear, and “select previous” goes back to the one before.

And these as a whole phrase: “select sentence” (or “select this sentence”), “select line”, “select paragraph”, “go to start of paragraph” (or “go to beginning of paragraph”) and “go to end of paragraph”.

You do not have to be exact. Capitals, punctuation and accents do not matter, and numbers match either way, so “select two cats” finds “2 cats”. Your corrections from My Words and Phrases are applied first, so the words are found the way you taught QUILL to spell them.

You hear the outcome once: “Selected: the blue car”, “Before the blue car” or “After the blue car”. If the place is three or more lines away from where you were, the line number is added, like “Selected: the blue car, line 40”.

If the words are not in the document, nothing moves. Your phrase is written as ordinary text instead, and you hear “Not found, written as text.” Say “scratch that” to take it back out. This works with every speech engine, and the short exact commands, like “select that” and “go to top”, still do what they always did.

Snippets and clips by voice

The Copy Tray, your snippets and your abbreviations all answer to your voice too.

As a whole phrase, on its own:

  • “copy all” (or “copy everything” or “copy document”) copies the whole document, just as Copy All does.
  • “copy that” copies the phrase you just said.
  • “show clips” (or “open copy tray” or “show copy tray”) opens the Copy Tray dialog.
  • “show snippets” opens the list from Insert Snippet.

With words after them, at the start of a phrase:

  • “paste clip three” (or “paste slot three”) writes what is in Copy Tray slot 3. Any slot from 1 to 12 works. If the slot is empty, you are told so and nothing is written.
  • “insert snippet” and its name, for example “insert snippet sign off”. You do not need the exact name: QUILL ignores capitals, punctuation and spaces, tries the whole name first, then a name that starts with what you said, then one that contains your words. If several snippets match, you hear how many and the list opens so you can choose. If none do, you hear “No snippet called sign off” and nothing is written.
  • “insert abbreviation” (or “expand abbreviation”) and its trigger writes what that abbreviation stands for.

A snippet with blanks to fill in asks for each blank by voice before anything is written. With the starter Bug Report Template, “insert snippet bug report” says “Snippet Bug Report Template, 3 blanks. Say cancel to stop. Summary?”. What you say next is the answer, with spoken punctuation obeyed. For a choice, such as ${choice:NVDA|JAWS|Narrator}, you hear “Choose NVDA, JAWS or Narrator.”: say one of them, or “choose two”. Say skip to leave a blank empty, scratch that to answer the one before again, or cancel to write nothing. ${date} and ${time} come from this computer’s clock, and ${cursor} puts the cursor where the snippet says.

Whatever goes in, it goes in as one phrase: one Ctrl+Z takes it out, and so does “scratch that”. The read-back keeps it short, “Snippet sign off” or “Pasted slot 3”, rather than reading the whole thing to you; say “read that” to hear all of it. These commands do not work while Just write what I say is on, because commands are off then.

Correct that: choosing another guess

Sometimes the engine was not sure what you said, and had a second idea. Say “correct that” straight after the phrase, and you hear up to three other guesses, numbered, for example: “1: right to Ann. 2: write two Ann. Say choose and the number.” Then say “choose one”, “choose two” or “choose three”, and that guess takes the place of what was written. You hear “Changed to:” and the new words.

Windows speech recognition keeps its own other guesses. Moonshine, Whisper and the optional models give one answer each, so with them “correct that” asks for a second opinion instead: the other built-in engine listens to your last phrase again. You hear “Listening again with Whisper.” (or Moonshine), and a moment later both answers: “1: the whether is nice. 2: the weather is nice. Say choose and the number.” Say “choose two” to take the second. If both engines heard the same words, you are told so, and nothing changes. Whisper listens again after Moonshine, and Moonshine after Whisper or a downloaded model.

The second opinion uses only the sound of your last phrase, kept in memory until you say the next one: nothing is saved to disk and nothing goes over the internet. It is not offered in Spanish, where only Whisper understands you, or with OpenAI or a speech company. Then “correct that” tells you there is nothing to choose from: say “scratch that” and say it again, or add a correction in My Words and Phrases so it comes out right next time.

Whatever a correction replaced is not lost. It goes into Recent Phrases (Shift+F11, in the Live Dictation submenu) marked “(before correction)”, just after the corrected words, so you can put it back with Insert Again or copy it.

With any engine you can also say “correct” and the words that came out wrong, then say the right ones (see “Moving and selecting by voice”), or “spell that” to spell the last phrase out (see “Letters, numbers and symbols”).

Replace and change that: fixing words without retyping them

Two commands put new words in place of old ones, so you do not have to select anything first. Say each one at the start of a phrase.

“replace” the words “with” the new words. Say “replace Tuesday with Wednesday” and the nearest “Tuesday” before the cursor becomes “Wednesday”; if there is none before the cursor, QUILL looks after it. The new words take the capital and the closing full stop of the old ones, and the cursor stays where it was, so you can carry on dictating. You hear “Replaced Tuesday with Wednesday.” If the words are not there, what you said is written as ordinary text, as with “select” (you hear “Not found, written as text.”; say “scratch that” to take it away). After the command word, “QUILL replace Tuesday with Wednesday”, nothing is written when the words are not found.

“change that to” and the new words replaces the whole phrase you dictated last. You said “see you Thursday.” and meant Friday: say “change that to see you Friday”, and you hear “Changed to: See you Friday.” The leading space, the capital and the closing full stop stay as they were.

“delete previous paragraph” (or “delete last paragraph”) deletes the paragraph just before the cursor, from its start up to the cursor, with the blank line in front of it. Say it again to delete the one before. If text is selected, the selection is deleted instead.

Replace and change that keep what they replaced in Recent Phrases, marked “(before correction)”. “Scratch that” straight after a replace inside your last phrase takes away the whole corrected phrase.

Speech engines. The Speech engine list in Dictation Settings (Alt+Shift+F6) offers these:

  • OpenAI, only with your own OpenAI key and only if you choose it. Your speech is sent to OpenAI; see “OpenAI dictation, with your own key” below.

The wake phrase

The wake phrase. Switch on Listen for the wake phrase while dictation is off in Dictation Settings, then say “Quill dictate” (or a phrase of your own, at least two words) to start without touching the keyboard. Anything you say after it in the same breath is written.

While the wake phrase is on, the microphone is open whenever QUILL is the window in front. Nothing it hears is written, kept or sent unless it begins with the wake phrase, and the microphone closes when another program comes to the front. It never listens in the background.

Your own words and phrases

Your own words and phrases. Edit My Words and Phrases… in Dictation Settings opens your dictation.md, the same file Locked Dictation reads. Vocabulary entries correct near misses to your spelling. Replacements (say => write, with \n for a new line) are spoken phrases of your own. In that file \t is a tab and \\ is one backslash, so a phrase that writes the folder C:\new is saved as my folder => C:\\new. My Words and Phrases writes it that way for you; it matters only when you edit the file by hand. A single backslash before any other letter is kept as you typed it.

Dictating in Spanish

You can dictate in Spanish. This is new, so treat it as something to try, and tell us how it goes.

To switch it on, open Dictation Settings (Alt+Shift+F6), move to Dictation language, choose Spanish, and press Enter. That’s all. To go back, choose English the same way. There is a quicker way too, with one key or by voice; see “Switching between English and Spanish” below.

What works now:

  • Your words come out in Spanish, accents and all. With Moonshine or Whisper, QUILL uses Whisper’s multilingual speech model, which comes with QUILL, so there is nothing to download. Moonshine only knows English, so in Spanish you get Whisper either way.
  • A better Spanish model, said once. The built-in Whisper gets about one word in four wrong in Spanish. If your computer can run NVIDIA Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 (in practice, 8 GB of memory and four cores), the first time you dictate in Spanish you hear one sentence saying so, for example: “For Spanish, NVIDIA Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 gets about 6 percent of words wrong, against 29 for the built-in model. It is a 670 MB download in the model list.” Nothing is downloaded or changed; the choice is yours. You hear it once, in whichever QuillVille program you dictate in first. A computer with 4 GB hears nothing, because the built-in model is the right one there.
  • With Windows speech recognition, Windows needs Spanish installed: add Spanish (Spain or Mexico) in Windows Settings, Time and language, Speech. If it isn’t there, dictation tells you so when you start.
  • The wake and stop phrases become “Quill dicta” and “deja de dictar”. If you have typed your own, yours are kept.

Commands stay in English for now. “Scratch that”, “select that”, “new paragraph” and the rest work exactly as they do in English. Spanish commands are on the way, once a native speaker has checked them.

Punctuation. With automatic punctuation on (the usual setting), Whisper puts in the commas, full stops and question marks for you, so just speak. Spanish punctuation words only work when automatic punctuation is off, or with Windows speech recognition. That’s because “coma” and “punto” are everyday words too, and we don’t want “que coma” turned into a comma. When they are on, you can say:

  • “coma” for a comma, “punto” (or “punto y seguido”) for a full stop, and “punto y aparte” for a full stop and a new paragraph
  • “punto y coma”, “dos puntos” and “puntos suspensivos”
  • “abrir interrogación” and “cerrar interrogación” for the question marks at each end, and “abrir exclamación” and “cerrar exclamación” the same way
  • “abrir paréntesis”, “cerrar paréntesis”, “abrir comillas”, “cerrar comillas”
  • “guion”, “guion largo” and “arroba”
  • “nueva línea”, “nuevo párrafo” and “tabulador”

Say “literal” first to write the word itself: “literal coma” writes coma. Say “what can I say” while dictating to see the whole list.

This is new, and we’d love to hear from you. If a word keeps coming out wrong, or something doesn’t work the way you expect, use Help > Get Help from Support and tell us what you said and what was written.

Switching between English and Spanish

If you write in both languages, you do not need to open Dictation Settings each time. Any of these switches you over, even in the middle of dictating:

  • Press Switch Dictation Language (Ctrl+Shift+F11). Press it again to go back.
  • In English, say “switch to Spanish” or “Spanish dictation”.
  • In Spanish, say “cambiar a inglés” or “dictado en inglés”. The accent on “inglés” is optional, so it works however the engine writes it.

You hear “Español.” or “English.” so you know where you are. QUILL remembers the choice, so next time you start dictating it is in the language you last used.

The first time you switch to Spanish in a session it can take a few seconds, because the speech engine is getting ready for the new language (with Moonshine, which only knows English, that means loading Whisper). After that, switching back and forth is quick, because both stay ready until you close QUILL.

QUILL does not guess the language phrase by phrase. You choose it, and it stays that way until you switch again. That means a Spanish name in the middle of an English sentence will be heard as English; for those, add the name to My Words and Phrases, or say “spell that” and spell it.

Better accuracy: optional speech models

Dictation works the moment you install. If you want more accuracy, you can download a larger model.

These are the same local models VS Code offers for its own dictation, plus the rest of the Whisper family. They are free, they are optional, and they run on your computer’s processor; no graphics card needed. Your voice never leaves the computer: the only thing that comes over the internet is the model itself, once, when you ask for it. Nothing downloads on its own, and the built-in Moonshine stays your engine until you choose another.

To download one:

  1. Open Dictation Settings (Alt+Shift+F6) and press Better Accuracy: Speech Models… (Alt+B).
  2. Arrow through the list. Each row says what the model is good for, how big it is, and whether it is already here. Details (Alt+T) says more: what it is better at, its languages, its download and disk size, which computers suit it, whether this computer should keep up, its published accuracy, its licence, and the folder it will be saved in.
  3. Press Download… (Alt+D). A question tells you where the model comes from, how big it is, its licence and where it will be saved. Answer Yes to start. On a metered connection you are asked about that first.
  4. You hear “Downloading”, then every quarter of the way. Cancel Download (Alt+L) stops it. What has arrived is kept, so pressing Download again carries on from there, even after you close QUILL.
  5. When it says the model is downloaded, press Use for Dictation (Alt+U), then OK in Dictation Settings. Or choose the model in the Speech engine list yourself: downloaded models are listed there with “(downloaded)” and the model’s tier after the name.

Every file is checked against a fingerprint (a checksum) we recorded when we added the model. If a file does not match, it is deleted and you are told, so a damaged or changed download can never be used.

Which model should I use? Speech Models recommends one. Near the top of the window, the read-only Recommendation box (Alt+C) names the model that suits this computer and your dictation language, and says why: whether it keeps up here, about how soon your words appear after you pause, whether your speech stays on this computer (it always does), and how big the download is, or that it is already installed. The lines after it say more, for example a model that makes fewer mistakes but would slow your screen reader here. What matters most (Alt+W) changes the advice: a balance of speed and accuracy (the usual choice), the quickest response, or the fewest mistakes this computer can keep up with. Show Recommended Model (Alt+S) moves to that model in the list, so you can read its details and download it. Dictation Settings shows the same advice in one line, in the Recommended for this computer box under the Better Accuracy button.

Switch to Recommended Model (Alt+H), beside it, chooses the recommended model as your speech engine and changes nothing else: your words, instructions, pauses and punctuation stay as they are. Speech Models closes, and Dictation Settings says “Speech engine:” and the model’s name, then “Press OK to keep it.” Press OK there to keep it, or Cancel to keep the engine you had. It never downloads anything. If the model is not on this computer yet, you hear that it is not here yet (“Download it, then switch.”), and you are taken to its row in the list, so you can press Download… (Alt+D). While a model is downloading, it asks you to wait for the download or press Cancel Download first.

How accurate are they?

Our own tests on real speech, which the advice uses, are in “How we chose: tested on real speech” below. These are the figures each model’s makers publish. A word error rate is the share of words a model gets wrong on a recorded test set; lower is better. Test sets differ, so compare numbers only within one line.

  • Nemotron 3.5 ASR Streaming: 7.91 percent on FLEURS English and 4.11 percent on FLEURS Spanish, at the chunk size this download uses (NVIDIA’s model card, huggingface.co/nvidia/nemotron-3.5-asr-streaming-0.6b).
  • Parakeet Unified: 1.63 percent on LibriSpeech test-clean and 3.11 percent on test-other (huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-unified-en-0.6b).

  • Whisper, on LibriSpeech test-clean (OpenAI’s Whisper paper, Appendix D, arxiv.org/abs/2212.04356): tiny 7.6, base 5.0, small 3.4, medium 2.9 percent; English-only tiny.en 5.6, base.en 4.2. How we chose: tested on real speech

QUILL’s advice comes from our own tests with real people’s recordings, run on 22 October 2026. Before that, it rested on ten recordings of a synthetic voice, which made every model look about the same.

We fed each model recordings the way dictation does, one phrase at a time, on one processor thread, because the screen reader needs the rest. The fairest test is public-domain audiobooks recorded after every model was trained, so no model can have learned them; we also used Google’s FLEURS read sentences. Everything ran on one laptop on one day, so your speeds will differ a little. The figures are the percentage of words wrong (lower is better) and the most memory each model used.

Nemotron or Parakeet: which should I try?

For the fewest mistakes, choose Parakeet. On real recordings, Parakeet Unified got 3.2 percent of words wrong on the held-out audiobooks and Nemotron 5.3 percent; on FLEURS English, 6.3 against 13.7. In Spanish, Parakeet TDT v3 got 4.4 percent wrong on FLEURS and Nemotron 6.4. Parakeet is also lighter: on one processor thread, Parakeet Unified needed 0.21 seconds of computing per second of speech, Parakeet TDT v3 0.11 and Nemotron 0.37, where dictation’s budget is 0.25. Nemotron used the most memory of the three, about 1.5 GB.

Nemotron is still the one that shows your words while you speak. If you write as you go and want to hear or feel each word arrive, that is worth more than a few extra corrections.

All three are free NVIDIA models of about 0.6 billion parameters, and run here on the processor alone, with your voice staying on your computer. All three write punctuation and capitals themselves.

  • Parakeet recognises each finished phrase after you pause. Nothing appears until you stop.
    • Parakeet Unified 0.6B is English only, and what QUILL recommends for English on a computer with 8 GB and four cores. NVIDIA trained it to work both while you speak and on a finished phrase; QUILL uses it on the finished phrase. About 663 MB.
    • Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 knows English, Spanish and 23 other European languages. QUILL recommends it for Spanish on 8 GB, and for English on 8 GB with two cores. About 670 MB.
  • Nemotron 3.5 ASR Streaming recognises while you speak, in short slices. Its words appear in the status bar and on braille while you talk, and each phrase is written when you pause. It decides whether a sentence was a question once it hears the next one, so a full stop it wrote can turn into a question mark a moment later. It knows English and Spanish. About 682 MB.

Rule of thumb: for the fewest mistakes, choose Parakeet; to see your words as you speak, choose Nemotron.

How to tell which is more accurate for you

The published figures come from other people’s voices. Yours is the one that matters.

  1. Download one model, choose it, and dictate the way you normally do for a day.
  2. Each time a phrase comes out wrong, note it. Recent Phrases (Shift+F11) shows what was written, so you can compare.
  3. Switch to another model in the Speech engine list and do the same.
  4. Keep the one that made fewer mistakes, and remove the other to free the space.

If you like a more exact answer, QUILL’s benchmark measures any model on your own recordings. Record ten sentences as 16 kHz mono WAV files, write what you said in a transcripts.txt beside them (one line per file: the name, a tab, the words), and run python scripts/dictbench.py <folder> --model parakeet_unified in a copy of QUILL’s source. It prints the share of words wrong, which marks were written, the speed, the memory and how long the model took to load.

OpenAI dictation, with your own key

If you have your own OpenAI key, you can have OpenAI recognise your speech instead of your computer. It is very accurate, punctuates well, gets names right that it has never met, and can show your words while you speak. It is off unless you choose it, and your computer’s own engines stay the default.

Before you choose it, please know this: what you say is sent to OpenAI. While dictation is on with this engine, each phrase you speak goes over an encrypted connection to OpenAI, with your own key, and the words come back. OpenAI bills your account for it. Nothing goes through QUILL’s servers, and QUILL keeps no copy of your voice. OpenAI’s own policies apply to what it receives; read them at openai.com/policies. Silence is never sent, only the speech dictation heard. The built-in engines never send anything anywhere.

What you need: your own OpenAI key, saved in Use My Own AI Key (see the AI chapter). QUILL’s free AI is never used for dictation. OpenAI dictation does not work in Safe Mode.

Adding your key from here. You don’t have to go looking for it: in Dictation Settings (Alt+Shift+F6), choose More Dictation Settings…, and under “OpenAI, with your own key” press Add or Change OpenAI Key… (Alt+K). Your settings are saved, Use My Own AI Key opens, and once you paste your key, OpenAI appears in the speech engine list. You can also open Use My Own AI Key any time with Alt+F2.

Setting it up, step by step:

  1. Open Dictation Settings (Alt+Shift+F6).
  2. In Speech engine, choose OpenAI (your own key; sends your speech to OpenAI). It is only in the list on a computer where an OpenAI key is saved.
  3. A question says exactly what is sent. Read it, then choose Yes to agree, or No to keep the engine you had. If you just press Enter, the answer is No, and nothing is sent.
  4. More Dictation Settings opens with the cursor on OpenAI speech model. QUILL asks OpenAI which speech models your key can use and lists them, newest first. The newest is already chosen.
  5. Press Enter to close More Dictation Settings, and Enter again to save Dictation Settings.
  6. Press Ctrl+F11 and talk, just as before.

Which model? The list comes from OpenAI, so it is always current, and models OpenAI is retiring are left out. On the day this was written it held two:

  • gpt-live-transcribe writes as you speak. The words heard so far appear in the status bar and on braille, and each phrase arrives very soon after you pause.
  • gpt-transcribe sends each phrase when you pause, and the words arrive a moment later.

If your model goes away. If OpenAI stops offering the model you chose, More Dictation Settings tells you once, and leaves the choice empty so you can pick another. If you are dictating when it happens, dictation stops and tells you. It never moves to a different model on its own.

Your words help. The names and terms in My Words and Phrases are sent with your speech as words to expect, so they come out spelled your way.

To stop sending your speech, choose another engine in Dictation Settings, or turn off Let OpenAI dictation send my speech to OpenAI in More Dictation Settings.

What it costs. OpenAI charges your account by the minute of speech. QUILL keeps no count; your OpenAI account’s usage page has the real figures.

What you might hear:

  • “OpenAI dictation needs your own OpenAI key. Save one in Use My Own AI Key, in the AI menu, first.”
  • “OpenAI dictation is not switched on.” Choose OpenAI in Dictation Settings and agree to the question.
  • “OpenAI could not be reached. Check the internet connection.” That phrase is lost, and dictation keeps listening.
  • “OpenAI did not accept the saved key.” Check your key in Use My Own AI Key.
  • “The OpenAI model … is no longer available to your key.” Choose another in More Dictation Settings.

Speech providers: dictation with your own account at another company

If you already have an account with a speech company, dictation can use it. QUILL knows twelve: Microsoft Azure AI Speech, Google Cloud Speech-to-Text, Amazon Transcribe, Deepgram, AssemblyAI, Speechmatics, ElevenLabs Scribe, Gladia, Rev.ai, Groq, Mistral Voxtral and IBM Watson Speech to Text. Your computer’s own engines stay the default and the recommendation; none of this is needed, and none of it is on until you choose it.

What is sent. With one of these chosen as the speech engine, each phrase dictation hears is sent over an encrypted connection to that company, with your account, and the words come back. Nothing is sent while nobody is speaking. The company bills you, not QUILL; QUILL shows no prices and counts nothing, so look at your provider’s own usage page. Nothing goes through QUILL’s servers and QUILL keeps no copy of your voice. None of it works in Safe Mode.

Setting one up:

  1. Open Dictation Settings (Alt+Shift+F6) and choose Speech Providers… (Alt+V).
  2. In Providers, arrow to the company. About this provider says whether it shows your words while you speak, whether it learns your words from My Words and Phrases (and how many), where your speech goes and its usage page.
  3. Tab to its fields and fill them in: usually a key, sometimes a region, a project or an address. Each field’s F1 says where to find it in that company’s console. A key is never shown again once saved; leaving a key field empty keeps the one already saved.
  4. Press Test Connection (Alt+T). QUILL asks the company about your account, never with your speech, and says what it answered, such as “Azure answered: ready, region westeurope.” If something is wrong it says which field, such as “The key was refused. Check Key.”, and puts you there.
  5. Press the Save button (Alt+S). Keys go to Windows Credential Manager at once and are never written in QUILL’s settings or logs. The other details are kept when you choose OK in Dictation Settings.
  6. Close the page. The company is now in the Speech engine list. Choose it, and a question says exactly what will be sent and to whom. No is the default; nothing is sent unless you choose Yes.

Remove (Alt+M) forgets everything saved for that company, keys included.

Which ones show words while you speak. Azure, Amazon, Deepgram, AssemblyAI, Speechmatics, ElevenLabs, Gladia, Rev.ai and IBM Watson do. Google, Groq and Mistral send each phrase when you pause, so there is no live preview with them.

If the connection goes. A lost phrase is said in one sentence (“Groq could not be reached. Check the internet connection.”) and dictation keeps listening; a refused key or an empty account stops dictation and says so. QUILL never switches to another engine by itself.

Where they work. Speech providers are for Live Dictation, here and in QUILL Lite, QUILL Transcribe and Quill Inkwell, which have the same Speech Providers page. They are not offered in Transcribe a Recording (Shift+F5), Transcribe Audio or Video (Offline), captions or watch folders, and they are never recommended; the recommendation only ever names a model on your computer.

In settings files, each company’s other details are kept in windows_dictation_speech_providers and your answers to the consent questions in windows_dictation_speech_provider_consent. Keys are never there.

Talking to the AI

You can have a spoken conversation with AI help. Open the AI Conversation window (see the AI chapter). The cursor is already in Your message.

  1. Press Ctrl+F11 and ask your question.
  2. Pause. Your message is sent, and you hear “Working.”
  3. The reply is read aloud. While it is being read, the microphone does not listen, so the reply is never taken as your next message.
  4. When the reply has been read, just carry on talking. If you want to speak before it has finished, press Escape: you hear “Listening.” and you can talk straight away.
  5. Press Ctrl+F11 again when you are done.

While you are in that box, dictation uses its Talking to AI settings, and it goes back to your usual ones by itself when you leave:

  • A longer pause (about a second and a half), so a breath does not send half a question.
  • Filler words removed, because the AI does not need them.
  • Automatic punctuation on.
  • “Just write what I say” does not apply here, because the pause is what sends your message.

All four are in More Dictation Settings, under Talking to the AI. If you would rather check each message before it goes, set Send a dictated message to the AI to When I press Enter. Then pause as often as you like, and press Enter when the message is right.

Each message costs the same as a typed one: one request on QUILL’s free AI, or nothing extra beyond your own key or plan.

Where the words go

Where the words go. Into the document you started in, replacing any selection. Each phrase is one step for Ctrl+Z.

Your words go where you were when you started speaking. If you move the cursor, or switch to another tab or window, while a phrase is still being recognised, it is still written where you were speaking. Your cursor is put back where you moved it, and you hear “Written where you started”, with the document’s name if you were somewhere else by then. If that spot has changed in the meantime, the words go at the cursor in that document instead, and you are told.

After that, if you speak while another tab, window, program or dialog is in front, nothing is written: dictation stops and tells you why. Closing the document stops it too. A live transcript is the one exception, and the next section explains it.

Live transcripts

A live transcript is for when you want everything written down and you do not want to steer: a meeting, a lecture, a long think out loud. Press Start or Stop Live Transcript (Ctrl+Alt+Shift+PageDown). QUILL opens a new, untitled document, never the one you were working in, and writes everything it hears into it.

It behaves differently from ordinary dictation, on purpose:

  • It just writes. Voice commands are written as words; only the stop phrase is obeyed. There is no tone and no read-back after each phrase, no wake phrase, and it never stops because the room went quiet.
  • It tidies as it goes. Filler words like um and uh are left out, and automatic punctuation is on.
  • A long pause starts a new paragraph. Four seconds or more of quiet, and the next words begin a fresh paragraph. If you turn on Time stamps in live transcripts in More Dictation Settings, each paragraph starts with the time in square brackets, like [10:42].
  • The words always go at the end. You can switch to another document, or another program altogether, and the transcript carries on writing at its own end. If you go back into the transcript to read or fix something, your place is kept and new words still arrive at the bottom.

The status bar keeps a count, for example “Live transcript: 12 minutes, 1,840 words”, and Dictation Status (Alt+F9) tells you the same.

To stop, press Ctrl+Alt+Shift+PageDown again, or Ctrl+F11. You hear “Live transcript stopped, 1,840 words.” The document stays open and is not saved yet, so save it when you are ready. Until then QUILL’s recovery protects it, the same as any unsaved document.

The very first time you start one, you hear: “Please record other people only when they have agreed.” It is said once, and that is the only reminder.

A live transcript listens to your microphone. It does not hear sound playing on the computer itself, so for an online meeting it picks up what reaches the microphone, not the other people’s voices through your headphones. When it is done, you can turn it into minutes or notes with a Transcript Action (see “Transcript Actions” later in this chapter).

Dictating well

Dictation gets much better with a few habits, and none of them are hard. This is what is worth knowing before you sit down to dictate for the first time.

Your microphone

Any microphone works, but the closer it is to your mouth and the further from everything else, the better your words come out.

  • A headset is best. A USB or Bluetooth headset with a boom microphone stays the same distance from your mouth however you move your head. That steadiness matters more than price.
  • Place it just off to the side. About two fingers from the corner of your mouth, not straight in front, so your breath does not puff into it.
  • A laptop’s own microphone will do in a quiet room, but it also hears the keyboard, the fan and your screen reader. If words keep coming out wrong, a headset is the first thing to try.
  • Check it. Test Microphone in Dictation Settings (Alt+Shift+F6) records four seconds and tells you how loud you were and what the engine heard. If it says you are quiet, move the microphone closer before you turn anything up.
  • Too quiet, and it may not hear you at all. If the microphone’s level is set very low, or you speak very softly or far from it, dictation can miss a whole sentence, especially just after something louder. In our tests with very quiet recordings it missed some entirely. Move closer, or raise the level in Windows’ sound settings.

The room

A quiet room helps more than any setting. A television, a radio or a conversation across the room gets heard as words. Close a door if you can. Hard, bare rooms echo; a room with soft furnishings sounds better to a microphone than a kitchen does.

Speaking in phrases

Talk in phrases, the way you would read a sentence aloud to someone, and pause at the natural places. Each pause sends a phrase to be written, so a phrase is also the size of thing “scratch that” takes back.

  • Speak at your normal pace. Slowing down to one word at a time actually makes it harder for the engine, because it uses the words around each word to work out what you meant.
  • Finish the thought before you pause where you can. If you do stop halfway, on a word like “the” or “and”, QUILL joins the two halves for you (see “When you stop in the middle of a thought”).
  • If you are a thoughtful speaker who often pauses between words, and dictation keeps writing your sentence in pieces, choose a longer Pause before a phrase is written in Dictation Settings. Longer waits two seconds and Longest three. Your words will appear a little later after each pause, but they will come out whole. If you talk quickly and want your words fast, Short is for you.

Punctuation: say it or let it be

With Moonshine, Whisper and the downloaded models, automatic punctuation is on, and for ordinary writing the easiest thing is to just talk and let the engine put in full stops, commas and question marks.

Say a mark when you want something particular: “new paragraph” to start one, “colon” before a list, “open quote” and “close quote” around a quotation, or a comma the engine would not have guessed. Your spoken mark always wins. If you would rather say every mark yourself, turn Automatic punctuation off, and the engine will write only what you say.

Two habits that help: say “new paragraph” as a phrase of its own, and say “period” at the end of a sentence you want to be sure is finished. The read-back says each mark by name, so you always hear whether it went in.

Fixing mistakes as you go

Every engine gets a word wrong now and then. Fixing it by voice soon becomes a quick loop:

  1. “scratch that” takes back the whole last phrase. Say it again, a little more clearly.
  2. “correct” and the wrong words (for example “correct red rocks”) selects them. Say the right words and they replace the wrong ones.
  3. “select” and some words does the same for anything further back.
  4. “spell that” is for a name or a word the engine has never heard. Spell it with the phonetic alphabet in your next phrase.
  5. Recent Phrases (Shift+F11) shows what was written for your last twenty phrases, so you can see what went wrong, and any words that were never written. Say “read last sentence” to hear a sentence back with its punctuation.
  6. When you are done, Tidy Dictated Text (Ctrl+F3) can fix sound-alike words, names and punctuation in a paragraph or a selection with AI, if you have a ChatGPT subscription or your own OpenAI key (see “Tidy Dictated Text” later in this chapter).

If the same word comes out wrong again and again, add it to My Words and Phrases (Alt+Shift+F10). That fixes it for good, in every engine.

Dictating with a screen reader running

QUILL was built for this. Two things make it smooth:

  • Wear headphones. If the read-back and your screen reader come out of speakers, the microphone hears them, and they can end up in your document. With headphones, the microphone only hears you.
  • Choose what you hear. After each phrase QUILL plays a soft tone and reads the phrase back, marks and all, so you can check it without moving the cursor. If that is more than you want, set What you hear after each phrase in Dictation Settings to a sound only, and read the line with your screen reader when you choose to. If you would like to hear the words but not the marks, turn off Say punctuation marks in the read-back in More Dictation Settings.

While dictation is listening, QUILL keeps the speech engine light enough that your screen reader stays quick. If you find it slowing down, see “On a modest computer” and the choices below.

Choosing a speech engine

  • Moonshine (built in) is the place to start. It is quick on any computer and good with a clear voice and a decent microphone.
  • Whisper (built in) is a little slower and sometimes better with accents or a busier room. It is also what QUILL uses for Spanish.
  • A downloaded model, such as Parakeet or Nemotron, is more accurate, especially with names, accents and fast speech, and still runs entirely on your computer. On a computer with 8 GB of memory and four cores, QUILL recommends NVIDIA Parakeet Unified 0.6B for English; on 8 GB, NVIDIA Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 for Spanish; see “Better accuracy: optional speech models”.
  • OpenAI, with your own key, is very accurate and very good with names, but your speech is sent to OpenAI and they charge you for it. See “OpenAI dictation, with your own key”.
  • A speech provider, with your own account, such as Azure, Deepgram or AssemblyAI, is the same idea with another company: very accurate, sent to that company, billed by them. See “Speech providers: dictation with your own account at another company”.
  • Windows speech recognition is there for a language the others do not know, but it puts in no punctuation and mishears an untrained voice often.

Speech Models can suggest a model for this computer: open Dictation Settings (Alt+Shift+F6), press Better Accuracy: Speech Models… (Alt+B), choose what matters most to you (Alt+W) and read the Recommendation (Alt+C). “Which speech engine? A guide to choosing”, at the start of this chapter, walks through it with an example.

Try one for a day before you judge it. Everyone’s voice suits a different engine.

Spanish

Everything here holds for Spanish too. Use Whisper or a many-language downloaded model; the larger ones are much better in Spanish than the built-in one. Let automatic punctuation do the marks, and switch languages with Ctrl+Shift+F11 or by saying “switch to Spanish” (see “Switching between English and Spanish”).

On an older or slower computer

Stay with the built-in Moonshine; it uses very little. Choose Normal or a longer pause rather than Short, close programs you are not using, and keep the laptop plugged in while you dictate, because battery saver slows everything down.

Practice: try these

Here are two short paragraphs to read aloud. Start Live Dictation with Ctrl+F11 in a new document, read each line as one phrase, and pause at the end of each. Then compare what you got with what should come out.

Practice one: let the engine punctuate. Just read these naturally, with no marks:

Dear Sam [pause] thank you for the lovely flowers they arrived this morning [pause] I will see you on Saturday at the market [pause]

Practice two: say the marks. Read these exactly as written, marks and all:

What should come out:

Locked Dictation (Ctrl+F9)

Locked Dictation is a keyboard-only way to dictate a longer passage without opening a dialog or leaving the editor. It uses the same offline engine and microphone as Dictate (Offline), so nothing is uploaded: Whisper.cpp, the QUILL speech models or Faster Whisper, whichever you chose in Manage Speech Models. It needs no speech model to start: until your engine has one, it uses the Moonshine tiny model that comes with QUILL. See “The offline speech tools’ engines”.

  1. Put your cursor where the text should go.
  2. Press Ctrl+F9. QUILL says “Locked dictation on.”
  3. Speak as long as you like, across sentences and paragraphs.
  4. Press Ctrl+F9 again to finish. QUILL transcribes what you said and inserts it at the cursor as one undoable edit.

There used to be a press-and-hold F9 mode. It was removed, because a held key repeats and announces itself endlessly under a screen reader.

Stopping, pausing and checking

  • Stopping safely. While recording, Escape stops and keeps your speech for transcription. Shift+Escape cancels and throws it away. A session also stops by itself after five minutes, and it stops and keeps your audio if QUILL loses focus.
  • Pause and status. Ctrl+Shift+F9 pauses or resumes the session. Alt+F9 speaks the current state without changing anything. When Locked Dictation is not recording, the same key answers for Live Dictation instead (see “Dictation Status”).
  • Distinct sound, one-time hint. Locked Dictation plays its own sounds, and the very first time you dictate, QUILL speaks a short hint about the keys.
  • Remappable. Ctrl+F9 and the rest are only the defaults. Change them in the Keymap Editor (Tools > Customize and Support > Keymap Editor…).

Locked Dictation needs no model: with no other model ready, it uses the built-in Moonshine tiny. With Whisper.cpp as the engine (the default), the first press of Ctrl+F9 offers the small Whisper.cpp engine itself (about 8 MB, verified); answer Yes, and dictation is ready when it finishes. With QUILL speech models as the engine, nothing is downloaded at all. For fewer mistakes, choose a larger model in Manage Speech Models (Tools > Speech > Speech and Dictation…). It also needs microphone-capture support, which standard installs include; if it is missing, QUILL tells you. In Safe Mode, dictation is turned off.

Nothing is lost

Nothing is lost. Your audio is saved to a recovery folder before transcription starts, and a transcript that cannot be safely inserted is kept for you to review.

Dictation History & Review. Tools > Speech > Locked Dictation > Dictation History & Review… lists every recording whose transcript was never inserted: ones left behind by a crash, and ones that could not be placed safely. For each you can insert it at the cursor, copy it, or discard it. Enter inserts the selected row. If anything is waiting for review when QUILL starts, QUILL tells you and points you here, so dictated speech is never silently lost.

Locked Dictation settings

Dictation Settings. Tools > Speech > Locked Dictation > Dictation Settings… holds the choices the dictation engine reads:

  • the session time limit
  • stop and keep speech when QUILL loses focus
  • intelligent insertion spacing
  • a reset that brings back the one-time first-use hint

Parakeet 3 and silence

Parakeet 3: the most reliable dictation engine, and QUILL prefers it once you install it. Whisper has a well-known flaw for dictation. Give it silence (a pause while you think, a breath before you stop) and it sometimes invents text, most famously a phantom “thank you”. Parakeet 3 only writes words when it actually hears them, so silence produces nothing.

Parakeet 3 is an NVIDIA model. It knows 25 languages, detects the language by itself, runs on the CPU only, and is about a 670 MB download, listed as NVIDIA Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 among QUILL speech models in Manage Speech Models and in Dictation Settings, Speech Models. It is the same download live dictation, QUILL Lite and QUILL Transcribe use, so a model you already downloaded in any of them is used here too, and nothing is downloaded twice. If you downloaded Parakeet 3 in an earlier version of QUILL, it is moved across by itself when its files are the same as today’s download; if they are not, it is listed as not downloaded, and downloading it once more fixes that. Once its model is installed, dictation switches to it automatically. If you have chosen an engine yourself, that choice is always respected, and removing the model simply returns you to Whisper.cpp, or to the built-in Moonshine tiny if Whisper.cpp has no model. Nothing else changes: same keys, same review, same recovery.

Locked Dictation works before you download a model. If Whisper.cpp has no model yet, Locked Dictation uses the Moonshine tiny model that comes with QUILL, the same one live dictation starts with. If you had chosen the older Nemotron engine, QUILL now uses the most accurate model you have; download NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 ASR Streaming 0.6B from the same list to have Nemotron again. None of these models takes an initial prompt; your words in dictation.md and My Words and Phrases still correct the text afterwards.

Silence is trimmed before transcription. QUILL removes the quiet lead-in and tail from each dictation take before the engine hears it. A recording with no speech in it at all gets the “no speech” sound, so no engine gets the chance to make something up. This applies to every engine, Whisper included, and there is nothing to set.

Teach dictation your words

Teach dictation your words. A small file called dictation.md in the family’s dictation folder (see “Where your word list lives”) holds your own vocabulary (so names and jargon come back spelled right), spoken-to-written replacements (such as “new line” becoming a real line break), and spoken command phrases of your own. It applies to Locked Dictation and the Dictate (Offline) toggle.

Your vocabulary is used twice. Engines that accept a hint, such as Whisper, are steered toward your words before they transcribe. Then every engine, including Parakeet 3, which takes no hints, gets a fuzzy correction pass afterwards. It recognises near misses by spelling and by sound, so “charge b” comes back as “ChargeBee” exactly as you wrote it, capitals and all. Names split in two, words run together, and “R&D” heard as “r and d” are all repaired. Ordinary words are left alone.

The Voice Interaction guide (voice-interaction.md), in its section “Teaching dictation your words,” explains the file format and how to choose a speech engine for these offline tools (Whisper.cpp, the QUILL speech models such as Parakeet 3 and Nemotron, or Faster Whisper).

Filler words

Filler words can be removed, but only if you ask. This option is off by default. Turn it on with the dictation_remove_fillers setting, and hesitations like “uh” and “hmm” are taken out of your transcripts. It pays attention to language: “um” is only removed when your dictation language says that is safe, because in Portuguese “um” means “a” or “an”, and in German it means “at” or “around”. When QUILL cannot tell the language, it only removes sounds that are fillers in every language.

Dictate (Offline)

Dictate (Offline) on Tools > Speech is a simple toggle that speaks straight into your document.

  1. Choose it, or press the QUILL key (Ctrl+Shift+Grave) then Shift+D. QUILL plays a start tone and the status bar shows “Dictation listening”.
  2. Speak.
  3. Choose it again to stop. QUILL plays a stop tone, transcribes what you said in the background, and inserts the text at your cursor as one undoable edit. The status bar shows the word count.

Dictation Microphone… on the same menu chooses which microphone this dictation uses, or the system default.

Dictate (Offline) listens with the same offline engine as Locked Dictation, but it needs one of that engine’s models: with Whisper.cpp and no model yet, QUILL offers to open Manage Speech Models so you can download one. With QUILL speech models as the engine, the built-in Moonshine and Whisper are its models, so nothing needs downloading. To change the engine or the model, see “The offline speech tools’ engines”.

Transcribing a recording

When you have a recording rather than a live voice, QUILL can turn it into text in three ways: with Live Dictation’s speech engines (Shift+F5), with the offline engine from Tools > Speech, or with a cloud service from the AI menu. Whichever you use, you get a draft to read and correct, because automatic transcription is never perfect.

Transcribing an audio file

Sometimes the words you want are already in a recording: a voice note from your phone, an interview, a lecture, a meeting somebody recorded for you. Transcribe a Recording listens to it and writes down what was said, using the same speech engines as Live Dictation: the two built in, any you downloaded, or OpenAI with your own key. It works in the background, so you can keep writing while it listens, and it tells you when it is done. It is the same command, with the same window and the same key, in QUILL Lite.

How it differs from the other two ways in this section: it needs nothing downloaded and no ffmpeg, because it uses the models dictation already has and reads recordings with parts of Windows; it can use any speech model you downloaded for dictation; and it writes paragraphs, with times if you want them, straight into a document. Transcribe Audio or Video (Offline) is the one for video files, captions and speaker labels, and AI > Transcribe Audio > Transcribe Audio File is the one for Transcript Actions such as meeting minutes.

Your first transcription, step by step

  1. Press Shift+F5, or choose Tools > Speech > Live Dictation > Transcribe a Recording…. The Transcribe a Recording window opens with the cursor in Recording.
  2. Type the full path of the file, or press Browse… (Alt+B) and choose it. You can also drag a file from File Explorer and drop it on the window.
  3. Check Language spoken in the recording (Alt+L): English or Spanish.
  4. Check Speech model (Alt+M). The most accurate model on your computer is already chosen for you. Just below it, About this model and this recording says what the model is good for, how long the recording is, and about how long it should take on your computer, for example “The recording is 12 minutes long. With NVIDIA Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3, transcribing it should take about 6 minutes on this computer.”
  5. The transcript is not inserted into the document you are editing. When it is ready, its completion notice appears in Notifications; activate that notice when you are ready to open the privately saved transcript.
  6. Press Enter. You hear “Transcribing meeting.mp3 in the background.”
  7. Carry on with whatever you were doing. The status bar shows how far it has got, and every quarter of the way you hear it quietly: “meeting.mp3: 25 percent”. When it is done you hear one sentence, such as “Transcribed meeting.mp3: 12 minutes, 1,804 words, in 6 minutes.” The completion notice stays in Notifications so you can open the result later.

That is all there is to it. The rest of this section explains the choices.

What the text looks like

The text is written the way dictation writes it: full stops, commas, question marks and capital letters put in by the speech engine, with the spacing tidied between sentences. A few more things happen on the way:

  • Paragraphs. A new paragraph starts wherever the recording goes quiet for two seconds or more. If somebody talks for more than two minutes without a long pause, the next pause of any length starts a new paragraph, so you never get one enormous block.

  • Timestamps, if you want them. Timestamps are off unless you turn them on. Check Add timestamps (Alt+T) and every paragraph begins with the hours, minutes and seconds where it was said, like this:

    [00:01:23] The budget for the spring term is nearly final.

    It makes it easy to find a passage again in the recording.

  • Your own words. The corrections in My Words and Phrases are used, so a name the engine always mishears comes out your way.

  • Filler words. If you have Remove filler words turned on in Dictation Settings, the ums and ahs are left out here too.

  • Spoken punctuation. In a recording, somebody who says “new paragraph” or “comma” usually means the words, so they are written as words. If you recorded yourself dictating on purpose, check Obey spoken punctuation and commands (Alt+O): then “comma”, “period”, “new line”, “new paragraph” and “scratch that” work just as they do when you dictate. Commands that move the cursor are ignored, because there is no cursor in a recording.

  • Translation into English. For a recording in Spanish, check Translate into English (Alt+E) and the transcript is written in English instead of Spanish. It needs a model that translates: the built-in Whisper, or a multilingual Whisper you downloaded (not the ones marked English). With any other model, or for an English recording, the box is unavailable. Off unless you check it.

Which model for which recording

The Speech model list shows every model on your computer, most accurate first: the two that come with QUILL, and any you downloaded in Dictation Settings, Speech Models (see “Better accuracy: optional speech models” earlier in this chapter), each with its tier. With your own OpenAI key, OpenAI’s file models are at the end (see below). Speech providers are not in this list; they are for live dictation only. Here is how to choose:

  • Voice notes and dictated letters. One clear voice, close to the microphone. Moonshine tiny is built in, is the fastest by far, and does this well. Whisper tiny is the one to try if Moonshine mishears an accent.
  • Interviews and meetings. Several voices, people interrupting, someone further from the microphone. A downloaded model is worth it here. In English, Parakeet Unified 0.6B is our first choice: in our tests with real recordings it made the fewest mistakes (3.2 percent of words wrong on audiobooks no model was trained on) and it is quick. Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 is close behind (3.8 percent) and quicker still; both Parakeet models never invent words in a silence. Nemotron (5.3 percent) and Whisper small or medium work too, but make more mistakes or are slower.
  • Lectures and talks. One voice, a long time, and plenty of special words. Parakeet again, or a Whisper model. Before you start, add the speaker’s names and terms to My Words and Phrases, and you will spend less time correcting.
  • Noisy rooms. A café, a car, a hall with an echo. On the harder, noisier recordings we tested, Parakeet Unified got 3.2 percent of words wrong, Parakeet TDT v3 5.2 and Nemotron 7.3; the two tiny models got more than 13. OpenAI is very good with noise too, if you use your own key (see below).
  • Spanish. Choose Spanish as the language first. Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 is our first choice: 4.4 percent of words wrong on FLEURS Spanish, where the built-in Whisper tiny got 30.5. Nemotron and the multilingual Whisper models know Spanish too. The list shows only the models that know the language you chose.

A model that is too slow for live dictation is fine for a recording: nobody is waiting at the end of each sentence. The estimate tells you the wait before you start.

How long it takes

How long depends on the model and on your computer. These are our estimates for a one-hour recording, worked out from the speeds we measured for each model on real recordings in October 2026, on a modest computer (two processor cores, 4 GB of memory) and on a more capable one (four or more cores, 8 GB or more):

Model Modest computer Capable computer
Moonshine tiny (built in) 6 minutes 3 minutes
Whisper tiny (built in) 12 minutes 6 minutes
Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 15 minutes 7 minutes
Parakeet Unified 0.6B 18 minutes 8 minutes
Nemotron 27 minutes 12 minutes
Whisper small 1 hour 49 minutes 46 minutes
Whisper large-v3-turbo about 6 hours about 2 and a half hours

For a real example: a three-minute recording took 10 seconds with Moonshine tiny, 20 seconds with Whisper tiny and 25 seconds with Parakeet on the computer QUILL is developed on, plus a few seconds the first time while the model loads.

Transcribing uses two of your processor’s cores, the same as dictation, so your screen reader stays quick. The model is put away a few minutes after the last transcription finishes, which gives its memory back.

Where the text goes

  • The document in front stays untouched. There is no destination picker, and the transcript is not inserted into the text you were editing.
  • Open it when you are ready. QUILL saves the transcript privately and adds a completion notice to Notifications. Activate that notice to open the transcript in the editor. Save the document with Ctrl+S if you want a separate file in a location you choose.

Several recordings, checking on it, and stopping

  • Several at once. In Browse, hold Ctrl and choose more than one file. They are transcribed one after another with the same choices, and each completed recording gets its own notice in Notifications. Activate a notice to open that transcript. If one is already running when you start another, you hear “Added to the queue”.
  • How far has it got? Press Shift+F5 again. At the top of the window, Transcribing now says which recording it is on, how far it has got, and how many are waiting. Press Escape to close the window and let it carry on.
  • Stopping. In that same window, press Stop Transcribing (Alt+S). The recording being transcribed stops, any waiting ones are forgotten, and nothing is written into a document.
  • If you missed what it said. Open Notifications to find and reopen a completed transcript. Shift+F9 opens Activity, which keeps every transcription this session, finished or failed, with the reason when something went wrong.

Which files it can read

MP3, M4A, AAC, WAV, Ogg, Opus, FLAC and WMA, and also the sound in an MP4 file and AIFF. QUILL reads all of them itself, using parts of Windows that are already on your computer, so there is nothing extra to download and no other program to install. Files with copy protection, such as some bought audiobooks, cannot be read.

Privacy, and OpenAI with your own key

With the models on your computer, nothing leaves your computer. The recording is read, not copied, and nothing is kept afterwards except the text.

If you have your own OpenAI key saved in Use My Own AI Key, OpenAI’s speech models appear at the bottom of the list a moment after the window opens. OpenAI is very accurate, especially with several voices, accents and noise, but it sends your recording to OpenAI, so it is never chosen for you, and it is never offered in Safe Mode. When you choose it and press Enter, a question says exactly what will be sent. The answer is No unless you choose Yes, every time. If you agree:

  • the speech in the recording is sent over an encrypted connection to OpenAI, about a minute at a time, with your own key, and the words come back;
  • OpenAI bills your account by the minute of audio;
  • the names in My Words and Phrases are sent as words to expect;
  • nothing goes through QUILL’s servers, and QUILL keeps no copy;
  • OpenAI’s own policies apply to what it receives; you can read them at openai.com/policies.

Please only send recordings you have the right to share, and that the people speaking in them have agreed to. The live model, gpt-live-transcribe, is not in this list: it is made for a microphone, not a file.

Messages you might hear

  • “meeting.mp3 was not found.” The file was moved or renamed. Choose it again with Browse.
  • “meeting.mp3 could not be read as a recording.” It is not one of the formats above, or it is damaged or protected.
  • “meeting.mp3 was read to the end, but no speech was found in it.” The recording is silent, or very quiet. Nothing is written.
  • “… could not be loaded, so this used the built-in engine.” A downloaded model would not load, so Moonshine did the work instead. Removing and downloading the model again in Speech Models may help.
  • “Stopped transcribing meeting.mp3.” You pressed Stop Transcribing.
  • “Could not transcribe meeting.mp3”, followed by the reason, for anything else. You hear the error tone, and the reason stays in Activity.

Transcribe on your computer

QUILL can transcribe entirely on your computer, with no cloud account and without uploading your audio. No AI account or key is required, and you do not need to turn on Artificial Intelligence to use it.

  1. Choose Tools > Speech > Transcribe Audio or Video (Offline)….
  2. Pick an audio or video file.
  3. Choose a transcript format: plain text, Markdown, or HTML.
  4. Keep working while it runs in the background. QUILL tells you when it is done and how many words it produced, and opens the result as a draft document you can edit.

This uses the offline speech tools’ engine: Whisper.cpp, the QUILL speech models or Faster Whisper, whichever you chose in Manage Speech Models, and the built-in Moonshine tiny when none of them is ready. The first time, QUILL may offer to fetch the Whisper.cpp engine and a model for you; nothing downloads unless you agree. “The offline speech tools’ engines”, at the start of this chapter, says which to choose, and the Speech section later in this chapter explains models and engines in full.

Supported formats and FFmpeg

Supported formats. You can pick MP3, M4A, AAC, FLAC, OGG, Opus, WMA, WAV, MP4, M4V, MOV, MKV, WebM, or AVI.

If ffmpeg is installed, QUILL converts the file for you before transcribing, so you never have to make a WAV yourself. QUILL does not ship ffmpeg, but it can fetch it:

  • The Download FFmpeg… button, on the Dictation (Offline) tab of Tools > Speech > Speech and Dictation…, downloads the official build (about 110 MB, with a progress bar you can cancel) and sets it up. It is also “Download FFmpeg” in the Command Palette (Ctrl+Shift+P).
  • Or install it yourself once (for example, winget install Gyan.FFmpeg) and QUILL finds it on your PATH.

ffmpeg is open source (GPL/LGPL) and comes straight from the official builder. QUILL never bundles or redistributes it. Without ffmpeg, the whisper.cpp engine needs a 16 kHz mono WAV, while the Faster Whisper engine can handle the other formats by itself.

Captions and speaker labels

  • Generate Captions (Offline)… on Tools > Speech transcribes a file with timestamps and saves the result as SRT or VTT caption files.
  • Speaker attribution. If you download the “Small English with speaker detection” model (in Manage Speech Models), QUILL marks who is speaking when. Each turn is labelled “Speaker 1”, “Speaker 2”, and so on in the transcript, in bold in Markdown and HTML. It tells the turns apart; it does not name the people.

Transcribe Audio File

If you have AI set up, AI > Transcribe Audio > Transcribe Audio File… sends a recording to OpenAI Whisper in the cloud. The Transcribe Audio submenu is one of the longer AI commands: it appears once AI > Use Artificial Intelligence is on and AI > Show advanced AI features is checked (Chapter 10 explains both).

  1. Choose AI > Transcribe Audio > Transcribe Audio File…. A file picker opens.
  2. Pick the audio file. Supported formats: MP3, MP4, M4A, WAV, WEBM, OGG, FLAC (maximum 25 MB).
  3. Choose your options:
    • Language: detect it automatically, or pick a language for better accuracy.
    • Speaker diarization (uses Deepgram): works out who is speaking when. Each turn is labelled Speaker 1, Speaker 2, and so on, with timestamps. This needs a Deepgram API key set up in AI Hub.
    • Translate to English: transcribes audio in any language and gives you an English translation in one step (Whisper translation mode).

When the transcript is ready, QUILL asks “What would you like me to make of this?” and offers a short list of Transcript Actions, chosen to suit the recording. Pick one and QUILL turns the transcript into a finished document, such as meeting minutes, action items, study notes or a clean draft, and opens it for you to review and edit. Or choose “Just keep the transcript” to go to the viewer, where you can copy it, insert it at the cursor, or open it as a new document. If AI is turned off, you go straight to the viewer.

To get an English translation of a recording, use the Translate to English option in the step above. Earlier versions also had a separate Translate Audio File to English command; it is no longer on any menu.

Transcript Actions

A transcript is rarely what you actually need. You want the minutes, the action items, or a clean draft. Transcript Actions make that last step one choice away. You can reach them in two places:

  • Right after transcription, in the “What would you like me to make of this?” chooser described above.
  • Any time, on text you already have, with AI > Transcribe Audio > Transcript Actions…. Paste in a transcript or notes (or select part of your document) and pick an action.

The built-in actions are:

  • Meeting Minutes: attendees, decisions, action items with owners, follow-ups.
  • Action Items: every task and commitment as a numbered list you can act on.
  • Executive Summary: a short briefing for leadership.
  • Interview Notes: questions, answers, strengths, concerns and an assessment.
  • Study Notes: a lecture or talk turned into organized notes.
  • Q&A Extraction: every question and its answer in clean Q&A format.
  • Clean Up & Draft: spoken rambling turned into a clean, readable draft.
  • Follow-Up Email: a warm, ready-to-send recap with next steps.
  • Key Quotes: the most notable quotes, word for word, with who said them.
  • Decisions Log: just the decisions made, each with its reason and owner.

QUILL puts the most likely ones first for the recording in front of you. A meeting with several speakers starts with Minutes and Action Items; a single voice starts with Clean Up & Draft. Every action is always there, though. The finished document opens in a new window, so your original transcript is never overwritten. Transcript Actions use whichever AI provider you have set up in the AI Hub.

Automate transcription with watch folders

A watch folder lets you set a rule once: drop a file in this folder, and QUILL deals with it. You will find the commands on Tools > Watch Folder.

The Watch Folder menu

  • Watch Folder Monitoring (in Settings)… turns on or off the automatic opening of supported files dropped into a folder you have chosen.
  • Watch Folder Profiles… sets the folder path, whether subfolders count, what happens at startup, and how often the folder is checked.
  • Watch Folder Queue… shows what is happening right now and the settings in use.

Watch folders are good for “drop and open” work: copy supported files into one folder and let QUILL open them in the background. They respect Do Not Disturb and run quietly.

Transcribing arriving audio

Each watch profile runs one action on every file it picks up. As well as opening, moving, copying, converting, running a macro or a sandboxed transform, and OCR, a profile can transcribe arriving audio. Any audio or video file dropped into the folder is transcribed on your machine with the offline speech engine, and a transcript is written next to it. Nothing is uploaded.

Choose the Transcript format in the profile:

  • plain Text (.txt)
  • SubRip captions (.srt)
  • WebVTT captions (.vtt)
  • Markdown (.md)

The caption formats carry timestamps. If the engine returns no timed parts, they fall back to plain text, so you never get an empty caption file. This needs no consent. If no speech model is installed yet, the profile tells you to download one from Manage Speech Models (Tools > Speech > Speech and Dictation…).

There is also a separate Transcribe audio (OpenAI Whisper) action for cloud transcription, once you have turned on AI and set up a key.

Making minutes automatically

A transcribe profile can also chain an AI Action onto each recording:

  1. In Tools > Watch Folder > Watch Folder Profiles…, point a profile at a folder (for example, Meetings).
  2. Choose a transcribe action (offline or OpenAI Whisper).
  3. Under Then make, pick a Transcript Action, such as Meeting Minutes.

From then on, every recording that lands in that folder is transcribed, and the minutes document is written next to it, with a name like standup-meeting-minutes.md. If AI is off or no provider is set up, you still get the transcript. The action step is skipped with a note, never an error.

The Watch Folders page in Settings

  • Enable folder watching by default starts watching your enabled profiles each time QUILL starts. It is off to begin with. Watch Folder Monitoring (in Settings)… brings you to Settings so you can turn it on.
  • Default watch folder: if you just want one folder where anything you drop gets opened, you don’t need a profile. Put the folder here, and QUILL opens each new supported file that lands in it. Blank to begin with.
  • Start watching automatically is the switch that watches your default folder, from the moment QUILL opens and for as long as it runs. It works on its own, so you can watch the default folder without turning on Enable folder watching by default, which starts your profiles. Safe Mode turns both off. Off to begin with.
  • Include subfolders: turn this on and files in folders inside your default folder count too. Off to begin with.
  • Process existing files on start: normally QUILL only opens files that arrive after it starts watching. Turn this on to have it open what’s already waiting in the default folder as well. Off to begin with.

Your profiles each have their own folder and their own Include subfolders, existing files and startup choices; these four only decide for the default folder.

  • Poll interval (seconds) is how often a watched folder is checked, from 2 to 300 seconds. The default is 5.
  • Watch folder: play a sound on each check plays a short tick every time the folder is checked, so silence means the watcher has stopped rather than found nothing. Off to begin with.
  • Watch folder: let results interrupt speech announces a newly opened file straight away, cutting across whatever is being said. Off means the notice waits its turn.

Speech

Tools > Speech is where QUILL keeps its on-device speech tools: the transcription and dictation commands you have already met, and the speech models and engines that make them work. You do not have to understand any of this to dictate. Come here when you want a more accurate model, a lighter one for an older computer, or a different engine.

Manage Speech Models

Tools > Speech > Speech and Dictation… opens Manage Speech Models (the command keeps that name in the Command Palette). It lists local speech models with their download size, accuracy and speed, and helps you pick one that will run well on your computer.

  • The dialog opens with a one-line summary of your machine: its RAM, and whether a GPU was found.
  • Each model shows roughly how much memory it needs. A model too big for your RAM is flagged.
  • Each model names its tier: Lightweight, Balanced or High accuracy, the same three words dictation uses.
  • The best fit for your computer is marked “Recommended for your computer”, followed by why, in the row itself: your memory and graphics card, and what that allows. The guided offline-speech setup shows the same reason below the engine’s description.
  • A larger model warns you when there is no GPU, because it will be slow on the CPU.
  • With QUILL speech models chosen as the engine, five English-only models are no longer in the list: Distil-Whisper small.en, Whisper small.en, Distil-Whisper medium.en, Whisper medium.en and Distil-Whisper large-v3. They were removed in this release. See “Models removed in this release”.

The tiers of the QUILL speech models come from our tests on real recordings; see “How we chose: tested on real speech”.

Choosing what to do. Pick a model, then choose an action: Download this model if it is not installed, or Remove this model from my computer if it is. Before a download starts, QUILL warns you if there is not enough free disk space.

Downloads run in the background and show progress. You can keep working. A progress window shows a percentage and has a Cancel button you can press at any time, and QUILL announces progress as it goes. Cancelling cleans up the partial file. Models come over a secure connection from the Hugging Face Hub and are stored on your computer. Downloading is turned off in Safe Mode.

Manage Speech Models is the Dictation (Offline) tab of the Speech Settings dialog. When more than one local engine is available, it asks which Speech Engine to use. A Set as Default button (or a right-click option) chooses which downloaded model QUILL uses without switching engines. QUILL remembers your choice and uses it for transcription, captions and dictation.

Choosing a speech engine

Choosing a speech engine. This choice is for the offline speech tools: Locked Dictation, Dictate (Offline), Transcribe Audio or Video (Offline), captions and watch folders. Live Dictation has its own engine setting, in Dictation Settings (Alt+Shift+F6). All three engines run entirely on your computer:

  • Whisper.cpp, the default: light and fast, and works on any computer. The engine is a small download (about 8 MB) the first time you need it; see “Getting the offline engine”. Its models download in Manage Speech Models, from Tiny (75 MB) to Large (v3) (about 3.1 GB); Small (465 MB) is the recommended starting point. Only Whisper.cpp has the Small English with speaker detection model, which labels who is speaking. Choose it when you are not sure.
  • QUILL speech models (Parakeet, Nemotron and more): the same models Live Dictation, QUILL Lite and QUILL Transcribe use, in the same folder, so a model you already downloaded for Live Dictation is ready here with nothing more to fetch. It includes the built-in Moonshine tiny, so it works with nothing downloaded. Choose it for Parakeet Unified 0.6B, which made the fewest mistakes in English in our tests, Parakeet 3, which did best in Spanish and never invents words from silence, or Nemotron, which knows English and Spanish. Once Parakeet 3 is installed, QUILL uses this engine by itself unless you chose one yourself. It replaces the separate Parakeet and Nemotron engines of earlier versions.
  • Faster Whisper: the most accurate, a larger download, more memory, and the only engine that can use your graphics card. It installs when you choose it, where your copy of QUILL can install it; otherwise it is not offered. It has its own models. It does not label speakers.

Whisper.cpp and Faster Whisper each have their own models, so download a model after switching to one of them. To switch, choose the Speech Engine on the Dictation (Offline) tab, or run the guided setup described in “Setting up offline dictation”. For a side-by-side comparison with Live Dictation’s engines, see “Which speech engine? A guide to choosing” at the start of this chapter.

Vosk is no longer a dictation engine. Since October 2026, Vosk is used only to work out podcast chapters. It ships with QUILL for that job, because it scored best at it: 0.372 against 0.316 for Whisper medium on hand-made chapter lists, from a 40 MB model, with no download. For dictation and voice commands it was the wrong choice: it knows only English, its last release was in 2022, and its models are checked by MD5 only, where every other download QUILL makes is checked by SHA-256. The built-in Moonshine tiny is faster and more accurate, and needs no download. If you had chosen Vosk for dictation, QUILL now uses your default engine; if you had chosen it for voice commands, QUILL now uses Moonshine tiny. QUILL tells you this once, the first time it starts after the change.

Getting the offline engine

The private, on-device speech engine (whisper.cpp) is not bundled in the installer. The first time you use offline dictation or transcription, QUILL offers to download it for you: about 8 MB, checksum-verified, with a progress bar you can cancel. It is turned off in Safe Mode. If you said no then, fetch it any time from the all-in-one Help > Download Optional Components… list described below. There is no separate menu item for it. You need the engine, but not yet a model, for Locked Dictation: until Whisper.cpp has a model, Locked Dictation uses the built-in Moonshine tiny. Dictate (Offline) and Transcribe Audio or Video need a Whisper.cpp model. To skip all of this, choose QUILL speech models as the engine instead. If you are upgrading from a version that bundled the engine, your existing copy is kept and keeps working, with nothing to download.

The installer does not include it and has no option to add it. In a portable copy you can also place the executable under tools\speech\whispercpp yourself. You do not need to install anything else or change your PATH. Then download a model from Manage Speech Models.

Offline dictation also needs microphone-capture support (the optional sounddevice package). If it is missing, QUILL tells you, and you can still use Windows dictation.

Hugging Face Token

Hugging Face Token… on Tools > Speech is optional. QUILL’s speech models are open source (MIT) and need no Hugging Face account to download. If you fetch a lot of models and run into Hugging Face’s rate limits, a free access token raises them.

The first time you open it, QUILL explains the steps: sign in or sign up at huggingface.co, open Settings > Access Tokens, and create a token with the Read role. It offers to open the token page in your browser, and then you paste the token in. The box hides what you type, and the token is stored in Windows Credential Manager, never in a settings file. Leave the box blank to remove a saved token.

Cloud transcription providers

The offline engines keep everything on your machine. If you also want a cloud provider, for example OpenAI Whisper for its accuracy, install the OpenAI Whisper Transcription Quillin (a bundled extension) and set up an OpenAI API key in AI Hub. It then shows up as a provider on the Dictation (Online) tab of Speech Settings. That tab is kept apart from the offline engines, so local engines (install once) and cloud ones (API key, paid per use) are never mixed in one list. If no cloud provider is installed, the tab tells you how to add one.

Cloud providers are opt-in and never silent:

  • Audio is uploaded only when you choose to transcribe a file with that provider. Never offline, and never in Safe Mode.
  • The watch folder’s offline transcription always uses the on-device engines, so a cloud provider can never upload your audio automatically.
  • An extension that adds a provider contains no code and asks for no network permission. QUILL does the upload itself, through its own checked network path, so the extension never sees your audio or your key.

Developers can read more in the Quillin guide, “Transcription providers”.

The Transcription page in Settings

  • Transcription provider preference is Local first (the default) or Cloud first, for when you have both kinds of provider.
  • Show cloud transcription providers lists cloud providers beside the ones on your computer. On to begin with.
  • Auto-open Status Page on model download opens the Status Page whenever a speech model starts downloading. Off to begin with.
  • Status page refresh announcements is how often the Status Page speaks its updates: Quiet (the default) says nothing on its own, Normal speaks about every thirty seconds, and Verbose about every ten.
  • QUILL Whisperer safe mode lock stops speech-model downloads and retries while leaving the status pages working.
  • Voice commands (push-to-talk) and Voice conversation mode turn on the voice commands described in Chapter 8.
  • Voice recognition engine chooses what listens for voice commands: Follow main engine, whisper.cpp (accurate) or Moonshine tiny (fast, light), which is built in and suits always-listening best.
  • Four settings pace conversation mode, all in milliseconds, and 0 switches each part off: Conversation: pause before a command ends (ms) (2000 to begin with; 0 uses the engine’s own pause), Conversation: cancel window before acting (ms) (900), Conversation: keep listening after a command (ms) (3000) and Conversation: ‘still working’ tick interval (ms) (2000).
  • Conversation: your name (optional) lets QUILL use your name in its prompts, as in “Listening, Sam.” Leave it blank for plain prompts.
  • Conversation: speak prompts aloud speaks the welcome and follow-up prompts with your Read Aloud voice. It is off to begin with, so QUILL does not talk over your screen reader; the sounds and the status bar still tell you what is happening.

Download Optional Components (Help menu)

To keep the installer small, QUILL fetches several large or optional pieces only when you want them. Help > Download Optional Components… is the one place to see, get, test and remove them all.

Everything here is optional. The base app, and the SAPI 5 voices built into Windows, work without any of it, so download only what you need. In portable mode, components install into your portable data folder, so your whole setup travels with you on the drive.

What is in the list

The list puts the most important first:

  • Pandoc: document conversion for Word, ODT, EPUB and RTF.
  • PDF and Office text extraction (MarkItDown, pdfplumber and pypdf): an optional second reader for Word, PowerPoint, Excel and PDF documents. You do not need it: QUILL reads all of these with its own readers, and for Word, Excel, PowerPoint and OpenDocument files QUILL’s own reader goes first, so MarkItDown is asked only when that reader cannot read a file (or when you choose MarkItDown first for Word files). It still goes first for Excel 97-2003 (.xls) files, which QUILL cannot read by itself without LibreOffice. In some builds it cannot run at all (it needs a part the installer leaves out), and then QUILL’s own readers do the work and nothing asks you to download it. Scanned or image-only PDFs still need File > Import > Import / Convert Document (OCR)… either way.
  • The braille translation pack: liblouis tables and BRF profiles for the Translation submenu and for BRF and embossing export.
  • Dictation (offline speech): a guided setup covering Whisper.cpp, QUILL speech models (Parakeet, Nemotron and more) and Faster Whisper, described below.
  • Kokoro and Piper neural voices, and eSpeak NG voices. DECtalk is listed too, but says “Install separately”: QUILL does not distribute it.
  • Audio: export, playback & chapters: FFmpeg for exporting compressed audio, the mpv playback engine, and MP3 audiobook chapter markers, all in one place. Each piece is fetched only when its feature is first used.
  • The MathCAT math-speech engine.
  • Git and the GitHub CLI: portable fallback copies for Tools > Git and GitHub > Local Git and the Codespaces commands on Tools > Git and GitHub. The section “Downloading git and the GitHub CLI”, where this guide covers Git and GitHub, has more.
  • The Node.js runtime, for Node Quillins and the Developer Console’s TypeScript interface. It comes last, as the extra people use least.
  • Any non-English spell-check dictionaries.

Each row shows whether it is Installed or Available to download, and its size. Selecting a row shows a plain description of what it gives you and what it costs.

Download, Test, Remove and Set as Default

  • Download fetches a component you do not have yet, checksum-verified, with its own progress.
  • Test proves an installed component works, so you can trust it before you rely on it. A voice speaks a short sample so you actually hear it. The offline speech engine transcribes a spoken phrase and tells you what it heard. Tools report their version. If a component just needs one more piece, Test takes you straight to it: the guided engine-and-model picker for offline speech, or Manage Voices for a voice engine with no voice yet. An unfinished setup is not treated as an error, and QUILL only offers to send a bug report when a test really fails.
  • Remove deletes QUILL’s downloaded copy of a component and turns its features back off. Removing a voice engine you were using, for example, switches Read Aloud back to the system voice. It only ever removes copies QUILL downloaded, never a system tool or a shared install.
  • Set as Default: for a voice engine (Kokoro, Piper and the other Read Aloud engines), the Set as Default button here makes that engine, and a voice you pick from it, your Read Aloud default without opening another speech dialog. The button is only available for a component that can be a Read Aloud default, and only once it is installed. The same Set as Default is also in Manage Speech Models and Manage Voices (with a right-click option on the list), for the offline dictation model and for voices. Downloading a model or voice engine makes it the default automatically; use these when you want to switch back to one you already have. Voice previews stop cleanly when you move to another before the first one finishes.

Setting up offline dictation

The Dictation (offline speech) row opens a guided setup instead of a plain download. It takes you one step at a time, and a banner at the top always says which step you are on and the one thing to do next:

  1. Pick and install an engine: Whisper.cpp (light and fast), QUILL speech models (the models QUILL Lite and QUILL Transcribe use: Parakeet 3, the most reliable for dictation and described earlier in this chapter, Nemotron, Whisper and Moonshine), or Faster Whisper (can use a graphics card).
  2. Download a model. The best fit for your computer is already selected.
  3. Choose Test dictation to make sure it works, then set it as your default.

QUILL remembers the engine you chose, so the setup reopens where you left off instead of going back to the built-in engine.

Pandoc on demand

Pandoc downloads itself when you need it. You do not have to visit this dialog first. The moment you Import or Export a Word, ODT, EPUB or RTF document (or run Convert File) without Pandoc, QUILL offers to download the official, pinned build right there: about 45 MB, verified, with a progress bar. The conversion is ready as soon as it finishes. Plain text and Markdown never need Pandoc, which is why it is not bundled.

On a Mac

On macOS, several of these come from Homebrew rather than a QUILL download.

  • The braille translation pack download contains a Windows lou_translate program, so on macOS QUILL points you to brew install liblouis instead. QUILL finds a Homebrew liblouis by itself, so no download is needed.
  • Pandoc and Tesseract work the same way. Install them with Homebrew (brew install pandoc, brew install tesseract) and QUILL finds them.
  • LibreOffice, used to import older Office formats and Apple Pages, is found automatically at its standard /Applications/LibreOffice.app location on macOS (it is never on PATH there). Install it from libreoffice.org or with brew install --cask libreoffice if you need those imports.

Performance and Memory

QUILL is built to run all of its AI and speech features on a modest computer with no graphics card and not much memory. It does that by being careful with memory, never by switching features off. Two settings under Settings > Performance and Memory control it:

  • Unload idle models after (minutes) frees the memory used by an AI, read-aloud or dictation model you have stopped using. The next time you need it, QUILL loads it again by itself. Set it to 0 to keep models loaded until you close QUILL.
  • Low-resource mode (one model at a time) keeps only one model loaded at a time and prefers the smallest model that fits. It never turns off AI or speech. It loads them one at a time, so you wait a moment for a reload in exchange for using much less memory. On a machine with very little memory it turns on automatically and tells you once, out loud, so it never takes you by surprise.

You do not have to touch either of these. QUILL picks sensible defaults for your machine, but they are there if you want to tune how much memory it uses.

What you learned, and where to go next

You can now dictate with Live Dictation (Ctrl+F11), pressing it to start and again to stop, or holding it while you talk once you turn that on, with Locked Dictation (Ctrl+F9) or with Dictate (Offline), and teach QUILL your own words in dictation.md. You know that your words go where you started speaking, how to see them while you talk with Nemotron or OpenAI, how to say “correct that”, how to talk to the AI out loud, and what OpenAI dictation sends and how to turn it on or off. You can spell, select, correct and move by voice, put in snippets and Copy Tray slots, switch between English and Spanish with Ctrl+Shift+F11, keep a live transcript with Ctrl+Alt+Shift+PageDown, and dictate into other programs through Quill Inkwell. You can transcribe a recording on your own computer or with AI > Transcribe Audio > Transcribe Audio File…, turn it into minutes or notes with Transcript Actions, and set a watch folder to do all of that for you. You know which speech engine each of these uses, how to choose one for privacy, speed or accuracy, and what a cloud engine sends. You also know where speech models, optional components and the memory settings live.

To practise with QUILL beside you, open Help > Tutorials… (Ctrl+Alt+F1) and try “Write by talking”. The same track, “How much QUILL says”, also has five short dictation lessons: “Punctuation and symbols by voice”, “Fix a word by voice”, “Snippets and clips by voice”, “Switch to Spanish and back” and “Make a live transcript”. Each takes a few minutes, with QUILL telling you what to say. The Dictation commands page and the Voice Interaction guide are the best companions to this chapter.
