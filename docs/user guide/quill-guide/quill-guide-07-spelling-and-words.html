Chapter 7: Spelling and words

This chapter is about getting the words right. You will check spelling a whole document at a time or one word at a time, hear exactly how a misspelled word is spelled, find a better word in the thesaurus, look up what a word means, and count what you have written. Near the end you will also find how to quieten QUILL’s sounds, or choose new ones. Everything here works on your own computer, with no AI and no internet connection, unless you choose otherwise. AI spelling and grammar help is in Chapter 10, AI, when you want it.

Spelling

QUILL checks spelling on your own computer. Nothing you write is uploaded, and no AI provider is involved. The everyday commands are on the Tools menu:

  • Spell Check… (F7) opens the Spelling Review, which walks you through every misspelling.
  • Spell Check Word (Alt+Shift+F7) checks only the word you are on.
  • Next Misspelling (Ctrl+F7) moves straight to the next one in the document, and Ctrl+Shift+F7 goes back to the previous one.

Spelling Review (F7)

Press F7 to start a guided spelling review.

  • What it checks. If you have text selected, QUILL checks only the selection. With nothing selected, it reviews the whole document, starting at your caret.
  • Where you land. The Spelling Review dialog opens with focus in a read-only Context field. The misspelled word is selected inside its sentence, so you hear it in context. You can arrow through it letter by letter or copy the text, just as you would in a read-only document.

The controls, in Tab order, are: Context, Change to, Suggestions, the Review most-frequent words first checkbox, then the buttons Change, Change All, Ignore Once, Ignore All, Read Sentence, Add to Dictionary, Undo Last and Close.

  • Suggestions lists the corrections QUILL has for the word. Arrow through them; choosing one fills Change to for you.
  • Change replaces this occurrence and moves to the next problem. Pressing Enter in the Change to field does the same.
  • Change All replaces every remaining occurrence in the part you are checking, and keeps the capitals as they were (teh becomes the, Teh becomes The, TEH becomes THE).
  • Ignore Once skips this occurrence. Ignore All skips every occurrence, for this session only.
  • Add to Dictionary adds the word to your personal dictionary for good.
  • Undo Last takes back your last spelling action without closing the dialog. It is unavailable until you have done something.
  • Read Sentence (Ctrl+R) reads aloud the whole sentence around the misspelling, so you can hear the word in context without leaving the dialog. The Read Sentence button does the same thing.
  • Alt+W takes you back to Context and selects the current word again, at any point. It is handy after you have been arrowing around the context.

When every problem has been dealt with, QUILL tells you how many words were changed, ignored and added to the dictionary, and puts you back in the document.

Review most-frequent words first

Normally the review goes from top to bottom. Check Review most-frequent words first in the dialog and it walks the misspellings by how often each word turns up instead, with the most common first. It is the same dialog and the same buttons (Change, Change All, Ignore Once, Ignore All, Add to Dictionary, Undo Last); only the order changes.

This is for really messy documents: a rough scan that came through OCR, or a document with one mistake repeated many times. Press Change All on the top word and the order is worked out again straight away, so whatever is now most common among what is left comes to the front. You clear the worst offenders first, without hunting for them in reading order. QUILL remembers the checkbox for next time.

How much the review says

Spelling review announcement verbosity, on the Spelling page of Settings, chooses how much the review tells you:

  • Concise: progress numbers and the result of each action, and nothing else.
  • Balanced (the default): the kind of problem, the current word, your progress and the results.
  • Detailed: all of that, plus hints about the controls and reminders of what you are checking.

Four more settings on the same page belong to the review:

  • Spelling review context display mode: the Context box usually shows the sentence with the misspelling plus the sentences either side. Choose Full paragraph to see the whole paragraph instead; Read Sentence then reads the paragraph.

  • Spell out the misspelled word letter by letter: after telling you the misspelling, QUILL reads it letter by letter. On to begin with.

  • Pause before spelling the word (milliseconds) is how long it waits before spelling starts, from 100 to 3000. The default is 800.

  • Wrap spelling review to the beginning: when the review reaches the end of the document, it offers to carry on from the top back to where you started. On to begin with.

Spell Check Word (Alt+Shift+F7)

If you are used to checking just the word under the cursor in another word processor, Spell Check Word (Alt+Shift+F7) is that. It checks only the word at your cursor and leaves the rest of the document alone.

  • If the word is spelled correctly, QUILL says so and that is all. No dialog opens.
  • If it is misspelled, a small list opens with the same choices as the right-click spelling menu: suggested corrections, Add to Dictionary and Ignore. Pick one and you are back to typing.

Use F7 when you want to work through a whole document or selection. Use Alt+Shift+F7 when you only want to check the one word in front of you.

Moving between misspellings

Sometimes you would rather stay in the document and hop from one problem to the next. Ctrl+F7 moves to the next misspelling and Ctrl+Shift+F7 to the previous one. When you land on one, QUILL says the word and then spells it (see How a misspelling is said, later in this chapter).

QUILLBee’s shortcut: Coming from Word? Alt+F7 also jumps to the next misspelling, just as it does there.

To hear them all in one place, open the Misspelling List (Alt+Shift+L). It lists every misspelling in document order. Arrow through it and press Enter to jump to any one.

Ranked spelling

Tools > Writing and Language > Misspelling List (Ranked by Frequency)… (Alt+Shift+R) shows the same list in a different order. The word that turns up most often comes first, and each entry carries its count, for example “teh (Ln 12, Col 4, 8 occurrences).”

A repeated typo or a single OCR misreading is usually the quickest thing to fix first. Deal with one entry, by correcting it or with Add to Dictionary, and you have dealt with every occurrence of that word at once. Arrow through the list and press Enter to jump to any occurrence, exactly as in the regular list. The document-order list on Alt+Shift+L stays as it was, and it is still the default.

Spell check as you type

With spell check as you type turned on, finishing a word the dictionary does not know plays a soft spelling sound, and the status line shows “Possible misspelling” with the word. The sound comes from your current sound pack; if the pack has no spelling sound, you hear the Windows beep instead. QUILL uses a sound here, not speech, so it never talks over your screen reader in the middle of a sentence.

The check waits until you have finished a word, with a space, a comma or a new line. It does not judge a word you are still typing, so “recie” on the way to “receive” never sets it off.

Web addresses, email addresses, file paths and code are never treated as misspellings. What spell check reads, below, has the details.

You can switch the spelling sound on or off by itself in Tools > Reading and Dictation > Sound Scheme… (look for “Possible misspelling as you type”). The spell check as you type toggle turns the whole live check on or off.

Quiet in code files

In source and configuration files the live check says nothing at all. That covers .py, .json, .js, .css, .ps1, .log, .yml and the other coding file types. Almost every name and setting in a file like that is a word no dictionary knows, so you would hear nothing but wrong alerts.

Markdown is writing, so .md files are checked. Only the code blocks, inline code, web addresses and email addresses inside them are skipped.

The setting is spellcheck_skip_code_files, and it is on by default. Turn it off if you want the live check everywhere, whatever the file type.

F7 is never silenced by this setting. The Spelling Review checks whatever document you run it on, a code file included, reading its strings and comments. Pressing F7 is you asking for a check.

What spell check reads

Spell check reads what you wrote as language, and passes over the rest. This applies everywhere: checking as you type, the F7 review, next and previous misspelling, the list of every misspelling, the context menu and browse mode.

The file’s extension picks one of four rules: writing, Markdown, markup or code. The rule follows the document’s name at the moment you check, so a new document is read as writing, and once you save it as notes.md or script.py the next check uses the Markdown or code rule.

Every rule skips these, wherever they appear:

  • web addresses: https://..., www.example.com, mailto: and file: links, and a bare example.com (a common ending such as .com, .org, .io or .uk, or any domain followed by a path);
  • email addresses;
  • file paths: C:\Users\..., \\server\share, ./notes, ~/notes, /usr/local/bin and docs\guide;
  • file names with an extension people recognise, such as notes.txt, report.docx, song.mp3 or setup.exe.

Before this, checking www.example.com stopped on www, example and com one by one.

Writing. Plain text, a document you have never saved, and any extension not named below. The only things skipped are the ones above. Logs, tables and patches (.log, .csv, .tsv, .diff, .patch) are read this way too, because there is no code in them to find.

Markdown. .md, .markdown, .mdown, .mkd and .mdx. The writing rule, and it also skips:

  • inline code, with any number of backticks;
  • fenced code blocks, between ``` or ~~~ lines;
  • link and image addresses, and the address in a reference line such as [1]: https://...;
  • HTML tags, and an HTML comment on one line.

It still checks link text and image alt text, because someone wrote those to be read.

Markup. .html, .htm, .xhtml, .xml, .xsl, .xslt, .svg, .opml, .rss and .atom. Spell check reads the text between the tags and the comments. It skips the tags, attribute names and attribute values, except the values people read or hear: alt, title, aria-label, aria-description and placeholder. Inside <script> and <style> it reads only the strings and comments, as it would in code.

Code. Only string literals and comments are checked. These extensions use the code rule:

  • C, C++ and other compiled languages: .c, .h, .cc, .cpp, .cxx, .hpp, .hxx, C# (.cs), Java, Go, Rust (.rs), Swift, Objective-C (.m, .mm), Kotlin (.kt, .kts), Scala, D (.d), Pascal (.pas), Fortran (.f, .f90) and assembly (.asm, .s);
  • scripting: Python (.py, .pyi, .pyw), Ruby, Perl (.pl, .pm), PHP, Lua, Tcl, R, Julia (.jl), Dart, Groovy, Visual Basic and VBScript (.vb, .vbs);
  • the web: JavaScript (.js, .jsx, .mjs, .cjs), TypeScript (.ts, .tsx), Vue, Svelte, and CSS with .scss, .sass, .less and .styl;
  • shells and builds: .sh, .bash, .zsh, .fish, PowerShell (.ps1, .psm1, .psd1), batch files (.bat, .cmd), makefiles (.mk), CMake, Gradle, Inno Setup (.iss) and .spec;
  • settings and data: JSON (.json, .jsonc, .json5), YAML (.yaml, .yml), TOML, .ini, .cfg, .conf, .properties, .env, .plist, .reg, .lock, .map, SQL, GraphQL (.graphql, .gql) and Protocol Buffers (.proto).

Inside a string or a comment it still skips:

  • placeholders: {0}, {name}, {0:N2}, {{ and }}, %s, %d, %(name)s, ${x}, $name and $(name);
  • escapes such as \n, \t, \x41 and \u00e9;
  • words shaped like names in code: snake_case, any word with a digit in it, camelCase, and names like HTTPServer;
  • the quote marks, and a string’s prefix such as the f in f"...", so a misspelled word in single quotes is reported without a quote stuck to it.

How code and markup are read. QUILL uses Pygments, which knows over five hundred languages, to find the strings, comments and text. With Pygments it also passes over regular expressions, single characters, the interpolated parts of a string, preprocessor lines such as #include, and the #! line at the top of a script. Pygments loads the first time you check a code or markup file, so it costs nothing when QUILL starts.

A simpler built-in reader takes over when Pygments cannot load, when it has no reader for an extension, or when the file is over 100,000 characters, where Pygments would be slow. It knows the comments and strings of these families:

  • # comments: Python, Ruby, Perl, the shells, PowerShell, R, Julia, Tcl, YAML, TOML, CMake, makefiles, .conf, .properties, .env, GraphQL and .spec;
  • CSS and its relatives; SQL, with -- comments; Lua; JSON, including // and /* */ comments;
  • Visual Basic and VBScript; batch files (rem and ::); .ini, .cfg, .reg and .iss (; and # comments);
  • everything else in the code list is read the C way: // and /* */ comments and strings in double quotes, single quotes or backticks.

For markup, the built-in reader reads the text, comments and the attributes above, reads <script> and <style> as code, and skips character entities such as &amp;. It is less precise than Pygments, but it is never silent.

Spell check a document before saving

Turn on Settings > Editing > Spell check a document before saving (off by default) and QUILL opens the Spelling Review for you each time you save, so you can fix misspellings before the file is written. Fix or skip them as usual; the save then goes ahead with your corrections. This applies to Save and Save As for the document you are working on.

Spell-check language

Tools > Writing and Language > Spell Check Language… chooses the language the spell checker uses. It affects both the F7 review and the check as you type.

English (United States) is built in and works offline straight away. Other languages are downloaded when you first choose them. Pick Spanish (Spain) or French (France), for example, and QUILL fetches that dictionary from its own verified source. The download is checksum-checked, and a progress window lets you cancel it. After that the language works offline, just like English.

QUILL remembers your choice between sessions. If you are offline, or you cancel the download, English keeps working and nothing else changes. The downloaded dictionaries are kept in your QUILL data folder, so reinstalling or upgrading QUILL never makes you fetch them again.

How a misspelling is said

Speech alone cannot tell you what is wrong with a misspelled word. “receive” and “recieve” sound the same, so hearing the word tells you nothing. The letters are the answer. So when you land on a misspelling, with Next or Previous Misspelling or in the F7 review, QUILL says the word, pauses, and then spells it.

The spelling comes as a second, separate announcement, so you can cut it off. Press your next key and the spelling is cancelled before you hear it. If you are moving fast you lose nothing, and if you want the letters they are there.

Twelve settings under Spelling in Settings control all of this. Both editors also gather them in a window of their own. In QUILL it is Spelling Announcements, on Ctrl+Alt+Shift+F6, or type its name in the command palette (Ctrl+Shift+P); it has no menu item in QUILL. In QUILL Lite it is Announcements…, on the Spelling submenu of its Tools menu. They are the same twelve settings in the same file, so setting them up once sets up both editors.

While you are typing

  • Play a sound when you type a misspelling (spelling_alert_sound) plays the falling blip when you finish a misspelled word. Turn it off for silence.
  • Also say the word when you type a misspelling (spelling_alert_speech) speaks the word as well. It is off by default, because being interrupted while you are writing costs more than it tells you.
  • Shortest gap between two alerts for the same word (milliseconds) (spelling_alert_repeat_ms) is the shortest time before the same word is reported again, so one stubborn name does not become a drumbeat. Zero means every time. The default is 750.

Whichever way you set those two, the status bar always shows the word, for example “Possible misspelling: teest”, so you can read it with your screen reader whenever you like. QUILL does not speak that status line on its own; it is spoken only when Also say the word when you type a misspelling is on, which is what the setting means in QUILL Lite too.

Spelling a word out

  • Spell misspelled words out letter by letter (spell_aloud_enabled) is the main switch.
  • How letters are spoken (spell_aloud_style) chooses how the letters are said. Plain Letters (R, E, C) are fastest. The Phonetic alphabet (romeo, echo, charlie) is clear where B, D, E, P, T and V all sound alike. Letters, then the phonetic alphabet is for learning a word rather than checking one.
  • Say which letters are capitals (spell_aloud_capitals) says “cap” before a capital letter, so you can tell MacDonald from Macdonald. It is worth having, because letters are always spoken as capitals, whatever the word does (several voices read a single lower-case letter as a word).

When the letters follow

There are three pauses, because the right wait is different in each place.

  • Pause before spelling a word (milliseconds) (spell_aloud_delay_ms) is the pause in the F7 review, where you have stopped to decide. The default is 800 ms.
  • Spell the word when you move to a misspelling (spell_aloud_on_navigation) and Pause before spelling when moving between misspellings (milliseconds) (spell_aloud_navigation_delay_ms) cover Next and Previous Misspelling. There you may just be passing through, so the default pause is shorter, 600 ms.
  • Spell each suggestion as you arrow through them (spell_aloud_suggestions) and Pause before spelling a suggestion (milliseconds) (spell_aloud_suggestion_delay_ms) spell whichever correction you arrow onto in the suggestions. Choosing between two spellings by ear is as hard in a list as it is in the document.
  • Spell the first suggestion on arrival (spell_aloud_first_suggestion) also spells the top suggestion when the review reaches a new word. It is off by default, because it doubles what you hear each time you arrive.

The thesaurus, Look Up and other word tools

The rest of the writing and language commands on the Tools menu are about words themselves: finding a better one, finding out what one means, and counting them. Chapter 4, Writing and editing, gives a short introduction; this is the fuller story.

Thesaurus (Shift+F7)

Tools > Writing and Language > Thesaurus… (Shift+F7) opens a two-pane picker for the word you are on. It finds the word even when it is in another form, so “running” finds “run”, and when you choose a replacement it goes back in the form your sentence needs.

Look Up Word (Alt+F10)

Look Up Word… (Alt+F10) is a dictionary with no AI. Out of the box it uses the thesaurus, which works offline. Check Use online sources in the window and it also brings definitions, more words, and a Wikipedia summary from three free online services. Nothing goes online until you check that box.

Say Word Summary and Dictionary Status

  • Press Ctrl+Alt+Shift+[ for Say Word Summary, which says what the thesaurus knows about the word you are on, without opening a window. There is no menu item for it; the key or the Command Palette (Ctrl+Shift+P) reaches it.
  • Dictionary Status… (Alt+Shift+;) shows which spelling engine QUILL is using, how many words are in your personal, document and project dictionaries, and whether the thesaurus is installed.

Document Statistics (Ctrl+Shift+G)

Tools > Writing and Language > Document Statistics… (Ctrl+Shift+G) gives you the number of words, characters, lines and paragraphs, and the reading time. It used to be called Word Count. It now has the same name and key as in Word and QUILL Lite.

Sounds, and turning them off

QUILL plays short sounds, called earcons, for things your screen reader does not mention, like a misspelling or a paste. Here is how to silence them all at once, change one, or build a set of your own.

Where QUILL’s sounds play, and turning them off

QUILL makes short sounds of its own. You choose where they play, and you can turn them off.

  • Alt+Shift+M turns QUILL’s sounds off. Press it again and they come back, on the same sound card as before. You hear a short sound as they go off, and another where they now play when they come back on, and both are spoken too. Tools > Reading and Dictation > App Sounds has a check mark that shows whether they are on.
  • The App Sounds Play On… button in Tools > Reading and Dictation > Sound Scheme… (Ctrl+Alt+Shift+O) lets you pick where they play:
    • System default. This is where it starts. The sounds go where Windows sends sound.
    • None (no sounds). QUILL makes no sounds at all. Everything it tells you is still spoken.
    • Any sound card or headset in the list. The sounds get a device of their own.

After you choose, one sound plays on the new device, so you know straight away where it went.

If the device you chose is unplugged or asleep, you still hear every sound, on the system default, and QUILL tells you once. When the device comes back, the sounds go back to it by themselves.

This changes QUILL only. Your other QUILL apps keep their own choice, unless you have checked “Share these choices with my other Quill apps”, which copies a change here to the others that share. Your screen reader is never moved. Until October 2026, Alt+Shift+M silenced QUILL and QUILL Lite together. Now every QUILL app has its own, on the same key; to silence all of them at once, turn off Sound notifications in Settings.

The Sound Scheme window

Tools > Reading and Dictation > Sound Scheme… (Ctrl+Alt+Shift+O) lists every sound QUILL can make, and lets you hear them.

As you arrow through the list, each event plays as you land on it. That turns a list of about a hundred and forty sound names into something you can browse by ear. It is a checkbox under the list, so if you are hunting for one row you can turn it off and pass the rest in silence.

Each row tells you everything about itself: its group, the event, whether it is switched on, which file it plays and how long that file is. Your screen reader reads it all as you arrive, so you never have to Tab away to find out. The buttons underneath work on the row you are on:

Button What it does
Play Play this event now, even when the event is switched off
Switched on Silence this one event without changing which sound it has
Browse… Use a WAV file of your own
No Sound Remove the sound from this event entirely
Use Default Put this one event back to the sound the scheme ships

QUILL refuses a file that is not a WAV, is longer than ten seconds, or is bigger than two megabytes, and tells you why when you choose it. Better to hear about it then than to have the event go silent later with no clue why.

  • Save As Scheme saves the whole set under a name you choose, as an ordinary folder with the sounds copied into it. It keeps working after you tidy your Downloads folder, and you can zip it up and send it to a friend.
  • Delete Scheme removes one of your own schemes. The schemes that come with QUILL cannot be deleted.
  • Restore All Defaults puts every event back and switches them all on. It always works completely, because QUILL never overwrites the sounds it ships.

QUILL Lite opens the same window over the same schemes, so a scheme you make in one editor is offered in the other.

The Scheme list at the top also offers VS Code Signals: the sounds Visual Studio Code plays for its own accessibility signals, used under the MIT licence. They cover errors, warnings, saving, finished and failed tasks, AI requests and answers, dictation and recording starting and stopping, and undo and redo. Anything they have no sound for stays quiet, or is spoken where QUILL speaks instead. Choose it in one app and the rest of the family plays it too, each from the next time it starts.

The sounds themselves

The Ink pack that comes with QUILL has a sound for every event. That includes the ones desktop programs have had for decades: open, close, cut, copy, paste, delete, undo, redo, print, information, question and task finished. Those are the moments a screen reader says nothing about; nothing is announced when a paste lands, for example.

Related sounds share a tone and differ by direction, so you learn a family once instead of one sound at a time.

  • Undo and redo are the same figure, played backwards from each other.
  • Open and close are one pair of bells, rising and falling. App start and app exit are the same pair, one note longer.
  • Cut, copy and paste are one dry wooden tick in three gestures. Delete is that tick dropped an octave and a half, with a breath of noise underneath.
  • Nothing to undo does not move in pitch at all, because nothing moved.

Starting with a blank document, or not

When you start QUILL without opening anything, it gives you an empty Untitled document, as most editors do. If you always open an existing file and would rather not close a blank one every time, turn off open_blank_document_at_startup, under General in Settings.

Turning it off changes only that one case. A file you double-clicked, a file named on the command line, the documents from your last session and recovered unsaved work all still open. QUILL Lite has the same setting, with the same name, in Preferences.

Press Ctrl+Alt+F9 for Add Word to Dictionary. It has no row on the menu bar: use the key, the Command Palette (Ctrl+Shift+P), or Add to Dictionary in the spelling choices for a misspelled word.

What you learned, and where to go next

You can now check a whole document with F7, or one word with Alt+Shift+F7, and hop between misspellings with Ctrl+F7. You know how to put the most common mistakes first, how QUILL spells a word out and how to tune that, and how to pick a spell-check language. You met the thesaurus, Look Up Word and Document Statistics, and you can silence QUILL’s sounds with Alt+Shift+M or change them in the Sound Scheme window.

To practise with QUILL open, try the lesson “Spelling, words, and the thesaurus” in Help > Tutorials… (Ctrl+Alt+F1).
