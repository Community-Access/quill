Why dictation recommends what it does: the evidence

QUILL, QUILL Lite and QUILL Transcribe suggest a speech model for your computer and your language. This page shows the measurements behind every suggestion, how we made them, and how we reasoned from them, so you can judge them for yourself.

Read this first: your results will differ

Every figure on this page was measured on one fast laptop, with people reading aloud from recordings, on two days in October 2026. Your own results will be different, and they may be very different. How well dictation hears you depends on things no test can stand in for:

  • Your microphone, how close it is, and how its level is set. A level set very low can make dictation miss speech altogether; we saw it happen, as the section on very quiet speech explains.
  • Your room: background noise, echo, a fan, a television, other people talking.
  • Your voice: accent, speed, clarity, and any speech difference or disability. Every recording here is someone reading aloud from a book or a script, which is easier to recognise than everyday speech.
  • Your words: names, technical terms and specialist vocabulary are harder than the plain prose most of these recordings contain.
  • Your computer: its processor, its memory, what else is running (above all your screen reader), and whether it is on battery.

So treat a recommendation as a good place to start, not a promise. If a model mishears you, try another: every model can be chosen, downloaded and removed in the model list, and switching changes nothing else.

How we tested

A few words used on this page

  • Core: a processor has several cores, and each one does one job at a time. A computer with four cores can do four things at once.
  • Thread: one line of work on one core. Dictation is allowed a quarter of one core's time, so that your screen reader stays quick.
  • Held-out audiobooks: recordings published after every model was built, so no model could have practised on them. They are the fairest test.
  • Memory: the computer's working memory (RAM), measured in gigabytes (GB) and megabytes (MB).

The measures

  • Words wrong: the share of the words that were said that the model got wrong, left out or added, ignoring capitals and punctuation (and, for Spanish, accents). Lower is better.
  • Processing time: seconds of computing for each second of speech, on one thread. Lower is faster. Dictation's limit is 0.25: a quarter of one core. Because computers differ so much, the apps time the built-in Moonshine tiny on your own computer for two seconds and work out every model's processing time from that.
  • Most memory used: the most memory the model needed on any set, because a model must fit the worst minute, not the average one.

The computer and the method

One laptop with an Intel Core Ultra 7 265H processor, 16 cores and 64 GB of memory, running Windows 11. That is much faster than the computers the advice is written for, which is why the apps compare each model with Moonshine tiny rather than using these seconds directly. Each model ran on the processor alone, one recording at a time, the way dictation hands over a phrase, and separately from every other model so that its memory figure is its own. The test program, called dictbench, is part of QUILL's source code, and anyone can run it.

Words while you speak: Nemotron live

Nemotron is built to recognise speech while you are still talking, and that is how dictation runs it. Our first test handed it whole phrases instead, which might have undersold it, so on 23 October we played the same recordings at real speed through dictation itself. The last column is how long you wait for your words after you stop talking: for 95 phrases in 100, they appeared within that time.

QUILL's Locked Dictation: whisper.cpp

QUILL also has Locked Dictation, which records first and writes the words afterwards. It uses whisper.cpp, another speech engine: a separate program that runs Whisper models, with its "base" model unless you choose another. We measured it the way QUILL runs it, against the same Whisper base model run by the engine the rest of dictation uses. The "words appear" column is the average wait after a phrase.

How we got from numbers to a recommendation

The apps apply the same steps, in this order, on your own computer:

  1. It must speak your language. For Spanish, only models we measured in Spanish are considered: a recommendation needs a number behind it.
  2. It must fit beside your screen reader. Each model's memory is compared with what your computer can spare: about 1.6 GB on a computer with 8 GB, less on 4 GB, and always leaving at least half a gigabyte of what is free right now.
  3. It must keep up with you. The app times a small built-in model on your computer for two seconds, works out from that how hard each model would make your computer work, and leaves out any model that would fall behind your speech or take more of the computer than your screen reader can spare.
  4. Of what is left, the fewest mistakes wins: by the held-out audiobooks for English, or by the two Spanish sets for Spanish. If you choose the quickest response, the quickest wins instead.
  5. On a modest computer, a tie goes to what is built in, so nothing needs downloading where every megabyte counts.

Two consequences are worth saying plainly. First, the recommendation is about this computer, now: open the model list after closing a large program and it may suggest something bigger. Second, it is only as good as the measurements: the figures above are averages over many people reading aloud, and the person who matters is you.

A limit we found: very quiet speech

Many of Google's FLEURS recordings are published very quietly, far quieter than a microphone at a normal setting. Played at real speed through dictation, nothing at all was written for 17 of 200 of them. The part of dictation that decides when you have started speaking never heard them start, mostly when a quiet recording came straight after a louder one. Brought up to a normal level, every one of them was heard.

What this means for you: if your microphone's level is set very low, or you speak very softly or far from it, dictation may miss what you say, especially just after something louder. Raising the microphone level in Windows' sound settings, or moving closer, is the first thing to try. We are working on dictation handling quiet input itself; until then, this is a real limit, and we would rather tell you than have you find it.

Models we removed, and why

Five English-only models were removed in October 2026: Distil-Whisper small.en, Whisper small.en, Distil-Whisper medium.en, Whisper medium.en and Distil-Whisper large-v3. On the held-out audiobooks they got 3.6% to 6.3% of words wrong, against Parakeet Unified's 3.2%, and each was slower and needed as much memory or more. Keeping them offered only a slower or heavier way to a worse result. Anyone using one was moved to Parakeet Unified if it was already downloaded, or otherwise to the built-in model, and told once; nothing was downloaded and no file was deleted.

How we keep checking

Numbers go stale. Ten of the held-out audiobook recordings, six English and four Spanish from ten different readers, are kept with QUILL's source code. Every week an automatic check runs every model the advice can recommend on them, and compares each one's speed with Moonshine tiny's on the same computer. A model that has become much slower or much less accurate than its published figures fails the check. Another check runs with every change to QUILL and confirms that, on 72 kinds of computer, in both languages, the advice never recommends a model that would break its own speed and memory rules.

Its first runs found two things. First, how much longer a model takes than Moonshine tiny depends on the processor: on GitHub's test computers the two Parakeet models took six to seven times as long as Moonshine tiny, against about four on our laptop. So the advice, which scales from Moonshine tiny on your own computer, can underestimate a bigger model on some processors. Second, on a dictated sentence of six or seven seconds, the built-in Whisper tiny takes about four and a half times as long as Moonshine tiny, not the 2.6 times we measured on longer recordings, because Whisper always works on a 30-second window. Every model takes a little longer per second on short phrases. Re-measuring them all at the length of a dictated sentence is the next step for the advice, and the result will appear here.

What we have not measured yet

  • A real modest computer with a screen reader running. Speed, memory, screen-reader lag and battery on a 4 GB Windows 10 computer with NVDA or JAWS still need a person at the keyboard.
  • Everyday speech. Every recording here is someone reading. Natural, hesitant or conversational speech is harder.
  • Speech differences and disabilities. We have not yet measured how each model does with dysarthria, stammering or other speech differences. If you would contribute recordings, please write to us.
  • Noise and microphones. Noisy rooms and a range of real microphones, including the quiet-speech limit above.
  • Spanish questions and exclamations. Whether Whisper writes the opening upside-down question and exclamation marks (¿ and ¡) itself needs recordings from a native speaker.
  • Whisper medium and large-v3-turbo were too slow to re-run with real speech.

The data and the tools

Every number on this page comes from two engineering records published with QUILL's source code on GitHub, each with a data file holding every result: the real-speech test of 22 October 2026 (on GitHub) and the live and whisper.cpp tests of 23 October 2026 (on GitHub). The test program and the scripts that fetch each set of recordings, with their licences and checksums, are in the same place, so anyone can repeat the tests on their own computer. For how to choose and change models, see the dictation guide.

QUILL stands for Quality, Usable, Inclusive, Lightweight, Literate. It is a writing program for Windows and macOS, made for people who use screen readers, by people who use them too.
