Chapter 4: Moving around a document

A long document is easy to get lost in when you cannot glance down a page. This chapter gives you the keys that take you straight to what you want. You will search for words and hear how many there are, look for characters you cannot type, jump to a line, a bookmark or a heading, and leave bookmarks that are still there tomorrow. And whenever you jump somewhere, there is a key that takes you back.

Finding things

These are the keys you will use most when you are looking for something:

Key What it does
Ctrl+F Find
F3 / Shift+F3 Find next / previous
Ctrl+H Replace
Ctrl+G Go To: a line, a page, a bookmark, a heading or a PDF’s outline
Ctrl+Alt+Shift+F8 Find in Open Documents: every open window at once
Ctrl+Alt+Shift+F Search in Files: a whole folder
Ctrl+Shift+R Replace Across Files

All seven are rows of the Edit menu itself, so if you forget a key, press Alt+E and arrow down: after Replace you hear Find in Open Documents…, Search in Files… and Replace Across Files…, each with its key. The two that answer “how many” and “where in this document”, All Matches… and Count Occurrences, are one step further in, on Edit > Matches.

When a search reaches the end of the document, it goes back to the top and keeps looking, and it tells you it has done so. That little message is worth listening for. It is how you know a document has only one match, and not none.

What is in the Find and Replace windows

The Find window (Ctrl+F) opens with your cursor in the box where you type what to look for. If you had something selected, it is already there; if not, your last search is. Tab past the box and you meet two checkboxes:

  • Match case: when it is checked, Cat and cat count as different words.
  • Whole word only: when it is checked, cat finds the word on its own and not the start of catalogue.

After those come the Search mode (more about that below) and the buttons. Find next is the one Enter presses, and Shift+Enter in the box finds the previous match, just like the Find previous button. Close, or Escape, puts the window away, and F3 still remembers what you were looking for.

The Replace window (Ctrl+H) has the same box and checkboxes, plus a Replace with box for what each match should become. Leave it empty to delete the matches. Its buttons are:

  • Find next, which moves to the next match without changing anything.
  • Replace, which changes the match you are on and moves on to the next one, so you can decide one at a time.
  • Replace all, which changes every match in the document at once and tells you how many it changed.
  • Close. Anything you have already replaced stays replaced, and Ctrl+Z takes it back if you change your mind.

Both windows stay open while you work, so you can go back to your document and return to them.

How many are there?

The Find window counts as you type. Type three letters and it tells you how many matches there are. Type a fourth and the number changes. Your screen reader picks up the new count on its own, so you do not have to ask.

Ctrl+Down and Ctrl+Up step through the matches while you stay in the search box. You can hear each one and still change what you typed. When you have the one you want, press Enter and you are back in your document, right on it.

You do not have to open the window to count. Count Occurrences (Ctrl+Alt+Shift+F3) counts your current search from anywhere in the document.

All Matches (Ctrl+Shift+F3) shows every match in one list, in order, each with its line, its column and the words around it. Open it before you press Replace All. It lets you see everything you are about to change before you change it.

Find and replace in all your open documents

Sometimes the thing you are looking for is in one of a dozen documents you have open, or you need to change a name in all of them. Find in Open Documents… (Ctrl+Alt+Shift+F8, on the Edit menu under Replace) searches every document QUILL Lite has open, not just the one in front of you.

It searches the text exactly as it is in each window, so it finds:

  • changes you have not saved yet,
  • new documents you have never saved,
  • formatted documents, such as Word and RTF files, searched as you see them.

Anything that is not open is left alone, and it never writes to a file on disk. If you want to search files that are saved in a folder instead, that is Search in Files, described next.

The window has the same choices as Find and Replace:

  • Find what and Replace with.
  • Search mode: Normal, Extended or Regular expression, just as in Find.
  • Match case, Whole word only, and Wrap around, which is checked when it opens. With it checked, the search goes on from the first document after the last one, and comes back to where you started.

Its buttons:

  • Find Next (Enter) and Find Previous go to the next or previous match. When one document has no more, they move on to the next document and bring it to the front, and you hear its name and the line: “Chapter 3.txt, line 12: She opened the door.” While you stay in one document you only hear the line, the way Find works. If the search goes past the last document and starts again from the first, you hear “Back to the start” first.
  • Find All lists every match in every open document, in order, each with the document’s name, the line number and the line. Arrow through it and press Enter to go straight to that match. It is the quickest way to check what Replace All is about to change.
  • Replace All does nothing until you have heard what it found, for example: “312 matches in 87 of 104 open documents. Replace them all?” Press Yes to go ahead.
    • Each document is changed on its own, so Ctrl+Z in any one window takes back just that document’s replacements. In a formatted document each replacement is its own undo step, which keeps the formatting around it exactly as it was.
    • Nothing is saved for you. Every changed document is marked as changed, and you save them when you are ready, one at a time or with Save All.
    • Documents that cannot be changed, such as a read-only file or a report QUILL wrote for you, are still searched, but Replace All leaves them alone and tells you how many it skipped.
  • Close (Escape) puts the window away.

After you close it, F3 and Shift+F3 keep searching across all your open documents, so you can work through them one match at a time without opening the window again. As soon as you start an ordinary Find (Ctrl+F) or Replace (Ctrl+H), F3 goes back to searching just the document you are in.

QUILL and QUILL Lite share this window and work the same way, and it is in the same place in both: the Edit menu, under Replace. The key differs by one chord: QUILL uses Ctrl+Shift+F10, because in QUILL Ctrl+Alt+Shift+F8 opens Quill Weather.

Searching a whole folder of files

Find in Open Documents works on what you have open. When the files you want are saved in a folder and you would rather not open them all, use the folder search, the same one QUILL has:

  • Search in Files… (Ctrl+Alt+Shift+F, on the Edit menu, just below Find in Open Documents; Alt+E, then S) looks through every file in a folder, and every folder inside it.
  • Replace Across Files… (Ctrl+Shift+R, the row below it) changes every match in those files, on disk.

Both are on the same keys and in the same place in QUILL. In a new install they are there from the start. If you chose the Notepad or WordPad profile in Tools > Customize Features… (Ctrl+Alt+F10), they are switched off with the rest of the Matches and wider searches area, because neither program had them; check that box there to bring all five back.

An example. You remember writing to somebody called Priya about a deadline, but not which of the forty letters in your Letters folder it was in. Open any document from that folder, press Ctrl+Alt+Shift+F, leave Starting folder as it is, type *.txt in File pattern and Priya in Search text, and press Enter. When the search finishes QUILL Lite tells you, for example, “3 matches in 2 of 40 files”, and a new window lists each file with the matching lines. Press Ctrl+O and type the file name it gave you to open the one you want.

Both open the same window, which asks for:

  • Starting folder: where to look. It starts at the folder of the document you are in.
  • File pattern: which files, such as *.txt or *.md. A star on its own means every file.
  • Search text, and for Replace Across Files, Replacement.
  • Match mode: Plain text, Wildcard (* and ?) or Regular expression.
  • Output format: how the results are written out, from just the file names to every matching line.
  • Case sensitive and Whole word.

The search runs in the background, so QUILL Lite stays usable while it works, even on a big folder. When it finishes you hear how many matches it found in how many files, and the results open in a new untitled window. Read it with your usual keys, and save it if you want to keep it.

Replace Across Files changes the files on disk, so it is careful:

  • Preview before replacing is checked when the window opens. With it checked, every change it would make opens in a new window first, and you are asked “Replace 312 matches in 87 files?” before anything is written. Answer No and nothing changes.
  • When it has finished, a report of what it changed opens in a new window.
  • Files you have open in QUILL Lite are changed on disk, not in the window. If one of them is open, QUILL Lite notices the file changed and offers to reload it.

The first time you use Replace Across Files, try it on a copy of the folder.

Searching for things you cannot type

The Find and Replace windows have a Search mode with three choices.

Normal text

This is the everyday one. What you type is what it looks for, punctuation and all.

Special characters

This lets you search for characters that have no key. \t is a tab, \n is a line break, \u2014 is an em dash, and \N{NO-BREAK SPACE} is exactly what it says.

It pairs well with Describe Character. Say Describe Character tells you that you are standing on a no-break space. Special characters mode then lets you find every other one, or replace them all with an ordinary space.

Regular expression

This treats what you type as a search pattern. A . matches any character, * repeats the thing before it, [abc] matches any one of those letters, and ^ and $ are the start and end of a line.

If your pattern has a mistake in it, QUILL Lite tells you which character is wrong. That way you know the difference between “my pattern is broken” and “there really is nothing to find”.

Whole word, in every mode

Whole word works with all three modes. In regular expression mode it applies to the whole pattern, so cat|dog finds the word cat or the word dog, and does not find “dog” in the middle of “hotdogs”.

Going somewhere

Press Ctrl+G to open Go To. It works like Word’s Go To: one window, and you choose what to go to.

  1. Press Ctrl+G.
  2. At the top, arrow to Line, Bookmark or Heading. A PDF adds Page, and Outline too if the PDF has one.
  3. The rest of the window changes to match. For a line you get a number box. For everything else you get a list. Bookmarks start with their number, headings with their level, and outline entries with their title, so you can pick one out by its first word.
  4. Press Enter to go there.

If you already know which kind you want, there are quicker keys. Alt+Shift+G goes straight to the bookmark list, and Ctrl+Alt+L goes straight to the heading list. Ctrl+G is the one to press when you are not sure.

Making a table

Insert > Table… (Ctrl+Alt+T, the same key as in QUILL) asks three questions: how many columns (1 to 12), how many rows (1 to 50), and whether the first row is a header row. Then it puts the table on lines of its own and leaves the cursor in the first cell, ready to type.

What you get depends on the kind of document:

  • In a rich text document (Alt+Shift+F switches to it), the table is a real table: a grid on screen, with no | characters anywhere, which your screen reader, Word and WordPad all see as a table. Its first row is the header row when you asked for one. Save the document as .rtf or as a Word document and it stays a real table, the header row marked so a screen reader in Word announces the column headers.
  • In a Markdown or plain text document, the table is rows of cells divided by |, the shape described below.
  • In an HTML document, the table is a real <table>, with its header cells marked so a screen reader in a browser announces each cell’s column.

Either way the table keys work on it at once.

Moving through a table

Navigate > Table works on every table QUILL Lite shows: a real table in rich text (every table you make there, and every table in a Word or RTF document you open as rich text), and rows of cells divided by | in Markdown and plain text. It reads one cell at a time and tells you where you are, on the same keys as QUILL:

Key Command What it does
Ctrl+Alt+Right Next Cell The next cell in the row
Ctrl+Alt+Left Previous Cell The previous cell in the row
Ctrl+Alt+Down Cell Below The cell below, in the same column
Ctrl+Alt+Up Cell Above The cell above, in the same column
Alt+Home First Cell in Row The first cell of this row
Alt+End Last Cell in Row The last cell of this row
Ctrl+Alt+Home First Cell The first cell of the table
Ctrl+Alt+End Last Cell The last cell of the table

Each move puts the cursor at the start of the cell’s text and says the cell’s place, then its text – but only the part that changed. Moving along a row says the column, “Column 3: Lead”; moving down a column says the row, “Row 4: Lead”; a jump to another row and column says both. The table’s size is said once, when you arrive in it. An empty cell is “blank”. The row of dashes under the header row is skipped; it is not a row you read.

At an edge the cursor stays where it is and you hear why: “No more cells” at the end of a row, “No more rows” at the bottom of a column, and “No more cells, end of table” on the very last cell. Outside a table, every one of these keys says “Not in a table” and does nothing else. Alt+Left (Go Back) returns from a cell move like any other jump.

For example, in this table:

| Name | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Ann | Lead |
| Bo | Tester |

put the cursor on “Name” and press Ctrl+Alt+Down: you hear “Row 2: Ann”. Press Alt+End: “Column 2, Role: Lead” – a new column says its header, because this table has a header row. Press Ctrl+Alt+End: “Row 3: Tester” (the same column, so neither the column nor its header is repeated). Press Ctrl+Alt+Right: “No more cells, end of table”.

The arrow keys read a table cell by cell. Inside a table, Up and Down go to the cell above and below in the same column and say it, “Row 3: Ann”, instead of the screen reader reading the whole row as one line. Right at the end of a cell’s text goes to the next cell, and Left at its start to the end of the cell before, “Column 2, Role: Lead”; inside a cell’s text they move letter by letter as always. Down from the line above a table lands in its first cell and says its shape once, “Table, 3 rows, 2 columns. Name”; Up past the first row or Down past the last leaves the table, and you hear “Out of table”. Shift with the arrows still selects. This works the same with JAWS, NVDA and Narrator, in rich text and in | tables, with nothing to install. JAWS keeps Ctrl+Alt with the arrows for its own table commands, so with JAWS use the arrows, Tab and the Navigate > Table menu.

The shape and “Out of table” go with Announce Headings in the View menu.

A cell whose own text contains a | is written \| and read as the | it stands for, so it never makes an extra column. If your screen reader uses Ctrl+Alt with the arrow keys for its own table commands, it may answer these keys first in some modes; the menu rows always work.

Editing a table

  • Tab and Shift+Tab move to the next and previous cell, on to the next row at the end of one, as in Word. Tab in the very last cell adds a row and puts you in its first cell. Outside a table, Tab works as it always has.
  • Navigate > Table also has Insert Row Below, Insert Column Right, Delete Row, Delete Column and Tidy Table, the same as in QUILL. Each is one Undo and says what it did, such as “Added row 3” or “Deleted column 2, Role. 1 columns left”. Tidy Table pads every cell to its column’s widest, so the columns of a | table line up on screen; a real table already lines up, and says so.
  • In a real table, adding or deleting a row or a column rebuilds the table with its text in place. Bold, italic or a font inside its cells does not survive that rebuild; type the formatting after the table has its shape.

A real table in rich text is saved as a real table: in an .rtf file with its first row marked as the header row, and in a Word document the same way. A | table in a Markdown document you switch to rich text becomes a real table, and switching back to Markdown or plain text turns a real table into | rows.

Reading without changing anything: the Read-Only Guard

When you open a long reference document only to read it, such as a manual, a book or minutes you must not alter, one stray key can change it, and a Ctrl+S an hour later saves the change. The Read-Only Guard locks the document against edits while you read.

Ctrl+Alt+Backspace, or Tools > Read-Only Guard, turns it on and off. The menu row shows a check mark while it is on. You hear:

  • “Document is read-only” when you turn it on;
  • “Document is editable” when you turn it off.

While it is on:

  • Typing, Backspace, Delete, Enter and pasting do nothing.
  • Commands that would change the text say “Document is read-only” and change nothing. That covers cutting, pasting, undo and redo, everything in Edit > Lines, the Insert menu (Insert Table too), the row and column commands on Navigate > Table, the formatting commands, changing case and indenting, Snippets, and a word tool’s Replace. The table keys and the arrows still move through a table.
  • Everything you use to read still works: the arrow keys and every way of moving, Find (Ctrl+F), Go To (Ctrl+G), the headings list, selecting and copying, Say Selection, the dictionary and the thesaurus (looking up, not replacing).
  • Bookmarks work as usual. Set one with Ctrl+Shift+B and come back to it with F2; a bookmark is kept beside the file, not inside it.
  • Inline notes work too. A note you add while the guard is on is kept beside the file rather than written into it, so the document stays exactly as it was.

QUILL Lite remembers the guard for that file. Close QUILL Lite, open the same file tomorrow from File Explorer, from Recent Documents or from Ctrl+O, and it opens locked again. It is the same file however it was reached, even if File Explorer spells the folder with different capitals.

If QUILL Lite cannot write down that you locked the file, for example because its settings folder is on a full disk, the document is still locked for now and you hear so, with the reason:

Document is read-only for now, but it could not be remembered for next time (There is not enough space on the disk).

A document that has never been saved cannot be remembered either, because it has no file name yet; save it first if you want the guard to stay.

A file that Windows itself marks read-only, or one on a share you cannot write to, opens locked as well, and QUILL Lite says so as it opens. Turn the guard off if you want to type anyway; you can then save under a new name.

The guard is the same command QUILL has, on the same key, so you only learn it once.

Bookmarks

A bookmark is a place you want to come back to.

Key What it does
Ctrl+Shift+B Drop a bookmark in the next free slot
Ctrl+Shift+1 to Ctrl+Shift+9 Set that numbered bookmark
F2 / Shift+F2 Next / previous bookmark
Alt+Shift+G The bookmark list: go to one, or remove one
Ctrl+Alt+B Clear them all

Bookmarks move with your text as you edit around them, so they stay on the right words.

If you would like to try bookmarks with the app guiding you, the tutorial Finding your way back walks you through them, along with the temporary bookmark and Go Back.

They are still there tomorrow

Close a document, open it again, and your bookmarks are right where you left them. So is the cursor. You do not have to do anything to keep them.

This is a real help in a long file. You cannot glance at a scrollbar to see where you are, so marking a few places and finding them again next time saves a lot of hunting.

A few things worth knowing:

  • A document you have never saved is not remembered. It has no file name yet. Save it once and its bookmarks are kept from then on.
  • Nothing extra is saved next to your file. No extra files appear in your folder. QUILL Lite keeps the list in its own settings folder.
  • If the file got shorter while it was closed, for example because another program edited it, any bookmark past the new end is moved to the end.

If you switch bookmarks off in Tools > Customize Features, none of this is saved at all.

The one with no number

Key What it does
Ctrl+Alt+J Set Temporary Bookmark: drop a pin where the cursor is
Ctrl+Shift+J Go to Temporary Bookmark: go back to it

There is one more bookmark, and it works differently. Use it when you are about to look something up and want to come straight back:

  1. Press Ctrl+Alt+J where you are.
  2. Go and look at whatever you need.
  3. Press Ctrl+Shift+J to come back.

It has no number, no name and no place in the list. Setting it again just moves it, without asking. It is forgotten when you close the document. It is meant for a quick trip, so it stays out of your way.

These are the same two keys QUILL uses.

Going back where you came from

Key What it does
Alt+Left Go back to where you were before the last jump
Alt+Right Go forward again

These are Go Back and Go Forward, at the top of the Navigate menu. They are the same keys a web browser uses.

QUILL Lite remembers every jump you make: going to a line, following a heading, choosing something from the heading list or the bookmark list, and landing on a search result. Alt+Left takes you back to where you were before the jump. Think of it as undo for moving around. Press F3 to check a word somewhere else, then Alt+Left to come home.

They are always there, even if you switch headings and bookmarks off.

Unlike bookmarks, this history is only kept while the window is open.

What you learned, and where to go next

You can find text with Ctrl+F, narrow it with Match case and Whole word only, replace one match at a time or all at once with Ctrl+H, hear how many matches there are, see them all in one list, and search for characters you cannot type. Ctrl+G takes you to a line, a bookmark or a heading from one window. Numbered bookmarks are still there after you close the document, the temporary bookmark is there for a quick trip, and Alt+Left takes you back after any jump.

The tutorial Finding your way back lets you practise bookmarks, the temporary bookmark and Go Back with the app guiding you.
