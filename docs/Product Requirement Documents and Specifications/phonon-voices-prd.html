Phonon Voices – Product Requirements

Version 0.2, 2026-11-03. Status: phases 1 and 2 shipped in QUILL and QUILL Studio (PR #1747, 2026-11-02): Phonon 31m and its 37 English voices ship in the shared runtime, in Read Aloud, audio export, voice previews, batch export and Studio narration. Where the build differs from this plan, the code and its docstrings (quill/core/speech/phonon_tts.py) are the record: the CPU check asks Windows for AVX2 instead of spawning a probe process, the model ships bundled rather than downloaded, and the speed range is 0.85 to 1.3, measured. Phases 3 and 4 are still to do.

The research behind it, with a link for every outside claim and the prototype measurements from this computer, is docs/design/2026-11-02-phonon-research.md (formerly phonon.md). Where this document states a fact about Phonon it is taken from there.

Licensing in one paragraph: the Phonon runtime is MIT or Apache 2.0. Phonon 31m (English) and the 102 voice files are Apache 2.0, so QUILL may bundle and mirror them. The five per-language 40m models and the 40m multilingual model are CC BY-NC 4.0 and gated on Hugging Face; the licence allows a free, non-commercial project like QUILL to redistribute them with attribution, but we ask Gradium in writing before mirroring any of them, and until they answer QUILL only points people to the upstream download.

1. Product statement

Phonon is an offline voice for Read Aloud and Studio narration that sounds natural and starts speaking almost at once, on the kind of computer most of our readers actually own. Today the choice on a cheap laptop is between Piper, which is quick and a bit flat, and Kokoro, which sounds better and is slow to start. Phonon is small (64.5 MB for the English model), needs no phonemizer, and its maker reports it running faster than Kokoro by a wide margin. If our own measurements agree on a weak machine, it becomes the natural-sounding voice for people who cannot wait for Kokoro.

It is one more voice, not a new product. It lives inside QUILL’s existing Read Aloud, Studio, Voice Previews and download machinery, and it adds no new window and no new key.

2. Users

  • U-1. Blind and low-vision people who listen to long documents, on a Windows laptop bought cheaply or handed down, often with NVDA or JAWS running beside QUILL. They notice a two-second wait before every paragraph; they also notice when a voice stumbles over a phone number.
  • U-2. People making audiobooks and narrated files in Studio, who care about total render time on the same kind of machine.
  • U-3. People without a reliable connection, for whom “offline” must mean that nothing leaves the computer after the one download.

3. The low computer standard (a hard requirement)

  • R-1. The standard. Windows 10 or 11, 64-bit; two CPU cores; 4 GB of RAM; no graphics card. This is the baseline QUILL’s dictation work already uses (“a 4 GB dual-core Windows 10 computer”) and matches every installer’s MinVersion=10.0 and ArchitecturesAllowed=x64compatible.
  • R-2. Phonon’s extra condition: AVX2, FMA and F16C. Gradium’s Windows x64 builds are compiled for x86-64-v3. Roughly: Intel Core 4th generation (2013) or later, AMD 2015 or later, and Celeron or Pentium only from 2020. On Windows on ARM, Windows 11 24H2 or later, where Prism shows AVX2 to x64 programs. A computer without these is inside QUILL’s standard but outside Phonon’s, and R-30 says what happens there.
  • R-3. Phonon is not offered as a voice unless it meets the budgets in section 14 on the reference low machine. If it only meets them on two cores, it is offered with the “needs a newer computer” note and is never the default.
  • R-4. Memory. Phonon, loaded and speaking, stays inside the speech budget QUILL already enforces: 600 MB on a 4 GB computer. Low-resource mode may unload it like any other engine.

4. Goals and non-goals

Goals:

  • G-1. A natural English voice that starts speaking in well under half a second on the low machine.
  • G-2. Thirty-seven English voices (twenty-five plus twelve character voices) chosen exactly the way Kokoro and Piper voices are chosen today.
  • G-3. Studio narration with the same voice, at least twice real time on the low machine.
  • G-4. Fully offline after one download, verified by a test.
  • G-5. Notices and attribution correct for every file we ship or mirror.

Non-goals:

  • N-1. Voice cloning or Voice Design. Those need Gradium’s online studio and an account; QUILL does not send anyone’s voice anywhere.
  • N-2. GPU, CUDA, Vulkan or WebGPU builds.
  • N-3. Building our own non-AVX2 Phonon binary in the first release (see D-4).
  • N-4. Any voice in QUILL Lite. Lite has no self-voicing by design (quill/apps/lite_voice.py); see R-12.
  • N-5. Replacing Piper or Kokoro. Both stay; Piper stays the voice that works on every computer QUILL supports.
  • N-6. macOS. QUILL supports it, but this program measures and ships Windows first; the macOS wheel exists and can follow.

5. Architecture

  • R-5. One engine id, phonon, added beside sapi5, dectalk, piper, kokoro, espeak, macos and elevenlabs everywhere those are listed: quill/core/read_aloud.py, settings.py, settings_specs.py, speech/document_speech.py, speech/batch_export.py, speech/project_profile.py, speech/pronunciation.py, voice_catalog.py, optional_components.py, release_assets.py, apps/studio.py, ui/batch_speech_runner.py, ui/main_frame_speech_hub.py, ui/main_frame_speech_downloads.py and ui/main_frame_voice_preview.py.
  • R-6. The engine code lives in a new wx-free module, quill/core/speech/phonon_tts.py, strict-typed and in mypy scope. read_aloud.py (1,702 lines and on a size budget) gains only the dispatch branch and a thin _run_phonon_live, as Kokoro and ElevenLabs did.
  • R-7. In process, through the ptts Python wheel (cp39-abi3, win_amd64, about 7 MB). One model is loaded and cached, exactly like _get_cached_kokoro_onnx, registered with lifecycle_service as tts:phonon so low-resource mode can evict it.
  • R-8. The CPU check runs before the import, in a child process. A library built for AVX2 that lands on a CPU without it dies with an illegal-instruction exception that Python cannot catch, which would take QUILL down with it. A short child process imports ptts and speaks one word from the local model; QUILL records the result per machine (CPU name and Windows build) and only then imports ptts in its own process. The check reruns when either changes.
  • R-9. Always a local path. QUILL never constructs the model without a local folder and never asks for a voice the folder lacks. The library has a Hugging Face fetch path for both; QUILL must never reach it (R-40).
  • R-10. Speech is per sentence span, written to WAV and played through QUILL’s existing player, the same shape as Kokoro and Piper, so pause, resume, the cursor and the synthesis cache all work unchanged. Streaming frames for faster first audio is phase 3, not phase 1.
  • R-11. Threads. Follow quill/core/onnx_threads.py: cores, at most four, and one on the low machine if measurement shows the second core is better left to the screen reader.
  • R-12. QUILL Lite. No change. Family rule 10 says Lite may never be ahead of QUILL; adding a voice to QUILL only is the permitted direction. The new settings are recorded as one_side in the settings vocabulary audit with the reason “QUILL Lite has no self-voicing”.

7. Read Aloud

  • R-18. Phonon appears in the Read Aloud engine list as “Phonon (neural, offline)”, after Kokoro.
  • R-19. Every existing Read Aloud command works with it unchanged: start, pause, resume, stop, next and previous sentence, read selection, read from cursor. No new command and no new key.
  • R-20. Rate. Phonon sets speed with its duration_delta condition. QUILL maps its usual rate control onto that over a range the prototype finds safe; if the mapping cannot cover at least 0.75 to 1.5 times normal without the voice falling apart, the rate control says its range rather than pretending.
  • R-21. Pronunciation dictionaries apply, because they rewrite text before the engine sees it. pronunciation.ENGINES gains phonon.
  • R-22. Text. Phonon reads raw text, numbers and email addresses itself, and its maker reports fewer mistakes on them than Kokoro. QUILL’s existing text normalising still runs first; the prototype checks it does not make Phonon worse, and if it does, Phonon skips the steps that hurt.

8. Studio narration

  • R-23. Phonon is offered in Studio’s engine list and in batch export, with the same voice and rate settings as Read Aloud.
  • R-24. It is added to _SINGLE_THREAD_ENGINES in batch_export.py until measurement shows two parallel jobs are faster on the low machine.
  • R-25. Narration projects (project_profile.py) remember phonon and its voice like any other engine, and a project saved with Phonon opened on a computer that fails the CPU check falls back as R-31 describes.

9. Voice previews

  • R-26. Each English voice has a short sample in quill/data/voice-previews/phonon, about 4 to 5 MB for 37 voices at Kokoro’s sample size.
  • R-27. The samples are Gradium’s own (Apache 2.0, from phonon-voices), converted to the format the other previews use, or made on the build machine with QUILL’s standard preview sentence. D-5 chooses. Either way they work on a computer that cannot run Phonon, so a person can hear what they would get before learning their computer is too old for it.

10. Settings and keys

  • R-28. New settings, each documented (GATE-SETDOC) and added to the settings vocabulary audit as one_side:
    • read_aloud_phonon_voice, default the model’s default voice;
    • read_aloud_phonon_rate, default 1.0;
    • warm_phonon_model, default false (Kokoro’s warm-up is true; Phonon loads fast enough that warming it should not be needed – measured, not assumed).
  • R-29. No new chords. Phonon is a choice inside existing windows, reached by existing commands, so rule 8 is already satisfied and the keyboard reference does not change.

11. Accessibility contract

  • A-1. Every new control (engine choice entry, voice list, rate) has an accessible name and an access key unique in its window (GATE-14). OK, Cancel and Close carry none.
  • A-2. Each new control has inline SetHelpText at its construction site, and the window’s F1 purpose mentions Phonon where it lists engines. The F1 help reference is regenerated.
  • A-3. Speak only what the screen reader does not already say (GATE-13). Selecting Phonon in a list is not announced; the list already says it. What is announced: a finished download, a failed CPU check, a fallback to another voice, and a model removed by low-resource mode while reading.
  • A-4. Everything announced is also brailled, through the announcement service (GATE-BRAILLE). No direct speech call.
  • A-5. Focus does not move when a download finishes or the CPU check completes. If the check fails while the person is in the engine list, focus stays where it is and the explanation is spoken once and written to the status bar.
  • A-6. Download progress uses the existing accessible progress path: a percentage, no more than one update per percent.
  • A-7. Phonon’s own voice must never speak over the screen reader’s. Read Aloud already stops when the person starts typing; that behaviour is tested with Phonon too.
  • A-8. Nothing depends on colour. “Needs a newer computer” is words.

12. Failure and fallback

  • R-30. CPU check fails (no AVX2). Phonon is still listed, with the words “needs a newer computer (AVX2)”. Choosing it says, once: “Phonon needs a processor from about 2015 or later, and this computer’s processor is older. Piper works here and sounds similar for most reading.” and offers Piper. It never crashes and never silently switches.
  • R-31. Too slow on this machine. QUILL measures the first few sentences. If Phonon cannot keep up – audio running out before the next sentence is ready, twice in one minute – QUILL says once: “Phonon is too slow on this computer to read without pauses. Piper is quicker here. Switch to Piper?” Yes switches for this session and offers to make it the default; No keeps Phonon and does not ask again until the next start.
  • R-32. Model missing or damaged. The SHA-256 check fails or a file is absent: say which, offer the download again, and fall back the way a missing Kokoro model does today.
  • R-33. Synthesis error on one passage. Split the passage and retry, as Kokoro does at 400 characters; if a piece still fails, skip it, say “one sentence could not be read”, and carry on.
  • R-34. Safe Mode does not disable Phonon (it is offline and local), but it skips the warm-up.
  • R-35. Error codes. One new exception, PhononUnavailableError, under ReadAloudUnavailableError with its own code, QUILL-READALOUD-PHONON-UNAVAILABLE (GATE-EC), and messages in plain words.

14. Performance budgets

Measured on the reference low machine (4 GB, two cores, Windows 10, AVX2), NVDA running, with a fixed 12-word sentence, a 150-word paragraph full of numbers and an email address, and a 2,000-word chapter; five runs, median reported.

Measure Budget How
Real-time factor, one core 1.5x or better audio seconds over wall seconds, affinity to one core
Real-time factor, two cores 2x or better affinity to two cores
Time to first audio under 300 ms after load start call to first sentence WAV ready
Load time under 2 s model construct to ready, warm disk
Peak memory under 300 MB peak working set of the process doing synthesis
Studio, 2,000 words under half the audio length wall time for a batch export
Screen reader no audible stutter while typing a person listening
  • R-39. A small benchmark tool, python -m quill.tools.tts_benchmark, prints these numbers for any installed engine, so the same table can be made for Piper and Kokoro on the same machine. It is a developer tool, not a gate: timings on shared CI runners mean nothing.

15. Privacy and network

  • R-40. No network at run time. The only download is the assets-v1 zip through release_assets, which the network egress audit already lists. The audit’s notes record that ptts contains a Hugging Face fetch path that QUILL never reaches, and why (R-9).
  • R-41. A test runs synthesis from a local folder with sockets blocked and fails on any connection attempt.
  • R-42. Text read aloud is never logged beyond what Read Aloud logs today.

19. Risks

  • K-1. AVX2. The cheapest laptops of 2015 to 2019 cannot run Phonon at all. Mitigated by R-8 and R-30; the real fix is D-4.
  • K-2. No x86 figures exist. Every speed number so far is Gradium’s, on Arm or Apple hardware with acceleration our Windows build does not have. Phase 0 exists for this.
  • K-3. Young project. First stable release 2026-10-20, five releases in ten days. Pin the version; update deliberately.
  • K-4. Rate control may not reach the range people use. R-20 says what we do.
  • K-5. The 40m gate. Mirroring without asking could sour the relationship with a vendor that has shown it is willing to help. Mitigated by D-2.
  • K-6. In-process native code. A crash in ptts is a crash in QUILL. Mitigated by the child-process check; if crashes appear in the field, move synthesis into a worker process like the one Piper already uses.

20. Open decisions for Jeff

  • D-1. Go or stop after phase 0? Recommended: go only if 31m makes 1.5x real time on one pinned core of the low machine with under 300 MB peak.
  • D-2. Ask Gradium to mirror the 40m models? Recommended: yes, now, by email, naming QUILL as free and non-commercial for blind readers and citing the iOS Phonon app as precedent. Until they answer, point only.
  • D-3. Bundle 31m in the Offline Edition? Recommended: yes, as an optional component beside Kokoro, after phase 1 has been used for one release.
  • D-4. Build a non-AVX2 ptts wheel ourselves? Recommended: not in phase 1. The licence allows it, but it means building and signing third-party Rust code in QUILL’s CI, and it will be slower without the AVX kernels. Revisit if the field shows many readers on older Celerons; ask Gradium first whether they would publish an x86-64-v2 wheel themselves.
  • D-5. Preview samples: Gradium’s or our own? Recommended: our own, made on the build machine with QUILL’s standard sentence, so every engine’s previews say the same thing and can be compared fairly.
  • D-6. Default voice for new users? Recommended: unchanged. Phonon is never the default in phase 1; revisit after one release of field reports.
  • D-7. Write a public “System requirements” page? Recommended: yes. QUILL has no single place that says 4 GB, two cores, Windows 10, and which features need AVX2 (Phonon and the local AI models). This program is a good reason to write it.
